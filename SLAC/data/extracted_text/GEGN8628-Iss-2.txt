Part 1
Purpose and introduction
G1.1
Purpose
G1.1.1
This document gives guidance on the preparation of infrastructure and rolling stock
for winter and their subsequent operation during frost, ice and snow conditions on the
Great Britain (GB) mainline railway. This guidance is intended to assist infrastructure
managers and railway undertakings in understanding their responsibilities. It does
not constitute a recommended method of meeting any set of mandatory
requirements.
G1.1.2
This document has been structured into separate sections covering infrastructure,
rolling stock and operations. When planning for winter, consideration can be given to
all of the guidance in GEGN8628 as there could be information in one section of the
document that is pertinent to other areas of the railway.
G1.1.3
This document sets out guidance on the actions that are implemented by the
infrastructure manager and railway undertaking working in isolation. However, for the
effectiveness of some elements, there is a need for co-operation and co-ordinated
actions between the infrastructure manager and railway undertakings. This also
applies between different railway undertakings operating on the same route.
G1.1.4
The general guidance is supplemented in the document by previous experience,
examples of which have been supplied by the rail industry.
G1.1.5
The content of this document concerns:
a) Guidance on weather forecasts (Part 2);
b) Planning for winter (Part 3);
c) Guidance on winterisation of infrastructure (Part 4);
d) Guidance on winterisation of rolling stock (Part 5); and
e) Guidance on operational arrangements in winter (Part 6).
G1.1.6
This document is supported by more detailed appendices:
a) Recommended timeline for winter preparation (Appendix A);
b) Guidance on air systems (Appendix B);
c) Guidance on brakes (Appendix C); and
d) Guidance on coupler reliability (Appendix D).
G1.1.7
Whilst not specifically covered by the document, elements of the winter guidance are
also potentially relevant to organisations outside the GB mainline railway, for
example, metro operations and suppliers of mobile plant used for maintenance of the
infrastructure.
G1.2
Health and safety responsibilities

own responsibilities to ensure health and safety at work and their own duties under
health and safety legislation. RSSB does not warrant that compliance with all or any

Part 2
Guidance on Winter Forecasts
G2.1
Introduction
G2.1.1
Cold weather generally occurs in GB between the months of October and April,
although this varies depending on the geographical region. It is good practice for
contingency plans to be capable of being implemented at any time. This was
demonstrated in November 2010 when winter weather conditions overlapped with
the autumn period. A particularly cold period between 24 February 2018 and
4 March 2018 struck the UK and caused a lot of travel disruption.
G2.2
Forecasting during the winter period
G2.2.1
The effectiveness of any measures to improve the operation of the mainline railway
during winter weather conditions depends on the timeliness and accuracy of the
weather forecast. It is good practice for infrastructure managers and railway
undertakings to have arrangements in place for weather forecasts to be received and
disseminated within their organisations.
G2.2.2
Using a common single weather forecasting source allows infrastructure managers
and railway undertakings to have consistent information that facilitates taking
co-ordinated actions.
G2.2.3
The Network Rail Weather Service (NRWS) provides a forecasting service available at
http://www.nrws.co.uk.
Note: Registration is required to access the forecasting information provided
by NRWS.
Note: NRWS only provides forecasting information for Network Rail routes
only.
G2.2.4
The NRWS website provides meteorological data daily before 04:00 for the 24-hour
period which commences at 06:00 that day along with a five-day rolling summary to
facilitate advanced planning. The five-day summary also includes levels of confidence
with respect to the predicted weather conditions.
G2.2.5
NRWS provides an additional notification service when winter weather is forecast.
This facilitates the triggering of any contingency plans including implementing
alternative timetables by railway undertakings as required. This also includes regular
forecasts for snow, frost, and ice.
G2.2.6
In interpreting the meteorological forecast, it is good practice to consider the whole
area of operation and adjacent routes. Implementation of appropriate measures
relevant to specific parts of the operational area can be undertaken and agreed
between the infrastructure manager and railway undertakings.
G2.2.7
The NRWS issue revised forecasts in the event that the weather is different from that
originally forecast. It is good practice to undertake regular checks of the NRWS
website during winter weather situations to check for any updates. Notifications will
be sent via email, where subscribed.

G2.3
Frost and snow alerts
G2.3.1
Forecast alert levels
G2.3.1.1
Frost and snow alert levels together with associated confidence levels are included in
the forecasts received from NRWS weather. Table 1 to Table 4 set out the criteria
used to determine the frost and snow alert levels. Typical consequences and their
impacts on operations are provided for information, and cannot be considered an
exhaustive list.
G2.3.1.2
Frost and snow alert levels are used to anticipate the effects that frost and snow have
on the area of operation. This provides information to enable the implementation of
detailed contingency plans to minimise the effects of any forecast winter weather.

G2.3.2.3
Frost alert levels issued with the NRWS forecast apply at any time during the forecast

NRWS forecast also gives predicted minimum temperatures and when the specific
alert level is expected.

operation. The forecasts are used to predict:
a) Wind in conjunction with ice accumulation on overhead wires and the
consequential abnormal sideways displacement of the contact wire and arcing at
the pantograph;
b) Wind and high seas leading to sea water freezing on the infrastructure;
c) Rapid changes in temperature or humidity causing snow and ice to melt on
equipment or rolling stock;
d) Freezing fog and rain, and how these cause the accumulation of ice; and
e) Flooding following the thawing of snow and ice.

Part 3
Planning for Winter
G3.1
Introduction
G3.1.1
The key to minimising winter disruption is planning and timely implementation of
actions in advance of the winter period.
G3.1.2
The Railway Operational Code (ROC), part of the Network Code, is a licence condition
between the infrastructure manager and railway undertakings to sustain the
operation of train services on the network and restore operation of the network
following disruption.
G3.1.3
The ROC requires a winter contingency plan to be developed by the infrastructure
manager and railway undertaking.
G3.1.4
The winter contingency plan sets out the general policy including:
a) Details of how all relevant personnel are to be informed of the plans and
procedures for the winter period, including any updates that have been made
before the winter period starts;
b) A review and update of all winter preparation procedures and contingency
planning; and
c) Details for informing relevant personnel when the contingency plan is invoked.
G3.1.5
A combined winter preparation checklist has been developed with contributions from
the Rail Delivery Group (RDG) and Network Rail. The checklist contains actions from
the Paper D Seasonal Preparation Checklist supplied by RDG and the Winter Stage
Gate Review Tracker for route and operator supplied by Network Rail. This checklist
can be used in conjunction with the development of winter preparation plans. Clause
or section numbers of GEGN8628 issue two are provided in the checklist to enable the
user to make references to the guidance note. A copy of the combined winter
preparation checklist can be obtained from RSSB's standards catalogue.
G3.2
Generating the contingency plan
G3.2.1
The effectiveness of the contingency plan relies on shared and reviewed information
supplied by the infrastructure manager and railway undertaking. It is good practice
for operations and engineering teams to work together to produce a robust and
effective plan for the winter period.
G3.2.2
Preparation for winter includes:
a) Reviews at executive meetings and / or the Joint Seasons Management Group,
where progress against the plan is monitored;
b) Maximising the level and consistency of the railway undertaking’s fleet
performance during winter;
c) A preparation timeline for activities before, during and after the winter period.
Further guidance on a timeline is provided in Appendix A; and
d) Information relating to any relevant modification programme that is in progress,
for example the installation of coupler heaters to rolling stock for the winter
period. Consider accelerating such programmes if they will not be completed in
time for the start of the winter period.

G3.3
Contingency plan elements
G3.3.1
Key route strategy
G3.3.1.1
Where two or more railway undertakings operate over the same route, consultation
on any contingency plans with any other affected railway undertakings will allow
them to assess how the plan impacts on their own operations.
Example
During winter 2010 / 2011, one railway undertaking
reported that decisions made between the infrastructure
manager and another railway undertaking, seemingly
without consultation, had adversely affected their services.
G3.3.1.2
For individual routes of operation, a key route strategy (KRS) sets out the policy for
managing the reduced availability of infrastructure when normal operations cannot
be maintained.
G3.3.1.3
It is recommended that the KRS be developed and agreed prior to winter that
considers :
a) Operating shuttle services that remove the need to operate points and crossings
whilst endeavouring to keep the railway open;
b) Reducing services on branch lines;
c) Suspending train services; and
d) Recovery back to normal service operation.
G3.3.1.4
The development of a KRS is based on a range of structured scenarios that anticipate
short-term reductions in infrastructure availability, the provision of rolling stock, the
availability of critical staff such as signallers, train crew and maintenance staff and
consideration of how they reach their place of work.
G3.3.2
Winter contingency timetables
G3.3.2.1
It is recommended that the level of service and routes to be maintained during a
winter contingency timetable be agreed between the infrastructure manager and all
affected railway undertakings. The contingency timetable permits regular services to
be maintained when only part of the network is adversely affected by winter
conditions.
G3.3.2.2
It is recommended that winter contingency timetables are published and
disseminated through all appropriate channels so that passengers understand the
conditions under which they are implemented. This gives passengers the information
to plan or modify their journeys to minimise disruption and inconvenience to
themselves.
Note: If a winter contingency timetable has been implemented, it is
recommended that it remain in force for a complete day (24 hours). Changing
the timetable within a 24-hour period is not recommended as it will cause
confusion to staff and passengers.
G3.3.2.3
The winter contingency timetable is developed with the intention of providing a
reliable service when implemented. When developing the timetable, it is
recommended that the following factors are included:

f) Operating a reduced or slower service minimises damage to rolling stock during
extreme winter weather. This facilitates the train service being restored as
efficiently as possible back to normal service once the weather has improved.
Example
During 2010, the TGV network in France implemented a
blanket 160 km/h emergency speed restriction as a
result of the winter weather conditions being
experienced. Speed reductions are also implemented on
the Shinkansen services in Japan and have been
effective at reducing the damage caused from icy
ballast balls being thrown up from the track at speed. It
is possible for these balls of ice and ballast to weigh up
to 15 kg and therefore are potentially damaging to
rolling stock.
Example
Following the winter of 2010 / 2011, a significant
proportion of rolling stock in GB was in service in a
degraded condition as a result of snow and ice damage.
Railway undertakings were also forced to defer
maintenance tasks. This backlog of defects and overdue
maintenance took a number of months to rectify.
Example
During winter 2010, one railway undertaking took the
decision to attempt to run a full timetable. As a result all
routes remained open for the majority of the time albeit
at reduced speed with some cancellations and a large
number of delays. The reduced speed service minimised
damage to rolling stock but punctuality dropped
15 percentage points against the full timetable.

ii)
To clear snow, it is recommended that the longest train formation possible is

30 minutes increases the risk of complete loss of electrical contact with the risk of
stranded trains.
Example
On 2 March 2018, the 2M48 service was unable to
depart Lewisham station due to a conductor rail that
was iced-over, preventing the train from drawing
sufficient power to continue its journey. This caused
other trains to be blocked behind it for many hours,
eventually leading to passengers undertaking
self-detrainment.
i)
On some routes the traction supplies have been designed assuming that the
train’s regenerative dynamic braking supplements the supply. Where winter
operation of trains reduces the output or isolates the regenerative brake, there will
be a corresponding reduction in the traction energy supply available to operate
the service. It is good practice to understand the impact of this on the timetable.
G3.3.3
Other contingency plan considerations
G3.3.3.1
It is important for contingency plans to contain arrangements for the care of
passengers on stranded trains. This includes provision of information, access to toilets
and a means of sending and receiving messages on a train or at a station. If the train
is stranded, it is good practice to ensure that refreshments are provided and
arrangements are made for safe passenger detrainment.
G3.3.3.2
Further guidance on passenger information during disruption and passenger needs

It is good practice for the contingency plan to include arrangements for holding

G3.4
Severe winter weather processes
G3.4.1
National co-ordination of severe winter weather is managed through the National
Extreme Weather Action Teleconference (EWAT) . This is chaired by Network Rail and
joined by railway undertakings, the Department for Transport and RDG.
G3.4.2
At a local level, the EWAT is an agreed process to review weather conditions at
specific decision points. It is good practice to incorporate the EWAT process into any
winter contingency plan.
G3.4.3
The EWAT is implemented in the event of the forecasted weather conditions
deteriorating to an extent where the infrastructure manager’s maintenance and
operations teams declare that their resources are unable to maintain any particular
part of the GB mainline railway. Decisions with regard to the implementation of the
applicable KRS is made at this time.
G3.4.4
The times for the decision points to implement a KRS for the following day are
nominally at 10:00 hrs (pre-planning) and 13:00 hrs (go / no go).

G3.5.1

Part 4
Guidance on Winterisation of Infrastructure
G4.1
Preparations prior to the start of the winter period
G4.1.1
Infrastructure winter preparation plan
G4.1.1.1
Winter weather conditions may have an impact on the reliability of equipment vital
to the effective operation of the infrastructure. The type of failure varies depending
on the type of asset, its location and the weather conditions faced.
G4.1.1.2
It is good practice for the infrastructure manager to have an infrastructure winter
preparation plan that identifies locations and assets through a risk assessment to
reduce the effects of winter weather. For example, the impact of the failure of
neighbours' equipment or supplies due to the extreme winter weather can be
considered.
G4.1.1.3
It is recommended that railway undertakings have an infrastructure winter
preparation plan when they are responsible for depots and other infrastructure
locations.
G4.1.1.4
The infrastructure winter preparation plan often includes the variation of ambient
temperatures and conditions. The effects of altitude and the local environment for
sections of route (due to orientation of the track, for example East – West or North –
 South) on air temperatures are considered.
G4.1.1.5
The infrastructure winter preparation plan is a document containing preventative
actions required in the range of frost and snow conditions as set out in Part 2. The
following are considered as part of the infrastructure winter preparation plan:
a) Temperatures at or below -17˚C, when waxing of winter grade diesel occurs;
b) Actions required at the end of the winter period;
c) Fuel suppliers are able to produce arctic grade fuel that operate down to
temparatures of -30°C, see BS EN 590:2009+A1:2010; and
d) New trains have AdBlue treatment for diesel emissions. The AdBlue liquid freezes
at -11˚C and as it is mostly water, it will expand upon freezing.
G4.1.1.6
It is good practice for the infrastructure winter preparation plan to identify those
locations and assets which present a hazard when subjected to heavy snow fall.
Consideration can be given to the effect of snow on trees and the likelihood of an
avalanche of snow from both adjacent higher ground and adjacent buildings and
structures. The infrastructure manager often develops strategies for additional
inspection and remedial action of those areas identified as a hazard. It is good
practice to implement actions aimed at reducing the identified hazard prior to the
onset of the winter period.
G4.1.1.7
The infrastructure winter preparation plan often includes identification of areas that
are at risk of flooding when snow thaws. Areas that can be considered include
properties adjoining the railway where thawing of snow could cause issues.
G4.1.1.8
It is recommended that the infrastructure winter preparation plan identifies locations
and assets which present a hazard when subjected to freeze / thaw action on
structures and earthworks. This includes the effects of ice expanding within the fabric
of buildings and structures. It is recommended that the infrastructure manager

Part 5
Guidance on Winterisation of Rolling Stock
G5.1
Preparations required prior to the start of the winter season
G5.1.1
Tasks within this section have a focus on preparing rolling stock for winter weather
conditions. These tasks are additional to standard maintenance and are in place prior
to winter operation.
G5.1.2
Instructions are developed of actions to implement on rolling stock in light of the
snow and frost alert levels as set out in Part 2. The instructions often include winter
precautionary tasks by vehicle type, and identify applicable critical operating
parameters.
G5.1.3
The instructions are distributed to relevant personnel so that they are aware of the
additional requirements during the winter period. For example, restrictions on
coupling / uncoupling could leave units operating in multiple and will have an impact
on services being run. Staffing resources are also considered during planning in light
of the actions included in these instructions.
G5.1.4
Consideration is given to withholding a proportion of rolling stock from service during
extreme winter weather to allow a normal service to quickly resume.
G5.2
Rolling stock winterisation preparation activities
G5.2.1
General
G5.2.1.1
It is good practice to develop and refine winterisation preparation maintenance plans
to enhance rolling stock operational resilience throughout the winter period. Routine
rolling stock maintenance plans do not cover all areas of maintenance which are
critical to maintaining operational service during winter weather. Develop specific
winter preparation inspections (it is recommended that these tasks are not ‘lost’
within general exams).
Example
During 2010, a railway undertaking commented that they
had witnessed an improvement in performance compared
to the previous winter through 'a sustained focus on
winter between their teams throughout the summer and

G5.4
Actions for rolling stock during extreme winter weather conditions
G5.4.1
General
G5.4.1.1
Tasks in this section are targeted at periods of sustained extreme winter conditions,
and reaction to snow and frost weather forecasting alerts. The purpose of this is to
incorporate flexibility to allow a positive reaction to changing conditions and
emerging trends.
G5.4.1.2
The production of a rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan will mitigate the
effects of prolonged or extreme winter weather on rolling stock. Consideration is
given to the deferral of non-safety critical maintenance tasks to create capacity for
additional repairs or maintenance arising as a result of operation during winter
weather, for example, ballast damage, broken windows and de-icing.
G5.4.1.3
Deferred maintenance plans are often developed in advance of the need to
implement them. It is recommended that the effects of any deferred maintenance be
understood and properly managed to minimise any long term impacts on the fleet.
G5.4.1.4
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan includes information for setting
up winter response teams. It is recommended that the winter response teams are
focused on in-service attention to key rolling stock systems, for example couplers and
doors. The rolling stock winter action plan also identifies actions where contractor
staff may be necessary to fulfil additional activities.
Note: Where winter response teams are set up, any additional supplies that
they need to complete their tasks are considered.
G5.4.1.5
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan includes, in the event of travel
disruption, the details for depot staff who will attend key outstations instead of their
home depot in the event that the outstation is more easily accessible to them.
G5.4.1.6
During the period of extreme winter weather, it is good practice to undertake failure
reviews and planning meetings at regular intervals (at least every 24 hours) to provide
clear instructions to manage rolling stock and personnel. The meeting issues are
captured at the time for subsequent review after the winter. The meetings cover:
a) Issues that are emerging;
b) Containment plans that are required; and
c) Short and medium term mitigations or arrangements.
G5.4.1.7
Rolling stock is designed to operate in specific temperature ranges. It is
recommended that railway undertakings consider the forecasted temperature range
and any degradation on the rolling stock that the forecasted temperature might
cause. Railway undertakings to consider the impact to services of reduced traction
power and the effects of low temperature on diesel fuel.
G5.4.1.8
The ability of the rolling stock to operate on their routes during the winter period is
assessed. It is good practice for rolling stock known to be more susceptible to extreme
winter weather to be operated with caution: attempting to operate this rolling stock
during extreme winter weather could potentially cripple the entire fleet.

G5.4.12
Catering equipment
G5.4.12.1
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan includes consideration of
draining and isolation of the catering water supply. This is often defined at a specific
trigger level to prevent damage to the vehicles.
Note: It is recommended that any associated water heater be isolated to
prevent damage if the water tanks are drained.
G5.4.13
Doors
G5.4.13.1
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan includes the following
considerations:
a) Any build-up of snow or ice from drivers’ and passengers’ access doors, handrails,
footsteps and tread plates is cleared;
Note: This also includes clearing any build-up of snow, ice and grit from
passenger doorway runners.
b) Application of anti-icing products to doorways;
c) Application of silicone grease to mating rubber door seals to aid operation; and
d) Additional cycling of all doors.
Note: It is recommended that all drivers’ doors are also routinely operated.
G5.4.14
Electrical traction power equipment / electrical machines
G5.4.14.1
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan also considers the following:
a) Close snow filters and covers;
b) Clear air vents and inlets of the build-up of snow or ice;
c) Suspension of push pull activities during conditions of dry powdered snow to
reduce the ingress of snow into a trailing locomotive; and
d) Isolation of dynamic brake where applicable (for more information see
Appendix C).
Example
One railway undertaking only isolated the re-generative
brake and found the rheo-brake fans became blocked
with snow leading to the rheo-brake resistors
overheating.
G5.4.15
Underframe / bogies
G5.4.15.1
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan also considers the following:
a) Check mechanical final drive lubrication for build-up of snow or ice;
b) Check the cardan shaft, including the universal joint, for build-up of snow or ice;
c) Check the security and condition of side skirts and underframe panels for any
damage;
d) Isolation of tilt mechanisms to prevent damage caused by build-up of snow or ice;
and
e) Defrosting / de-icing of rolling stock underframes when out of service.

G5.4.15.2
Temperature differences between depot workshops and the outside are considered.
Consideration can also be given to trains that hold water while in a workshop as the
water will freeze quickly once the train is moved outside.
G5.4.15.3
De-icing with warm air or hot water causes problems when rolling stock enters service
later on. Rolling stock that has been de-iced and taken outside before it has fully dried
results in any water that remains refreezing. This causes the rolling stock to gather
more snow and ice during operation. This type of incomplete de-icing is considered to
be worse than no de-icing at all.
G5.4.15.4
When de-icing with hot water, it is recommended that no water or moisture is left on
the rolling stock before it is taken outside, the braking system is of special importance.
The time required to remove the water or moisture is shortened by warm air drying or
the use of anti-icing chemicals.
G5.4.15.5
De-icing with air takes a long period of time, four hours per train is typical. De-icing
with glycol based products is more effective although there are environmental
concerns that are considered when using these chemicals.
G5.4.15.6
The use of de-icing chemicals increases the risk of compromise to the performance of
transmission systems, for example, lubricants could be displaced due to the
application method. It is recommended that only tested and approved de-icing
chemicals be used.
G5.4.15.7
It is also possible for anti-icing and de-icing chemicals to cancel each other out,
resulting in them having no effect. It is good practice to ensure that chemical
compatibility is checked if it is intended to apply anti-icing chemicals to a surface that
has been treated with de-icing chemicals.
G5.4.16
Couplers
G5.4.16.1
The rolling stock extreme winter weather action plan also considers the following:
a) Defrost or clear the build-up of any snow or ice from couplers and electrical heads;
b) Fitment of coupler bags or covers;
c) Couplers are checked for snow and ice prior to coupling; and
d) Rolling stock operating in semi-permanent formations, fitted with buckeye
couplers that have been exposed to winter weather, are checked to mitigate the
potential for train division as a result of the build-up of snow or ice on the buckeye
release chain.
G5.4.16.2
Additional information is provided in Appendix D.

G5.5.2
In-service examination of freight wagons
G5.5.2.1
Some freight wagons, by the nature of their operation and maintenance, rarely visit
maintenance depots. The prime opportunity for monitoring the condition of freight
wagons during winter operations is during the in-service examination (traffic exam).
G5.5.2.2
A winter traffic examination sheet is prepared for each vehicle type that is being
operated. The traffic examination sheet illustrates acceptable and unacceptable
levels of snow or ice build-up to allow staff carrying out the traffic exam to judge if
the vehicle is fit to remain in service, moved at reduced speed, or needs to be de-iced.
G5.5.2.3
The acceptable and unacceptable levels of snow and ice build-up is based on a risk
assessment of the freight wagon performance in snow or ice conditions. The risk
assessment considers:
a) The type of braking system, for example tread brakes (single / double), disc brakes
(wheel mounted / inboard discs);
b) The type of friction material, for example composite or cast-iron brake blocks;
c) Incidents where the braking system was involved;
d) Similar freight wagons in previous incidents;
e) Similar bogies in previous incidents;
f) Typical train weights / speeds; and
g) Rolling stock aerodynamic performance, for example the ability to create a snow
swirl.

Further information on the operation of freight wagons is set out in RFOG 001.

continued operation while fleet repairs and recovery activities are carried out.

An initial review is undertaken of the rolling stock fleet position and any deviations
from the maintenance plans and diagrams. Rolling stock recovery plans allow the

Part 6
Guidance on Operational Arrangements in Winter
G6.1
General staff preparation
G6.1.1
Staff resources
G6.1.1.1
Travel to and from places of work are affected by the weather conditions. The winter
contingency plan identifies any issues with staff arrangements for getting to and
returning home from their place of work. This includes:
a) Access to and from key sites, for example crew stabling and maintenance depots;
b) Advising staff of planned changes to services so that they know the best way to
get to work or home;
c) Contingency arrangements allowing the use of special stop orders on trains to
permit staff to be dropped off at a station closer to their home. Staff could reside
in a location where the weather conditions are significantly different to those
where they work;
d) Asking staff in advance to establish who might be amenable to take up
accommodation away from home;
e) Welfare provision to support staff availability, for example provision of catering
and accommodation where appropriate when staff are unable to get between
work and home. If staff are relocated to perform key tasks at locations other than
their normal place of work, welfare provision is considered;
f) Consideration of accommodating control, traincrew and maintenance staff in
hotels close to their place of work during extreme winter weather conditions; and
g) Reduced availability of resources including traincrew, other operations staff, and
maintenance staff. Consideration can be given to keeping the driver, guard and
train together for the duration of their duty.
Example
One railway undertaking has geographically mapped
train crew and other operation critical staff’s home
addresses and has identified individuals who will be at
risk of not attending their workplace with the onset of
extreme winter weather conditions. Hotel facilities close
to their home depot, station or other work location are
made available for these staff. This has proven to be
highly successful.
G6.1.2
Staff clothing and equipment
G6.1.2.1
The winter contingency plan also includes a review of whether the clothing and
personal protection equipment (PPE) provision is adequate for the prevailing weather
conditions against the expected duration of exposure and the duties that staff are
expected to perform.
G6.1.3

G6.1.3.1
The winter contingency plan also includes issues related to information that staff be
briefed on when extreme winter weather is forecast (as set out in Part 2).

G6.2.2
Specific guidance for drivers on winter related braking techniques
G6.2.2.1
When snow is falling or there is movement of fallen snow, the performance of the
friction materials is reduced due the presence of moisture (melted snow) on the brake
pad / disc and block / tread interfaces. Drivers are briefed on implementing operating
instructions that have been prepared for that type of rolling stock. These instructions
include:
a) Revised driving policy for rolling stock fitted with brake pads and composition
brake blocks that are susceptible to a reduction in braking performance during
snow fall and disturbed snow conditions;
b) The underlying reasons why additional full service running brake tests are
necessary and the importance of carrying out such tests at the required frequency;
Example
During winter 2010 / 2011, one railway undertaking
introduced ‘extreme’ running brake tests. This is where
the frequency of additional running brake tests is
increased to every two minutes (from between every
three to five minutes) for Class 158 and Class 170 units.
In extreme weather conditions, leaving up to five
minutes between running brake tests is considered to be
too long to maintain the required level of brake
performance.
c) Operational issues on rising and falling gradients. Further information is provided
in the Rule Book and RFOG 001;
Example
See RAIB report (R02/2011) into Near miss involving a
freight train and two passenger trains at Carstairs,
December 2009.
d) As part of the contingency plan, trains are run at a reduced speed. This is
necessary to compensate for a reduction in the braking performance of the
friction materials or to avoid disturbing lying snow;
Example
During winter 2010 / 2011, one railway undertaking
reduced the maximum operational speed of Class 158
and Class 170 units to 120 km/h (75 mph) as a result of
significant snow and ice build-up due to the wind chill
factor and the volume of snow being disturbed
increasing at higher speeds.

G6.2.3
General operating conditions of trains with increasing snow depth
G6.2.3.1
To assist operational decisions on whether services need to be suspended, the
capability of locomotives and other leading vehicles to operate through snow depths
greater than 200 mm (8”) is recorded and made available to the relevant operational
control centres.
G6.2.3.2
The following guidance is offered once the snow depth reaches the following
indicative depths above rail level:
a) Up to 200 mm (8"): normal working;
b) Greater than 200 mm (8"): rolling stock only operate when the leading vehicle is
fitted with miniature snow ploughs. Obstacle deflectors are not be used for this
purpose unless specifically designed as a snow plough;
c) Greater than 300 mm (12"): potential suspension of train operation subject to
local agreement on rolling stock capabilities; and
d) Greater than 450 mm (18"): consideration is given to the suspension of all services.
G6.2.3.3
Prolonged use of miniature snow ploughs leads to the accumulation of snow being
left on or close to the line. It is possible for this accumulation of snow to be drawn
back into the running gear of rolling stock. Additional control measures such as the
periodic use of independent snow ploughs or snow blowers to clear the accumulated
snow are considered when there is prolonged use of miniature snow ploughs.
Example
See RAIB report (R03/2011) into Derailment of a freight
train at Carrbridge.

Recommended Timeline for Winter Preparation
A.1
Actions in April (or after the end of winter)
A.1.1
Weather forecasts are checked for possible late winter events. At the end of the
winter period, it is recommended that all equipment specifically related to winter be
removed or isolated.
A.1.2
A review of the preceding winter period is undertaken. It is critical to have a complete
review of all information and data gathered so that continuous improvement is
maintained and effective plans for future operations are developed. This not only
considers the operation of the fleet, maintenance plant and infrastructure, but also
the adequacy of the management arrangements in place for the continued operation
of depots and outstations.
A.1.3
The review of the preceding winter period includes:
a) Lessons learned;
b) Good and bad practices;
c) Review of the previous contingency planning against service delivery;
d) Fleet performance review identifying what systems failed;
e) Review of depot management and maintenance activities, for example
underframe cleaning and whether any depot systems failed such as a frozen CET
plant;
f) Infrastructure review including the effectiveness of de-icing arrangements, snow
clearance teams, switch heating or conductor rail trace heating;
g) Material management issues;
h) Staff and resourcing issues;
i)
Feedback and experience from passengers; and
j)
Feedback and review from trials / modifications of existing practice.

Appendix B
Guidance on Air Systems
B.1
Air systems general
B.1.1
Air at atmospheric pressure includes a level of moisture. Compressing the air raises
the temperature and the moisture content remains as a vapour. However, on cooling
the compressed air back to ambient temperature the moisture condenses. Addressing
this condensate within the air system enables satisfactory operation of air operated
train equipment, including pneumatic braking systems during winter.
B.1.2
The action of venting reservoirs causes a rapid expansion of the compressed air when
released. This causes a localised reduction in temperature, which when combined with
frost conditions, results in the condensate freezing and blocking drain valves. To
mitigate against the risk of the condensate freezing some rolling stock is fitted with
heated drain valves.
B.1.3
Manually draining reservoirs during frost conditions is carried out inside a depot after
giving time for the air system to reach the depot temperature. If it is necessary to
carry out the venting outside then a slow release of the air pressure reduces the risk of
the drain valve freezing.
B.1.4
It is recommended that any rolling stock taken out of service and put into temporary
storage during the winter period to have its air system drained.
B.1.5
Air intakes for compressors are susceptible to blockage during snow conditions. This
causes loss of air supply to the train. It is recommended that care be taken when
removing any build-up of snow so as not to damage the air intake filters and other
compressor mounted equipment such as the safety valves.
B.2
Air systems with air dryers
B.2.1
Rolling stock since the early 1990s has been built with air dryers fitted between the
compressors and the vehicle’s main reservoirs.
B.2.2
Air dryers are an effective means of removing moisture in the air system. As the
compressed air is passed through the air dryer the moisture is absorbed into a
desiccant which is periodically purged during the cyclic operation of the air dryer. Air
dryers are generally capable of reducing the moisture content so that the dew point is
below -40°C. The dew point is the temperature to which air is cooled for water vapour
to condense.
B.2.3
The discharge valves for the air dryers are often fitted with heating elements and it is
recommended that their operation be checked as part of the winter preparation.
B.3
Air systems with anti-freeze dispensers
B.3.1
Rolling stock not fitted with air dryers rely on drain valves (automatic or manual
operation) to permit the removal of condensation from the air system. To reduce the
risk of the condensate on the drain valves freezing, air systems are often modified to
incorporate an anti-freeze dispenser. The delivery rate for the dispenser is checked as
part of preparation for winter.

Appendix C
Guidance on Brake Systems
C.1
Causes of braking problems
C.1.1
Introduction
C.1.1.1
This appendix has been included to provide further guidance on brake related
problems. It also provides an explanation for some of the actions needed to prepare a
train for winter operation and appropriate driving techniques that can be adopted
during winter conditions.
C.2
Underframe brake equipment
C.2.1
The position of the brake rigging on the bogies and underframe of a rail vehicle
means that it is susceptible to the build-up of snow or ice. The accumulation of snow
or ice restricts the movement of the brake rigging and at worst prevents the brakes
from applying with the consequent reduction or loss of brake performance.
C.2.2
On rolling stock fitted with air brake distributors, attention is given to any build-up of
snow or ice on the distributor release mechanism, including distributor release cords.
It is recommended that any buildup of snow or ice be removed before the train enters
service.
C.2.3
Application of a de-icing fluid proves effective against low to medium accumulation
of snow and ice. When applying de-icing fluid, it is recommended that particular
attention be given to those areas where ice and snow builds up. De-icing fluids have a
limited duration effect. Their use can be repeated during a working day.
C.2.4
Following application of the de-icing fluids, drivers are advised to make a brake
application when first moving the train to remove any de-icing fluid that could have
been over sprayed onto the brake pad / disc or block / tread interface.
C.2.5
A recognised de-icing treatment for brake rigging is, for example Kilfrost Rail
(formerly known as ABC-3), BR catalogue number 007/025008.
C.3
Mechanical removal of ice and snow from brake rigging
C.3.1
To reduce the risk of damage to brake equipment, consideration can be given to
removing snow or ice build-up using alternatives to manual methods such as placing
rolling stock in warm sheds or other heated air facilities.
C.3.2
All processes for the manual removal of snow and ice from brake equipment in service
have limitations in their use. Techniques that are considered acceptable include:
moderate use of hand tools such as brushes and scrapers in a manner that has no
adverse effect or cause damage to the equipment concerned, and application of mild
heat, such as warm air generated in a manner that has no adverse effect on the
equipment in the area concerned.
C.3.3
In all cases of manual snow and ice removal, it is recommended that care be taken to
avoid damage to the area being targeted as well as adjacent equipment, especially
hoses and actuator / cylinder gaiters. It is recommended that staff are trained in the
use of these techniques and warned of potential damage to brake components which
occur from the manual removal of snow or ice.

It is good practice to avoid the use of mechanical force such as impact blows from

C.3.5
Other de-icing methods that are not recommended include application of excessive

C.3.6
Anti-icing and de-icing chemicals may cancel each other out resulting in them having
no effect. It is good practice to check the chemical compatibility if it is intended to
apply anti-icing chemicals to a surface that has been treated with de-icing chemicals.
C.4
Brake pad materials
C.4.1
The frictional performance of the brake pad material is generally unaffected by
weather conditions, including rain, when the braking effort quickly removes any
surface moisture. However, the situation is different when operating during snow or
ice conditions. Melted falling and disturbed snow passing under the train will form a
water film on the warm brake discs. This water film refreezes to form an ice layer on
the disc and can also be absorbed into the brake pad material. This wet pad
performance is one of the characteristics declared by the brake pad manufacturers.
C.4.2
European railways that operate for extended periods with snow conditions specify
that the wet brake performance of the brake pad does not deviate by more than
15% from the equivalent dry brake performance. To achieve this requirement the
brake pad often incorporates a high metal content which means that the pads have
aggressive disc wear characteristics.
C.4.3
In GB, where operation in snowy conditions is for a relatively short period, the
emphasis on brake pad selection has been for their wear properties and less
aggressive disc wear characteristics. Brake pads have thus been accepted that have a
wet brake performance that exceeds the target of 15% reduction in performance.
C.4.4
British Rail addressed the reduction in braking performance of the brake pads during
snowy conditions by a reduction in the maximum speed of disc brake stock by
imposing a 15 km/h (10 mph) reduction below line speed.
C.4.5
Railway undertakings, in conjunction with their brake pad suppliers, are to establish
the reduction in braking performance of the brake pads during snow fall and
disturbed snow conditions. It is good practice to prepare operating instructions for
the rolling stock and brief the operating staff (as detailed in section G6.2.2).
C.4.6
The ability of the brake pad to deal with snow or ice build-up on the disc is influenced
by the contact pressures generated at the brake disc face. Rolling stock in GB has
often utilised larger area brake pads of 500 cm2, compared to 400 cm2 more
commonly used in Europe. This increases the wearable material and gives a longer
pad life, however, it reduces contact pressures and the ability to clear the brake disc of
water and ice films.

C.5
Brake block materials
C.5.1
There has been a move by the GB railway industry away from cast-iron materials and
toward composite materials for tread brake applications, which are quieter in their
operation.
C.5.2
Composite brake blocks, like composite brake pads, operate satisfactorily in normal
weather conditions where the frictional performance of the brake block material is
generally unaffected by rain when the normal braking effort quickly removes any
surface moisture. However, the situation is different when operating during snowy
conditions. The freeze thaw action of snow accumulating around the brake blocks
leads to moisture being absorbed into the brake block material. This wet performance
is declared by the brake block manufacturers as part of the block characteristics.
C.5.3
Railway undertakings, in conjunction with their brake block suppliers, are to establish
the reduction in braking performance of the brake blocks during snow, ice and
disturbed snow conditions. It is good practice to prepare operating restrictions for the
rolling stock and brief to the operating staff (as detailed in section G6.2.2).
C.6
Dynamic brake
C.6.1
Dynamic brakes are a feature of electric locomotives and multiple units using electric
traction. Some DMUs have a hydrodynamic brake system that blends with the friction
brake at predetermined levels. The brake system is generally configured to utilise the
dynamic brake in preference to the friction brake, thus extending the life of the disc /
tread brake friction materials. The current designs of dynamic brake systems are not
capable of braking the train to a stop and they rely on friction brakes to make the
train come to a complete stop.
C.6.2
The dynamic brake is often capable of substituting for the friction brake during low
and medium brake applications with the friction brake only applying at low speed as
the dynamic brake fades. This operation is suitable for most operating conditions
however it has implications for operation during snow and ice conditions.
C.6.3
The friction brake functions as the main emergency brake on the train as it has to be
capable of taking over from the dynamic brake under all operating conditions. During
snow, ice or disturbed snow conditions the friction brake needs to be kept free of
snow and ice build-up so that it is always available. This is generally achieved by
regular applications of the friction brake.
C.6.4
A strategy can be produced for the operation of those trains fitted with dynamic
braking systems so that the availability of the friction brake is maintained during
snow or ice conditions. Factors that can be taken into consideration include:
a) Increased stopping pattern of the service including frequent station stops to
generate sufficient heat to keep the friction brakes available;
b) Availability to select different dynamic blending settings, such as:

C.7
Snow brake
C.7.1
Rolling stock such as the Class 390 have a snow brake feature. When initiated, the
snow brake requests a controlled brake application during normal running, whilst
maintaining traction. This effectively creates a controlled dragging brake at high
operating speeds thereby generating sufficient heat to remove any ice from the
brake disc surfaces, keeping the brake actuators free and operational.
C.7.2
Regular use of the snow brake enables the friction brakes to remain operational when
required whilst still retaining the use of the dynamic brake. The procedures for using
the snow brake feature are dependent on the particular type of rolling stock. It is
good practice to prepare operating instructions for the rolling stock and brief the
drivers.

application for 15 seconds. There is no requirement to
shut off power during this action. A repeated snow brake
application cannot be made within a three-minute period
following previous snow brake activations.
C.8
Braking-related winter driving techniques
C.8.1
The build-up of snow or ice compromises the availability and performance of the
friction brake systems on rolling stock. The accepted remedial action across Europe is
frequent application of the service brake to generate heat in the friction brakes to
remove ice from disc surfaces and keep the brake callipers and rigging free.
C.8.2
Regular and repeated full service applications generate the highest contact forces
between the brake pad / disc and block / tread necessary to remove snow or ice build-
up on the brake equipment. The full service brake application is used and maintained
until the driver has confidence that the brakes are functioning satisfactorily and a
speed reduction of 15 km/h (10 mph) is observed.
C.8.3
It is recommended that light brake applications are avoided on trains fitted with
composite brake materials as this could cause any moisture from melted snow and ice
to be absorbed into the friction brake material. The absorbed moisture then refreezes
with the consequence that the friction performance of the brake material is degraded
until sufficient heat has been generated to dry out the material.
C.8.4
When snow is falling or there is significant movement of fallen snow, the
performance of the friction materials is reduced due to the presence of moisture

C.9
Train operating on falling gradients
C.9.1
Whilst descending gradients during snow and ice conditions, it is good practice for
drivers to make frequent full service applications of the train brakes. This generates
heat within and maintains movement of the brake system components thereby
reducing the likelihood of the brake system freezing.
C.9.2
If the driver has any concerns about the brake performance of the train in snow or ice
conditions a full service brake application is can be maintained and the train brought
to a stand to avoid the risk of an uncontrolled descent.
C.9.3
During snow and ice conditions it is recommended that drivers reduce speed when
approaching falling gradients and maintain a lower speed during the descent, even if
the driver is satisfied that the brakes are operating correctly.
C.10
Trains operating on rising gradients
C.10.1
When ascending rising gradients during snow and ice conditions it is often impractical
to carry out frequent running brake tests. This increases the risk of brake equipment
becoming adversely affected by the prevailing conditions leading to a reduction in
braking performance. Therefore, drivers are to carry out a running brake test where
this is not likely to bring the train to a stand or cause serious difficulties in ascending
the gradient.
C.10.2
As soon as the train reaches the summit the driver is to make a full service brake
application to ensure that the brake is still functioning correctly, and observe a
positive reduction in speed of at least 15 km/h (10 mph).
C.10.3
A prolonged full service brake application may be necessary to remove any snow or
ice build-up that has accumulated on the brakes during the ascent of the gradient.
C.11
Post-incident investigations
C.11.1
Following an incident, when the braking performance of a train has been
compromised by the winter weather, it is important to preserve perishable evidence.
Consideration can be given to undertaking a practical brake test immediately
(post-incident) with appropriate control measures in place (it may not be appropriate
to return the train to a depot as this could permit any ice / snow or frozen equipment
to melt before undertaking any testing).

Appendix D
Guidance on Coupler Reliability
D.1
Causes of coupler problems
D.1.1
Introduction
D.1.1.1
The coupler problems considered in this appendix are those directly related to
restricted or compromised coupler operation due to the effects of snow or ice. This is
either as a result of snow and ice accumulation making it difficult or impossible to
couple or uncouple, or due to such issues as water ingress from melting snow and ice
affecting electrical signals between vehicles.
D.1.1.2
The guidance set out in this appendix is supported by the findings of the RSSB
Research Project T958 ‘Ensuring coupler reliability during ice and snow’ that was
commissioned after the winter of 2010. The project identified indicative costs
associated with implementing different coupler preventative and recovery strategies.
D.1.2
Meteorological factors
D.1.2.1
Dry snow with minimal liquid content is the most problematic snow type when it is
blown into small gaps on couplers and underframe equipment. Snowfall greater than
3 cm per day with ambient temperatures at or below -4°C leads to rapid snow and ice
build-up on couplers.
D.1.2.2
Temperature fluctuations around freezing point cause particular problems with
thawing and refreezing of snow and ice build-up. This process is induced by a number
of processes such as wind-chill and heat convection from vehicle movement, as well as
localised temperature variations and overall ambient temperature change along a
route of operation.
D.1.3
Coupling and uncoupling activities
D.1.3.1
The frequency of scheduled coupling and uncoupling activities influence the effect of
snow and ice on the reliability of affected couplers. A railway undertaking that has no
or infrequent in-service coupling or uncoupling activities is better able to manage the
effect of any adversely affected couplers on their operation.
D.1.4
Type and design of coupler
D.1.4.1
The type of coupler fitted to any class of rolling stock has an effect on the severity of
any problems experienced. Different coupler types have different issues associated
with them.
D.1.4.2
BSI compact and Tightlock couplers have electrical heads which engage before the
mechanical coupler heads which increase the likelihood of misalignment and damage
to seals due to the impact. Dellner and Scharfenberg latch type couplers mechanically
engage before the electrical heads are coupled.
D.1.4.3
The electrical heads on couplers, when positioned below the mechanical head, are
prone to penetration of de-icing fluids, melting snow and ice draining from the
coupler above.
D.1.4.4
Other design aspects that influence the coupler performance in winter are rolling
stock fitted with buckeye couplers that have been exposed to winter weather. It is
recommended that these couplers are cleared of snow and ice build-up before

D.2
Solutions available
D.2.1
Introduction to available solutions
D.2.1.1
In identifying available solutions to mitigate or prevent coupler problems, no single
solution is applicable to all rolling stock. From the potential solutions listed, it is
considered that a mix and match approach is more appropriate. Tables 5 and 6 in

b) Recovery strategies such as actions taken after snow or ice build-up have been

D.2.1.2
The appropriate action to mitigate or avoid coupler problems in adverse winter
weather conditions is dependent upon the winter preparation and the use of the
trigger conditions in Part 2. Assessment of the actions also consider the
implementation time against the forecast duration of the weather conditions. For
example, the time taken to fit the whole fleet with coupler bags could be longer than
the duration of the actual snow and ice conditions.
D.2.1.3
The solutions detailed in this appendix are viable practices that have been
successfully used in GB and elsewhere. This does not preclude the use of alternative or
improved strategies which could be developed in the future.
D.3
Preventative strategies
D.3.1
Corrective maintenance - long term
D.3.1.1
All scheduled maintenance is undertaken to keep coupler mechanisms in optimum
condition prior to the start of winter. Periodic inspections are to also be carried out to
check that any covers and seals, which are essential for preventing the ingress of
moisture, are in place and are serviceable and sealing correctly.
D.3.1.2
Consideration is given to carrying out a dedicated winter preparation campaign for
couplers in the months leading up to winter. For rolling stock operating in
semi-permanent formations, the temporary removal of the buckeye release chains to
reduce the risk of unintended uncoupling due to ice build-up operating the release
mechanism is undertaken.

D.3.2.1

as high an ambient temperature as possible, rolling stock are stabled under cover or

D.4
Recovery solutions
D.4.1
Manual removal of snow and ice
D.4.1.1
All processes for the manual removal of snow or ice from couplers in service have
limitations in their use, the optimum solution is to prevent such accumulation. It is
recognised that manual removal is necessary as an on-going requirement.
D.4.1.2
Techniques that are considered acceptable include:
a) Moderate use of hand tools such as brushes and scrapers; and
b) Application of mild heat such as warm air to the affected area.
D.4.1.3
In all cases of manual snow and ice removal, it is good practice for care be taken to
avoid damage to the area being treated as well as any adjacent equipment,
especially cables and electrical contacts. It is recommended that training in the use of
these techniques, and warnings of the potential damage to coupler components, is
provided.
D.4.1.4
Manual techniques that are avoided include:
a) High levels of mechanical force using inappropriate methods, for example, impact
blows from hammers and chisels;
b) Application of hot air or excessive heat, for example, a propane flame; and
c) Use of hot water, steam or any other unapproved fluids and chemicals.
D.4.2
De-icing and anti-icing chemicals
D.4.2.1
Application of a de-icing fluid is proven effective against low to medium
accumulations of snow or ice. De-icing fluids however have a limited duration and
their use may be necessary on repeated occasions during a working day.
D.4.2.2
It is good practice for anti-icing fluids to only be used where approved by the coupler
manufacturer and they are demonstrably safe and not detrimental to any coupler
components, especially contacts and pin carriers (contact blocks) in the electrical
head. Currently, no coupler manufacturers have approved the use of any anti-icing
products nor has there been any suitable investigation into any detrimental effects
on the couplers.
D.4.2.3
Whenever a preventative anti-icing build-up chemical is applied, it is recommended
that the surface is clean, dry and free from any de-icing chemicals.
Note: Anti-icing and de-icing chemicals may cancel each other out resulting in
them having no effect. Chemical compatibility is to be checked if it is intended
to apply anti-icing chemicals to a surface that has been treated with a de-icing
chemical.

Contingency plan
A specific action plan developed by infrastructure managers and

predicted to occur the next day. Contingency plans permit revision
to normal operation the following day.
DMU
Diesel Multiple Unit.
Dry snow
Form of precipitation where tiny ice crystals bond together into
flakes, which have little to no liquid water content and a particle
size of about 20 μm. The density of this snow is approximately
200 kg/m3 and can go up to 350 kg/m3 if wind pressed as set out in
BS EN 16251:2011.
ECM

General preparation
A series of defined actions to be implemented in advance when
trigger events are forecast.
Ghost train
A train consisting of more than one vehicle used to open or keep a
line open. They may be a dedicated formation of rolling stock, an
empty stock working of the normal service trains or a freight train.
Ground heave
The localised raising of the ground caused by the expansion of
freezing water below the surface.
Joule-Thomson effect
Is the change in temperature that accompanies expansion of a gas
without production of work or the transfer of heat. At ordinary
temperatures and pressures, all real gases except hydrogen and
helium cool upon such expansion.
