Synopsis
This document mandates requirements
to be applied to railway vehicles so that
they can operate under gale conditions in
Great Britain. It applies to vehicles with
conventional cant deficiency and design
speeds not exceeding 140 km/h, and to
all vehicles operating above conventional
cant deficiencies and design speeds
below 250km/h. The objective is to
minimise the likelihood of vehicle roll-
over.

Part 1
Purpose and Introduction
1.1
Purpose
1.1.1
This document mandates requirements to be applied to railway vehicles so that
the vehicles can operate safely in the prevailing wind conditions in Great Britain
(GB). The objective is to minimise the likelihood of vehicle roll-over.
1.1.2
This document sets out specific limits and constraints associated with the speeds
of cross winds to be used in the design of a rail vehicles. It applies to vehicles
with conventional cant deficiency and design speeds not exceeding 140 km/h and
to all vehicles operating above conventional cant deficiencies and design speeds
below 250km/h. These requirements are necessary to achieve technical
compatibility between vehicles and infrastructure, because they are not
addressed by the Locomotives and Passenger Rolling Stock Technical
Specification for Interoperability (LOC & PAS TSI)
1.2
Introduction
1.2.1
Background
1.2.1.1
The underlying principle of this document is that all vehicles are required to meet
or exceed a minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speed, as set out in
clause 2.1 of this document. The minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind
speed should not be viewed as an actual maximum wind speed that a vehicle will
experience in service conditions. It is a reference wind speed on straight track
that, when used in the method described, will ensure that the vehicle can operate
safely in the prevailing wind conditions in GB.
1.2.1.2
Appendix A of this document sets out the method to be used to calculate the
intrinsic roll-over wind speed for a vehicle.
1.2.1.3
Further guidance on the technical background to clause 2.1, clause 2.2 and
Appendix A of this document is given in Appendix B. Appendix C provides a
calculated example.
1.2.1.4
For trains travelling at higher than conventional cant deficiencies, GC/RT5021
requires a probability assessment to be carried out to determine the enhanced
permissible speeds of the train on each curve on a route. GC/RC5521 sets out a
methodology for undertaking the required probability assessment. The
aerodynamic characteristics of critical vehicles within the train formation are a key
factor in the probability assessment. Section 2.2 of this document therefore
requires information about vehicle characteristics to be determined and recorded
so that the information can be supplied to the infrastructure manager as part of
checking compatibility between the vehicle and the infrastructure on the specific
route (GE/RT8270) and for the infrastructure manager to take suitable mitigation
actions as necessary. Further recommendations relating to this aspect are
provided in GM/RC2542.
1.2.1.5
Additional background information about rail vehicle roll-over in gales is given in
BS EN 14067-6:2010.
1.2.1.6
Deleted
1.2.2
Related requirements in other documents
1.2.2.1
GM/RT2141 sets out the design requirements for rail vehicles to ensure
acceptable resistance against flange climbing derailment and against roll-over
induced by over speeding.

Part 2
Requirements to Minimise the Risk of Vehicle
Roll-Over in Gales
2.1
Design criteria
2.1.1
Minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speed
2.1.1.1
The intrinsic roll-over wind speed (vw,int) for a vehicle running in tare condition,
within a train formation at its maximum design operating speed on straight and
level track, shall not be less than the following minimum acceptable intrinsic
roll-over wind speeds:
a)
36.5 m/s for vehicles designed to carry people, including passengers and
crew.
b)
26.3 m/s for freight and other vehicles not designed to carry people.
2.1.1.2
The intrinsic roll-over wind speed (vw,int) shall be determined in accordance with
Appendix A.
2.1.2
Vehicles not required to operate over exposed routes
2.1.2.1
Where a vehicle is not designed or required to be operated over exposed routes it
is permissible to reduce the minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speed.
In this case it is permissible to apply a reduction factor of 0.92 to the minimum
acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speeds set out in clause 2.1.1.1.
2.1.2.2
Where the requirements set out in 2.1.2.1 are applied, any limitations of use shall
be recorded in the Technical File for the vehicle and identified clearly on any
associated acceptance certification produced for the vehicle.
2.1.3
Variations in vehicle condition
2.1.3.1
Vehicles shall meet the requirements of this document over the full range of
variations in vehicle condition that are likely to be experienced. As a minimum,
variations in vehicle condition shall include tolerances in vehicle dimensions,
mass distributions and wheel loadings, suspension characteristics, normal
variations in vehicle maintenance condition and wear, and any other relevant
variables.
2.2
Vehicle characteristics for vehicles operating above conventional cant
deficiencies
2.2.1
Vehicle characteristics required for determining maximum enhanced permissible
speeds
2.2.1.1
The following characteristics for a vehicle designed to carry people that operates
at cant deficiencies greater than 6°, or a freight vehicle that operates at cant
deficiencies greater than 4.25°, shall be determined and recorded:
a)
Maximum design operating speed.
b)
Total projected side area of vehicle or side area used to determine its
aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient about the lee rail from tests as set out
in A.5.1.
c)
Vehicle mean longitudinal roof height above axle centreline or height used to
determine its aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient about the lee rail from
tests as set out in A.5.1.

Part 3
Application of this document
3.1
Scope
3.1.1
Except as set out in section 3.2.1, the requirements of this document apply to all
new rail vehicles, including on-track machines (OTMs) in running mode. It
applies to all vehicles operating either:
a)
with conventional cant deficiencies and design speeds not exceeding 140
km/h; or
b)
with above conventional cant deficiencies and design speeds below 250
km/h.
3.1.2
When vehicles are undergoing major modifications that have the potential to
affect compliance with the requirements in this document then the requirements
of this document are applicable
3.1.3
Action to bring existing railway vehicles into compliance with the requirements of
this document is not required
3.2
Exclusions from scope
3.2.1
The requirements in the document are not applicable to the following types of
vehicles:
a)
General Contract of Use (GCU) wagons.
b)
Vehicles designed to operate in excess of 140 km/h at conventional cant
deficiencies.
c)
Vehicles designed to operate at speeds equal to or higher than 250 km/h.
3.3
General compliance date
3.3.1
This Railway Group Standard comes into force and is to be complied with from
07 March 2015, except as specified in clause 3.4.1. Where the date specified in
clauses 3.4.1 below is later than the above date, this is to allow sufficient time to
achieve compliance with the specified exceptions.
3.3.2
After the compliance date, or the date by which compliance is achieved if earlier,
compliance with the requirements set out in this Railway Group Standard is to be
maintained. Where it is considered not reasonably practicable to comply with the
requirements, permission to comply with a specified alternative is sought in
accordance with the Railway Group Standards Code.
3.4
Exceptions to general compliance date
3.4.1
It is permissible for vehicles to continue to be built to the same design as a
vehicle having an authorisation for placing into service or a Certificate of
Engineering Acceptance, but which does not comply with the requirements of this
document, until 05 September 2017.

Health and safety responsibilities

B.2
Determination of vehicle side area (A)
B.2.1
For a vehicle with little or no underframe equipment, the vehicle side area (A)
shall be determined from the sum of the side area of the projected body side to
the bottom of the sole bar, plus the side areas of the bogies down to 420 mm
above the top of rail (extended to the front of the train for the leading vehicle).
This includes half the area of the intercar connections, but excludes small
attachments to the carbody (for example external exhausts). An example of a
vehicle with little or no underframe equipment, fairings or skirts is shown in
Figure A.2.

Figure B.2
Example of a vehicle with side area (A) enclosed in red with little or no
underframe equipment, fairings or skirts
B.2.2
For a vehicle with underframe equipment which form continuous blockages to the
flow under the vehicle (with gaps smaller than 50 mm between adjacent
equipment components) or a vehicle having underframe fairings or skirts, the
vehicle side area (A) shall be determined as set out in clause A.2.1. The area
shall also include the area of the underframe equipment forming continuous
blockages, fairings or skirts. An example of a vehicle with underframe equipment
causing a continuous blockage is shown in Figure A.3. In this example, the three
equipment components have gaps between them of less than 50 mm and their
areas are included, the other equipment components are isolated and more than
50 mm from adjacent equipment and their areas are ignored.

Figure B.3
Example of a vehicle with side area (A) enclosed in red having underframe
equipment causing a continuous blockage
B.2.3
In case of doubt about the inclusion of underframe equipment side area, the
vehicle side area (A) shall include the side area of all individual pieces of
underframe equipment unless a specialist in the field of rail vehicle aerodynamics
advises that it can be ignored. For vehicles that carry complex shaped loads, the
worst case projected side area (largest) shall be determined and used for the
calculation of the intrinsic roll-over wind speed.

B.3
Determination of vehicle reference height (H)
B.3.1
The vehicle reference height (H) shall be the distance between the mean
longitudinal roof height and the rail minus 420 mm. An example of the vehicle
reference height (H) for a vehicle with wheels of 420 mm radius is shown in
Figure A.4.





Figure B.4
Example of vehicle reference height (H), based on mean roof height
B.4
Vehicles with articulated bogies
B.4.1
For vehicles with articulated bogies, or with other forms of weight support
arrangements between adjacent vehicles, masses shall be appropriately shared
between vehicles for the purpose of calculating the restoring moment (MR).
B.5
Values for the aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient about the lee
rail CMx,lee(β)
B.5.1
Unless more accurate values for the aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient
about the lee rail (CMx,lee(β)) are obtained from wind tunnel tests using the track
model described in GM/RC2542, or are inferred from existing data, for
aerodynamically similar vehicles (see clause B.3.3.1), the values for CMx,lee(β)
shall be determined in accordance with clause A.5.3 and clause A.5.4.
B.5.2
The terms used to describe roof profiles in Table A.1 and Table A.2 (for example
streamlined) are defined in the definitions. They refer to the vehicle
cross-sectional profiles. The choice of streamlined, intermediate and
unstreamlined is available for vehicles designed to carry people. Vehicles not
designed to carry people are only permitted to use the freight classification.

Appendix C Technical Background and Guidance
The content of this appendix is not mandatory and is provided for guidance only.
C.1
Roll-over design criteria
C.1.1
Introduction
C.1.1.1 The acceptance criterion in clause 2.1.1.1 requires that the intrinsic roll-over wind
speed (vw,int) for a vehicle is not less than a prescribed value.
C.1.1.2 The minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speeds in clause 2.1.1.1 are
those associated with the benchmark vehicles set out in clause B.3.1.5.
C.1.1.3 If a vehicle operates at higher than conventional cant deficiencies, then the
probability of a vehicle roll-over on its proposed route(s) of operation is to be
determined. Such probability assessments are based on vehicle characteristics
determined in accordance with clause 2.2.1.1.
C.1.2
Minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speed (see 2.1.1)
C.1.2.1 It is anticipated that most vehicles will meet the requirements of clause 2.1.1.1.
However, some vehicles may fail this simple test. In such cases, the vehicle may
actually have sufficient resistance to roll-over in gales to operate safely. In these
cases, an application for a deviation from this document can be submitted in
accordance with the Railway Group Standards Code. The deviation will need to
be supported by evidence that the vehicle’s resistance to roll-over in gales is
sufficient, taking account of the vehicle’s particular characteristics. The technical
issues involved in vehicle roll-over are complex and advice taken from a
specialist in the field of rail vehicle aerodynamics should be sought.
C.1.2.2 The intrinsic roll-over wind speed is calculated for the case of a vehicle running
on a straight and level track only.
C.1.3
Vehicles not required to operate over exposed routes (see 2.1.2)
C.1.3.1 For railway vehicles that are only required to operate over less exposed routes,
the application of the benchmark vehicles’ intrinsic roll-over wind speeds would
result in a much lower level of risk, which could be unnecessarily restrictive.
Therefore, with the aim of equalising risk, reduced values of minimum acceptable
intrinsic roll-over wind speed are permitted. This is specified by the reduction
factor of 0.92 in clause 2.1.2.1.
C.1.3.2 Because application of the reduction factor is only acceptable if a vehicle is not
required to run over exposed routes, vehicles exploiting this factor will not be able
to be used on all routes in GB.
C.2
Calculation of intrinsic roll-over wind speed
C.2.1
Calculation principle
C.2.1.1 The roll-over wind speed (vw) for a vehicle is that wind speed which is just
sufficient to cause 100 % unloading of the wheels on the windward side of the
vehicle, when the vehicle is running in its tare condition at its maximum design
operating speed on straight track.
C.2.1.2 When the windward side wheels are 100 % unloaded, the aerodynamic rolling
moment (MA) is balanced by the restoring moment due to vehicle weight (MR).
The calculation of the vehicle’s roll-over wind speed (vw) is therefore carried out in
the condition when MA = MR.

C.2.7
Determining conformity with clause 2.1.1.1
C.2.7.1 The equations developed in clause B.2.1 to clause B.2.6 form the process to
determine the vehicle’s intrinsic roll-over wind speed as set out in clause A.1.
The minimum value of the roll-over wind speeds determined is the vehicle’s
intrinsic roll-over wind speed for the purpose of determining conformity with
clause 2.1.1.1.
C.2.7.2 For most vehicles, the worst case wind angle, which minimises the value of a
vehicle’s roll-over wind speed, usually lies in the range  = 70° to 85°. It is not
however possible to determine in advance if it is outside this range. Therefore,
the required wind angle range for the intrinsic roll-over wind speed calculations is
between 30° and 90°. This wind angle range is usually covered by starting with a
resultant wind angle range of  = 10° to 40°.
C.3
Values for the aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient about the lee
rail, CMx,lee(β) (see clause A.5.1)
C.3.1
Concept of benchmark vehicles
C.3.1.1 The basic roll-over risk is coupled with the concept of benchmark vehicles.
C.3.1.2 The intrinsic roll-over wind speed defines under specified operating conditions
what wind force and hence, what wind speed is required to cause a vehicle to
roll-over.
C.3.1.3 Benchmark vehicles have been chosen which, whilst being relatively light in
weight per unit side area, have safe histories of operation over exposed routes in
GB. They therefore define benchmarks in terms of actual acceptable levels of
risk at typical frequencies of operation. Having accepted those levels of risk, their
intrinsic roll-over wind speeds are themselves adopted as benchmarks.
C.3.1.4 The values of aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient about the lee rail (CMx,lee())
given in Table A.1 and Table A.2 have been derived from low turbulence tests in
two comparable wind tunnels for a range of railway vehicles. The categorised
curves for different roof profiles are in general conservatively chosen for vehicles
of the given type. The curves, when chosen appropriately, will ultimately allow
conservative estimates to be made of intrinsic roll-over wind speeds. This
conservatism and uncertainty can be removed by undertaking wind tunnel tests
for the actual vehicle under consideration (see clause B.3.3).
C.3.1.5 The values of minimum acceptable intrinsic roll-over wind speeds are set out in
clause 2.1.1.1 and are based on values determined by considering railway
vehicles which have had long periods of widespread service in GB. The vehicles
were representative of passenger carrying or freight vehicles with different
aerodynamic characteristics. The vehicles were the Mark 3 Coach and the
Class 108 ‘Derby Lightweight’ Diesel Multiple Unit for passenger carrying vehicles
and a FEA (B) vehicle with 60’ of empty 9’ 6” containers for freight vehicles. The
method for determining a vehicle’s intrinsic roll-over wind speed, as outlined in
this document, was used to derive the benchmark values of minimum acceptable
intrinsic roll-over wind speeds.

C.3.2.1 Some vehicles designed to carry people are not easily placed into the named

vehicle aerodynamics.
C.3.2.2 The aerodynamic design of the leading end of a vehicle, unless the end is
particularly long, has no significant effect on the overturning moment and is not a
factor in determining the classification of roof profile to be used.
C.3.3
Accurate values of CMx,lee(() relevant to actual vehicles
C.3.3.1 More accurate values of CMx,lee() relevant to the actual vehicle under
consideration can be obtained from suitable wind tunnel tests on a scale model of
the vehicle. In this case, values of the vehicle side area (A) and mean
longitudinal roof height (H) above axle centreline may differ from those described
in clause A.2 and clause A.3. It is important that the appropriate values are used
to determine the correct aerodynamic rolling moment coefficient.
C.3.3.2 GM/RC2542 sets out a methodology for undertaking wind tunnel testing to
determine the aerodynamic force and moment in more detail.
C.3.3.3 For vehicles that are similar aerodynamically to other vehicles for which values of
CMx,lee(β) already exist, it may be possible to infer new values of CMx,lee(β) from
existing data. Such evaluations should be validated and endorsed by a specialist
in the field of rail vehicle aerodynamics.

C.4.1
Vehicle characteristics required for determining maximum enhanced permissible
speeds (see clause 2.2.1)
C.4.1.1 For a vehicle operating at higher than conventional cant deficiencies (for example
a tilting train),vehicle specific information is to be provided to satisfy the
requirements of clause 2.2.1.1. This enables the assessment of the likely
increased susceptibility to roll-over in gales.
C.4.1.2 For vehicles designed to curve at higher speeds and at higher than conventional
cant deficiencies, roll-over behaviour during curving also needs to be considered.
It is likely that the roll-over wind speeds on curves will be less than those of
existing stock of comparable aerodynamic characteristics travelling on the same
curves. It is thus necessary to determine the vehicle characteristics, as set out in
clause 2.2.1.1, such that the probability of roll-over for a tilting train over its
proposed routes of operation can be assessed. GC/RT5021 and GC/RC5521 set
out the requirements for calculation of enhanced permissible speed for tilting
trains, including the determination of the roll-over probability, taking into account
train, infrastructure and environmental conditions. These calculations are to
ensure that the probability of roll-over is acceptable.

Definitions
Cant deficiency
The difference between (i) the angle to which track would have to be canted on a curve to
just counterbalance the centrifugal forces acting on a vehicle, and (ii) the actual cant angle
of the track.
Exposed route
An exposed route is an existing railway route for which the value of the fundamental basic
wind velocity before the altitude correction is applied, vb,map is ≥ 22 m/s. vb,map is given in
Figure NA.1 in the National Annex (NA) to BS EN 1991-1-4:2005.
Freight roof profile
The roof profile of a vehicle characterised by all or most of the following: extensive
discontinuities in body profile with large irregular inter-vehicle gaps, corners are sharp and
extensive bluff surfaces are presented to the wind. Examples of such vehicles are the
MGR coal-hopper wagon, flat wagons with containers and the open car-carrier vehicle.
Gales
Extreme wind conditions, specifically gust wind speeds above 30 m/s (67 mile/h) as
reported by the Meteorological Office in broadcast gale warnings.
General Contract of Use (GCU) wagons
An internationally registered wagon built in compliance with UIC / EN standards.
Intrinsic roll-over wind speed
The wind speed that is just sufficient to cause a vehicle to roll-over when (i) the vehicle is
running within a train formation at its maximum design operating speed on straight and
level track, and (ii) the wind blows at such an angle to the direction of travel of the train that
the roll-over wind speed is minimised.
Lee Rail
The rail about which a vehicle will begin to rotate at roll-over.
Maximum design operating speed
The maximum speed at which a train is designed and approved to travel.
On-track machine (OTMs)
On track machines (OTMs) are vehicles specially designed for infrastructure work. OTMs
are transported on rail wheels and can be self-propelled or hauled. The machine is
specifically designed to have characteristics necessary for the operation of track based
train detection systems.
Permissible or enhanced permissible speed
The maximum speed published in the Sectional Appendix at which traffic is allowed to run
on a line.
Roll-over
The situation reached when all the wheels on one side of a vehicle reach 100 % unloading
with their running rail and the whole weight of the vehicle is supported by the wheels on the
other running rail.
Roll-over probability
The probability that a vehicle will roll-over when exposed to gales in conjunction with the
normal range of operating speeds and cant deficiencies.

Roof profile
•
Intermediate roof profile
The roof profile of a vehicle which is mainly dome shaped. Examples of vehicles
with such roof profiles include: Mark 3 Coach, Class 158 and Class 321 multiple
unit vehicles and Mark 4 driver van trailer (DVT).

•
Streamlined roof profile
The roof profile of a vehicle which is mainly flat or which has a large radius main
part and which is not sharp edged (as defined). An example of a vehicle with such
a roof profile is the Class 142 (excluding the roof-mounted equipment pack).

•
Round-topped wagons
Freight wagons with rounded roof corners, whether generated by curved or
facetted sections that follows the gauge profile, such as the HYA hopper wagon
and tank wagons.

Unstreamlined roof profile
The roof profile of a vehicle which is sharp edged (as defined) for 25 % or more of
the vehicle length. In the case of a pantograph well with a side fairing which
continues the general vehicle roof profile, this is to be treated as sharp edged if the
side fairing stops within the cant rail corner radius. Examples of vehicles with such
roof profiles are the Class 87 and Class 91 locomotives. Class 390 vehicles are
also considered to have an unstreamlined roof profile, as they have sharp edged
roof fairings.

Sharp edged (of a roof profile)
Having an average radius at the cant rail less than 20 % of the body width measured just
below cant rail height.
Specialist in the field of rail vehicle aerodynamics
An expert with expertise in the aerodynamics of railway or other ground-borne vehicles and
is recognised in the United Kingdom or internationally, and is one of the following:
a)
A member or group of staff or a department of a university or similar
institution.
b)
A research or combined research and consultancy group in private industry.
Or
c)
An individual or group within any railway organisation.
Tilting train
A train that has a system which tilts the train body to reduce the lateral acceleration
experienced by passengers when operating around curves, allowing the train to run at
higher speeds through curves than non-tilting trains.
Yaw angle
The resultant wind angle relative to the train caused by the combination of the forward
movement of the train and the wind.
