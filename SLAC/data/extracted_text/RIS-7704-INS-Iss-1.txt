Part 1
Purpose and Introduction
1.1
Purpose
1.1.1
This document is a standard on calculating enhanced permissible speeds for tilting
trains, for members of RSSB to use if they so choose.
1.1.2
This document sets out requirements and guidance for train manufacturers or
operators to calculate enhanced permissible speeds for curves on a particular route of
the Great Britain (GB) mainline railway.
1.1.3
The method outlined is applied when tilting trains are introduced on a route or when
increases are proposed to existing enhanced permissible speeds on a route.
1.2
Introduction
1.2.1
The design of track curves and the rules governing cant and cant deficiency are
detailed within track design manuals.
1.2.2
With the advent of tilting trains, particularly the Class 390 Pendolino units, there was
the recognition that an additional design process was required to establish how to
define safe enhanced permissible speeds for specific routes of operation for tilting
trains.
1.2.3
A train manufacturer / operator who seeks to either introduce new tilting stock to the
GB system, or seeks to cascade existing tilting stock to new lines of operation may
develop their own design process by which enhanced permissible speeds for curves on
a particular route can be calculated. This document is one option that can be adopted
to provide a methodology for the calculations required. It brings together the
experience gained in the introduction of the Class 390 units and details a probabilistic
approach to safe operation. It should be noted that this process is not one that can
be adopted in part; if a designer chooses to adopt this approach then the document
should be followed in full.
1.3
Principles
1.3.1
Cant deficiencies higher than those specified for the calculation of permissible speeds
are permitted for tilting trains travelling at enhanced permissible speeds around
curves. Higher cant deficiencies may bring a higher probability of overturning if no
additional controls are imposed.
1.3.2
In addition to the basic factors of curve radius and cant, the important factors
determining the likelihood of a train overturning on a particular curve are:
a) The dynamic roll-over resistance of the train.
b) Loss of cant.
c) Horizontal misalignment of the curve.
d) Gust wind speed at the particular curve location.
e) The aerodynamic resistance of the train to wind.
f) The speed of the train and the extent to which overspeed can occur.

1.3.3
The maximum speed at which a train could travel round a curve without a significant
probability of overturning is calculated taking the factors above into account,
together with a safety margin. This safety margin is an allowance for errors and
uncertainties in the determination of the factors input into the calculation. The
maximum speed is used to determine the type of train speed control required in
accordance with RIS-8012-CCS.
1.3.4
The enhanced permissible speed for a particular curve is a speed at which the risk of
overturning is considered acceptable. The enhanced permissible speed is calculated
probabilistically, taking into account the extent to which speed beyond the maximum
safe speed can occur. The degree to which overspeed can occur will depend on the
train speed control system in place at a particular curve.
1.3.5
The wind speed at a particular curve location and the extent to which overspeed can
occur are not deterministic. Therefore, a probabilistic methodology to combine these
factors is used to ensure that the enhanced permissible speed selected is as high as it
can safely be. (A non-deterministic method would result in lower values of enhanced
permissible speeds.)
1.3.6
The acceptability of the risk of overturning is determined by application of the
principles of the Common Safety Method for Risk Evaluation and Assessment (CSM
RA).
1.3.7
These principles are described in more detail in Appendix A.
1.3.8
The process for determining enhanced permissible speeds described in this standard
is shown in Figure 1. The process should be used in its entirety if adopted.

Part 2
Requirements for Enhanced Permissible Speeds Calculation
2.1
Choice of vehicle for the risk calculation
2.1.1
The overturning risk calculations shall be made for the vehicle of the train with the
lowest overturning wind speed.
Rationale
G 2.1.2
It is essential that the most critical vehicle can travel around curves at enhanced
permissible speed when exposed to crosswinds at an acceptably low probability of
overturning.
Guidance
G 2.1.3
The vehicle of the train with lowest overturning speed is the most critical vehicle and
therefore is chosen for the risk calculation. Other vehicles in the train travelling
around the curve at the same train and crosswind speeds will have a lower probability
of overturning than the critical vehicle.
G 2.1.4
Aerodynamic cant deficiency coefficient sets out the recommended approach to be
taken if it is not known which vehicle is critical.
2.2
Division of the route into sections
2.2.1
The route over which it is intended to run tilting trains at an enhanced permissible
speed shall be divided into route sections.
Rationale
G 2.2.2
Dividing the complete route into route sections makes it easier to develop arguments
for the acceptability of risk from overturning when applying explicit risk estimation or
when comparing with existing reference vehicles using the route sections (similar
reference systems) according to the CSM RA.
Guidance
G 2.2.3
Long-distance routes, such as the West Coast Main Line (WCML), are comprised of
sections of line having different characteristics, (for example, number of curves,
different exposure to the wind and operational frequencies), which distinguish the
various parts. In addition, sections of the routes between main population centres can
be considered broadly representative of long-distance commuting runs. Typically,
route sections are about 100 miles in length, although distances between main
population centres will be a key determinant.
2.3
Target enhanced permissible speeds on curves in the route section
2.3.1
For each curve over which trains travel at enhanced permissible speeds within the
route section, a target enhanced permissible speed shall be determined.

Rationale
G 2.3.2
The target enhanced permissible speed is the aspirational train speed through the
curve and forms the basis for the initial determination of the overturning risk for
trains operating on the curve.
Guidance
G 2.3.3
The conditions under which trains may travel at an enhanced permissible speed are
set out in RIS-8012-CCS.
G 2.3.4
The following track factors are considered when calculating permissible and
enhanced permissible speeds:
a) The radius of the curve.
b) The applied cant.
c) The permitted values of cant deficiency.
d) The permitted values of rates of change of cant and cant deficiency on the
transitions either side of the curve.
G 2.3.5
GCRT5021 sets out further information on:
a) Permitted values of curve radius and applied cant at permissible and enhanced
permissible speeds.
b) Permitted values of cant deficiency and rates of change of cant and cant
deficiency at enhanced permissible speeds and permissible speeds.
c) Enhanced permissible speeds on the through route of S&C.
G 2.3.6
The following additional factors are considered when calculating enhanced
permissible speeds and are considered in more detail in this document:
a) The maximum cant deficiency at which the train is designed to travel (see D.3).
b) The dynamic roll-over resistance of the train (see D.3).
c) The maintenance tolerances on cant (see D.4).
d) The maintenance tolerances on radius (see D.4).
e) The expected local wind conditions (see D.3).
f) The effect of wind on the train, taking into account the characteristics of the train
(see D.3).
g) The system adopted for controlling the speed of the train and the extent to which
overspeed can occur (see D.3).
h) A safety margin equivalent to no less than 50 mm of cant deficiency (see A.3).
G 2.3.7
The initial target enhanced permissible speed is selected to meet operational
aspirations, for example, for reduced journey times. Usually, this will be the maximum
attainable speed using the limiting service cant deficiency, which is the lower of the
following two values:
a) The maximum design service cant deficiency supplied by the train operator for the
particular train considered.
b) The limiting design value for cant deficiency, appropriate to the design radius of
the curve under consideration, set out in GCRT5021.

In this case, the target speed is calculated using the following relationship:

2.4
Total probability of overturning - individual probabilities for each curve
2.4.1
The total probability of overturning for the route section contributed by all curves
over which trains travel at target enhanced permissible speeds shall be determined by
adding the individual probabilities for each curve for a single transit through the
curves.
Rationale
G 2.4.2
Evaluating the probability of overturning for the whole route section, assuming the
target enhanced permissible speeds, requires determining the probability to overturn
for each curve for each train transit.
G 2.4.3
The total probability is required so that the acceptability of overturning risk can be
assessed.
Guidance
G 2.4.4
In the first instance the probability of overturning has to be obtained for the whole
route section assuming the target enhanced permissible speeds. This requires
determining the probability to overturn in each curve, taking into account the
different infrastructure characteristics, the train speed and the local wind conditions.
If mitigation measures are required to ensure that the route section overturning
probability is acceptable, then these can be considered successively at the individual
curves having the largest overturning probabilities.
G 2.4.5
A method for determining the overturning probability on curves is given in Part 3.
2.5
Assess the acceptability of overturning risk for the route section
2.5.1
For each route section, the acceptability of the risk of using enhanced permissible
speeds shall be assessed following the principles of the CSM RA, to determine whether
further risk controls for the enhanced permissible speed are required to ensure safety.

Rationale
G 2.5.2
The CSM RA sets out a risk management process that must be followed for
'significant' technical, organisational, and operational changes. It defines three risk
acceptance principles; codes of practice; reference systems and explicit risk
estimation. The first two of these represent current good practice, which is an
established way of ensuring safety so far as is reasonably practicable (SFAIRP). Whilst
it is not necessary to follow CSM RA for changes that are not significant, it still
represents good practice.
Guidance
G 2.5.3
This document sets out two methods available to support a decision associated with
the acceptability of risk from the hazard of a train overturning - a comparative
method (analagous to CSM RA comparison with a reference system) and an absolute
method (CSM RA explicit risk estimation).
G 2.5.4
The comparative method relies on comparing the calculated overturning risk with
that of a reference vehicle travelling through the curves over which trains are to travel
at enhanced permissible speeds. This is the method most commonly used, because it
is generally easier and reduces the impact of the modelling uncertainties that affect
both vehicles in a similar way.
G 2.5.5
It becomes difficult to apply a comparative method when the vehicle for which the
enhanced permissible speed is being determined has a lower resistance to overturning
than the reference vehicle or if no suitable reference vehicle can be identified. In such
cases, it is necessary to assess the acceptability of absolute values of the overturning
risk.
G 2.5.6
The two methods of determining the acceptable risk of overturning are considered in
greater detail in Appendix C.
G 2.5.7
There is further guidance in the industry risk management framework document,
Taking Safe Decisions, authored by RSSB, and which references other relevant RSSB
Guidance Notes with more details.
2.6
Reduce route section risk if necessary
2.6.1
If the total risk of overturning for the route section is found to be unacceptable, the
risk of overturning for the route section shall be reduced to an acceptable level. Risk
reduction measures may themselves introduce new hazards, which shall be assessed
as part of the acceptability analysis.
Rationale
G 2.6.2
If the risk of overturning in the route section is unacceptable, measures must be
undertaken to reduce the risk.
Guidance
G 2.6.3
If the overturning risk for the route section is acceptable, no risk reduction measures
are required and the enhanced permissible speeds on all the curves may be adopted.

G 2.6.4
Risk reduction can be achieved in a number of ways by:
a) Reducing the target enhanced permissible speeds on selected curves.
b) Providing wind barriers or fences to limit the wind speed on selected curves.
c) Introducing a wind alert system or strategy to allow the speed of trains to be
reduced when the wind speed exceeds, or is predicted to exceed, a chosen
maximum wind speed on selected curves.
d) Modify the critical vehicle, for example, by increasing its weight.
G 2.6.5
The first three risk reduction measures set out above are listed in order of preference
and practicality, based on the robustness with which they will achieve the required
risk reduction.
G 2.6.6
The risk reduction measures are applied preferentially to those curves in the route
section having the highest calculated probability of overturning for a single train
transit through the curve. This will permit the target enhanced permissible speeds to
be achieved on the greatest number of curves on the route section.
2.7
Assess acceptability of the reduced route section risk
2.7.1
Once risk reduction measures have been selected, the total probability of overturning
for the route section contributed by all curves over which trains travel at enhanced
permissible speeds shall be recalculated.
2.7.2
The calculated risk of overturning for a single train transit through any specific curve
shall be limited to a fraction, not exceeding 25%, of the total risk of overturning for
the route section.
2.7.3
If the total risk from overturning for the route section remains greater than
acceptable levels, further risk reduction measures shall be applied and the total risk of
overturning for the route section shall be recalculated.
2.7.4
This process shall be repeated until the total risk of overturning for the route section is
less than or equal to the acceptable levels.
Rationale
G 2.7.5
It is necessary to check that the risk reduction measures have reduced the total risk of
overturning for the route section to confirm that it is less than or equal to the
acceptable risk levels and that no single curve’s risk dominates the risk profile.
Guidance
G 2.7.6
A limitation of the probability of overturning on specific curves to being a fraction of
the total probability of overturning on the route section was introduced at Network
Rail’s request for advice on permitted limits for future changes to track radius on
WCML due to maintenance or other activities.

Appendices
Appendix A
General Background of Enhanced Permissible Speeds
for Tilting Trains
A.1
Cant deficiency margin against overturning
A.1.1
GMRT2141 requires that rail vehicles are able to run around smooth curves at
constant speed, without overturning (roll-over), at not less than a specified cant
deficiency.
A.1.2
GCRT5021 sets a limit for design cant deficiency of 150 mm (approximately 6°) for
non-freight vehicles travelling at permissible speeds.
A.1.3
There is therefore a minimum margin of cant deficiency between a train travelling at
permissible speed and its roll-over resistance. Experience shows that this margin is
sufficient to ensure that the risk of overturning is acceptable.
A.1.4
Tilting trains have a system that tilts the train body to reduce the lateral acceleration
experienced by passengers when operating around curves, allowing the trains to run
at higher cant deficiencies (and therefore higher speeds) through curves than non-
tilting trains.
A.1.5
A tilting train may have a maximum design service cant deficiency of up to 12°. In
this case there is a reduced margin of cant deficiency between a train travelling at
enhanced permissible speed and its roll-over resistance. This is a significant reduction
in the margin against overturning compared to that offered at permissible speeds.
Particular consideration therefore needs to be given to the adequacy of the margin
and the additional controls against overturning required when trains travel at
enhanced permissible speeds.
A.1.6
Figures 4 and 5 show the margin against overturning at permissible and enhanced
permissible speeds and the factors increasing cant deficiency. The figures show the
probabilistic combination of the effect of the wind on the train and overspeeding of
the train to arrive at a residual probability of overturning.
A.1.7
Figure 5 also shows the effect of additional controls on track irregularities (when
applied) and the use of a speed supervision system. Additional measures to reduce
the probability of overturning can be applied, if necessary. These include increasing
the train’s resistance to roll-over and measures to reduce the allowance needed for
the effect of wind (for example, by installing wind fences or by introducing systems to
reduce the speed of trains when the wind exceeds a given limit).

A.2.1

A.3
Probabilistic combination of factors increasing cant deficiency
A.3.1
The enhanced permissible speed for each type of tilting train on each curve is
calculated in a way that takes the factors set out in A.2 into account. However, if all
the effects which increase cant deficiency are considered to act simultaneously, a
very conservative enhanced permissible speed would be produced. It is therefore
necessary to consider the probabilities of those factors which are subject to random
variation.
A.3.2
The interaction can be modelled by:
a) Assuming probability distributions for each variable.
b) Determining which combinations of the variables give rise to overturning.
c) Determining the probabilities with which these combinations will occur, and
summing them to form a total probability of overturning.
A.3.3
If specific controls on track irregularity are put in place, the effect of track
irregularities on cant deficiency is small. The conservative assumption has therefore
been made that the maximum possible loss of cant and reduction in curve radius are
present on every curve (that is, the maximum expected cant deficiency increase from
track irregularities occurs with a probability of 1.0).
A.3.4
A fixed safety margin is assumed to account for errors in estimating the train’s roll-
over resistance and the effect of the factors which increase cant deficiency, unless an
approach based on addressing bias and uncertainty is used (see K.1).
A.3.5
These assumptions leave the effect of the wind on the train and overspeeding of the
train as factors requiring probabilistic combination to determine the probability of
the equivalent cant deficiency exceeding the train’s roll-over resistance, causing the
train to overturn.
A.4

assumed to be adequately controlled by meeting the requirements of GMRT2141 and
GMRT2142. Therefore, for the purposes of calculating enhanced permissible speed,
the risk from overturning only needs to be considered for those curves over which
trains travel at enhanced permissible speeds.
A.4.2
This assumption defines the scope of application of the calculation methodology set
out in this document. The stages of this calculation are set out in Part 2.
A.4.3
The method of calculating enhanced permissible speeds is set out in greater detail in

C.1
Methods of determining an acceptable risk of overturning
C.1.1
There are two methods available to support the decision on the acceptability of risk
from overturning. These follow the principles of the CSM RA criteria.
C.1.2
The first is a method which relies on comparing the calculated overturning risk with
that of a reference vehicle.
C.1.3
When it is difficult to apply the comparative method, it is necessary to use an explicit
risk estimation of overturning and determine if this is acceptable.
C.2

C.2.1
The acceptable risk of overturning is determined by comparison with existing
reference vehicles, usually travelling at permissible speeds through the curves being
considered for an enhanced permissible speed on a route section. This is analogous to
using the CSM RA reference system risk acceptance principle.
C.2.2
Reference vehicles will be those travelling at permissible speeds until more routes are
operated by tilting trains, at which time the latter could be used as reference vehicles.
C.2.3
GEGN8573 gives guidance on choosing suitable comparator vehicles for gauging. The
same general principles apply to the choice of comparator vehicles for the
assessment of overturning probability.
C.2.4
In particular, the reference vehicles must have a history of running without
overturning that is long enough to allow the assumption to be made that the risk of
overturning associated with these vehicles is acceptable. The Mark 3 coach, for
example, which has had a long period of widespread safe service in GB, may be
considered as a reference vehicle.
C.2.5
The leading vehicle of the tilting Class 390 train, which travels at enhanced
permissible speeds on the WCML, may be considered as a comparator vehicle.
C.2.6

C.4
Absolute method
C.4.1
The risk acceptance principle applied to assess the acceptability of overturning risk is
to ensure safety SFAIRP. Reducing risk to SFAIRP means demonstrating that, for all
potential risk control measures, that the costs (time, effort and financial) are grossly
disproportionate to the safety benefit of implementing the risk control measure. A
quantified cost-benefit analysis is often used, in which the various elements including
costs, performance and safety are expressed in the same financial units.
C.4.2
The cost-benefit analysis balances the costs of the change with the safety benefits
gained from any risk reduction. The latter is a collective risk, that is, including all
people exposed to the risk (not the risk to an individual), and is usually expressed in
units of fatalities and weighted injuries per year(FWIs/yr). The safety benefits are
derived by expressing the risk reduction in monetary terms by using the value of
preventing a fatality (VPF) added to the costs of avoiding accidents due to the
proposed change.
C.4.3
The decision whether to apply risk reduction measures on enhanced permissible
speed curves will be based on whether the costs are grossly disproportionate to the
safety benefit.
C.4.4
Further details of how to apply explicit risk estimation are given in the industry’s risk
management framework document, Taking Safe Decisions, authored by RSSB, and
using cost benefit analysis for assessment of safety decisions in RSSB’s Guidance on
the use of cost-benefit analysis when determining whether a measure is necessary to
ensure safety SFAIRP.

D.1
Application of enhanced permissible speeds on a curve
D.1.1
When setting enhanced permissible speeds for different trains on a curve there are
specific requirements to be applied depending on the characteristics of the trains
travelling on the curve, the characteristics of the curve itself and the mode of
operation on the tracks. These requirements are set out in D.2.
D.1.2
In addition, Railway Group Standards have certain requirements regarding the
signage of enhanced permissible speeds, which are set out in D.5.1.
D.1.3
GCRT5021 requires that there is a procedure in place for calculating enhanced
permissible speeds. Other aspects that are required to meet the requirements of
GCRT5021 are set out in Appendix E.
D.2
Setting enhanced permissible speeds on curves for different trains
D.2.1
GCRT5021 requires that the enhanced permissible speed is calculated for each type
of train on each curve. The speed on each track of a double or multiple line is
considered separately. On bi-directional tracks, the speed in each direction is
considered separately.
D.2.2
The enhanced permissible speed is calculated for each type of train because each
type of train will differ in its characteristics (see D.3). The enhanced permissible speed
is calculated for each curve, because each curve will differ in its characteristics, even if
they have the same curvature and applied cant (see D.4).
D.2.3
On bi-directional tracks, the speed in each direction is considered separately because
the effect of the wind on a train is dependent on the combined direction of the train
and the direction of the wind.
D.3
Train characteristics to be considered
D.3.1
The different train characteristics to be considered are:
a) Maximum design service cant deficiency
• Different types of tilting trains are designed to operate up to particular maximum
cant deficiencies. The maximum cant deficiency is set to limit the lateral
acceleration experienced by passengers when travelling around curves. It will
depend on the tilt capability and suspension characteristics of the train.
• Typical maximum design service cant deficiencies for tilting trains are 9°
(approximately 225 mm) and 12° (approximately 300 mm).
• GMRT2141 requires that the maximum design service cant deficiency of all
vehicles required to operate at cant deficiencies in excess of 6° is determined.
• GCRT5021 sets out limiting design values for cant deficiency at enhanced
permissible speeds. GCRT5021 also sets out the requirements for using exceptional
limiting design values rather than normal limiting design values.

b) Dynamic roll-over resistance
• GMRT2141 sets out design requirements for traction and rolling stock to ensure

induced by overspeeding.
• For all vehicles that operate at a cant deficiency greater than 6°, particular

• In practice, it is likely that tilting trains will have a roll-over resistance that is

• GMRT2141 requires that the resistance to roll-over of all vehicles required to
operate at cant deficiencies in excess of 6° is determined. The roll-over resistance is
expressed as an equivalent cant deficiency, measured in degrees.
• The roll-over resistance is used in the calculation of the probability of overturning.
Note: The calculation is made for the critical vehicle of the train, as defined. When it is not
certain which vehicle is critical, the roll-over resistance of the leading and second vehicles of
the train, together with any other vehicles which could have a lower roll-over resistance or a
higher aerodynamic cant deficiency coefficient (see also Appendix B) than these vehicles, is
required.
c) Aerodynamic characteristics
• GMRT2142 requires a number of vehicle properties to be determined for vehicles
that are required to operate at cant deficiencies in excess of a nominal 6°. These
include the speed, the mass and its distribution, and the aerodynamic
characteristics.
• GMRC2542 sets out details of recommended methods using wind tunnel testing

• The vehicle characteristics are those that determine the behaviour of vehicles

• GERC8517 supports RIS-8012-CCS. It sets out recommendations for the speed

system limits overspeeding by the automatic application of the train’s brakes. The
recommendations of GERC8517 include guidance on determining intervention
criteria for the speed supervision.
• A speed supervision system needs to allow a driver, when driving correctly, to
achieve the enhanced permissible speed as indicated on the speedometer display
seen from a normal driving position. Therefore, the speed supervision system
intervenes at a speed above the prevailing enhanced permissible speed. The
intervention level is set at a level that avoids unwarranted intervention, taking into
account:
a) The accuracy of the speed measuring system.
b) The type of speedometer display and how it is read.

D.4.3
Other factors such as vertical alignment (top), twist and gauge are not significant
when considering the probability of overturning, although they are important causes

D.4.4
In order to cause overturning, irregularities in cant and curve radius must extend far
enough to influence the entire vehicle. To do this, they must have wavelengths of the
order of 100 m. This is in contrast to flange climbing derailment, where the
irregularities are measured over distances of metres rather than tens of metres.
D.4.5
The standard speed equation shows that the effect of loss of cant and reduction in
curve radius can be expressed in terms of an equivalent cant deficiency increase.
D.4.6
The following data is needed to determine the proposed enhanced permissible speed
and is to be used in the calculation of the probability of overturning, taking the
effectiveness of maintenance systems into account:
a) The value for reduction in radius, or
b) The bias and uncertainty in the values of radius.
c) The value of loss of cant, or
d) The bias and uncertainty in the values of cant.
D.4.7
Unless special controls are put in place, the value taken for the reduced radius is no
larger than:
a) 80% of the design radius for curves with radii less than 3000 m.
b) 70% of the design radius for curves with radii greater than or equal to 3000 m.
D.4.8
These values are based on measurements of curvature on typical main line routes by
track geometry recording vehicles, filtered to remove short wavelengths.
D.4.9
Measurements of cant on typical main line routes made using track geometry
recording vehicles, and filtered to remove short wavelengths, suggest that in most
cases the expected variation in cant from the design value will be less than 10 mm
(0.38°).
D.4.10
Local wind conditions (see Appendix F).
D.4.11
The presence of a side wind increases the aerodynamic loading of a vehicle on a
curve and reduces the nett moment resisting overturning. The wind speed is
influenced by a number of factors including the location of the curve in the country,
track altitude, wind direction, local terrain ground roughness, height of the curve
above surrounding ground, curve infrastructure (for example, embankment, viaduct or
cutting) and local sheltering effects. These can be different either side of the curve
under consideration.
D.4.12
The speed on each track of a double or multiple line is considered separately for the
reasons given above.

D.5.2
Calculation of enhanced permissible speed to be displayed
D.5.2.1

may have differing enhanced permissible speeds. The principal characteristic that will
cause trains to have differing enhanced permissible speeds is likely to be their
differing maximum design service cant deficiencies.
D.5.2.2
The maximum design service cant deficiency is dependent on the tilt capability of the
train. If this cant deficiency is exceeded, the consequence will only be increased
passenger discomfort, provided the train speed is such that it can still safely traverse
the curve without an unacceptable risk of overturning.
D.5.2.3

may be determined as follows:
a) Calculate a ‘display speed’ for each train using the methodology set out in Part 3,
but using the appropriate limiting design value for cant deficiency set out in
GCRT5021, rather than the maximum design service cant deficiency, when
selecting the initial target speed. If the limiting design value for cant deficiency
set out in GCRT5021 is the required service cant deficiency, the value of the
display speed is equal to the enhanced permissible speed.
b) The speed to be displayed is the lowest display speed of all the tilting trains using
the section of route under consideration.
Note: If trains have significantly differing resistances to overturning or aerodynamic
characteristics, the enhanced permissible speed to be displayed would be determined by
the train with the poorest characteristics. This may penalise the performance of the trains
with better characteristics.

Appendix F
Determination of Local Wind Speeds
Note: The determination of the local wind speeds for a curve is based on the use of
reference mean wind speeds, derived from long-term wind measurements and statistics, in
a map form. The local reference wind speed is factored for local effects which adjust the
wind speed to the form required for the calculation of overturning probability on the curve.
Note: The wind speed model used here has been developed using the Engineering Sciences
Data Unit (ESDU) Engineering Data Items 82026, 83045 and 91043.
F.1
Overview of local wind speeds
Reference wind speed
F.1.1
The extreme mean hourly wind speed for the particular curve location under
consideration is determined from data based on an analysis of historical
meteorological data. This value is the ‘reference wind speed’, VREF, for the curve site.
F.2 gives further information on such data for the UK.
Local wind speed
F.1.2
The reference wind speed is then adjusted for altitude, wind direction, ground
roughness, topography, gust duration and shelter effects to arrive at the speed of a
three second duration gust with a 50 year return period.
F.1.3
This value is the local wind speed, VWL, which is used in the calculation of the
probability of overturning.
F.1.4
The three second gust duration is chosen by consideration of the time needed for
train aerodynamic forces to completely develop. The method of calculating enhanced
permissible speeds set out in this document assumes that peak overturning effects
can be determined from the three second gust without requiring any allowances for
dynamic and aerodynamic admittance effects.
F.1.5
F.3 provides a method that may be used to determine local three second gust wind
speeds.
Probability of high wind speeds coming from particular directions
F.1.6
The effect of the wind on a train depends on a directional factor as high winds are
most likely to blow from the south-west.
F.1.7
The probability of the wind approaching a curve depends on the orientation of the
track and whether the train is travelling clockwise or anticlockwise around the curve
(see F.3.2).

Appendix K
Analysis of Bias and Uncertainty
K.1
Analysis of bias and uncertainty
Scope
K.1.1
This appendix sets out a method to analyse bias and uncertainty in the nominal
calculated probability of overturning. It also sets out how to derive other required
measures of probability.
K.1.2
The use of bias and uncertainty analysis was a significant technical innovation in the
approach to risk in the Class 390 studies. It is important to include it here in detail, as
such techniques have not been used to date in the railway industry. It is also a critical
component of the alternative method outlined in Appendix L, which removes the
requirement for the use of a safety margin in GCRT5021. As presented, the detail
permits users to become familiar with the method and to make check calculations
using the tabulated data and equations.
Analysis of bias and uncertainty definitions
Nominal probability
Overturning probability per route section transit calculated using
the method set out in this document. Calculating the probability of
overturning for a single train transit through a curve and on a route
section.
Propagated bias
Bias in the nominal probability taking account of estimates of bias
errors in the inputs to the probability calculation.
Propagated uncertainty
The ‘worst error which could reasonably be expected’, taking
account of estimates of uncertainties in the inputs to the
probability calculation.
Best estimate probability
Nominal probability adjusted for propagated bias.
Expected probability
Most likely value of overturning probability, given that the inputs
are uncertain and biased.
Upper bound probability
Nominal probability adjusted for propagated bias and the worst
propagated error which could reasonably be expected due to
uncertainties in inputs.

K.1.1
Overview of the bias and uncertainty analysis
K.1.1.1
This bias and uncertainty analysis was used in the analysis of the calculated
overturning probabilities undertaken during the West Coast Route Modernisation
Project. Other methods may be used, but this method has been applied, critically
examined and accepted as an appropriate methodology for the assessment of
overturning probabilities.
K.1.1.2
Figure 9 is a schematic of the bias and uncertainty analysis process explained in
detail in this appendix.
K.1.1.3
The analysis obtains an estimate of the bias and uncertainty in the result, r. The
objective is to express the result as rtrue = rnominal - b ± u where b is the ‘best estimate’
of the bias, and u is the estimated ‘worst error which could reasonably be expected’.
The bias is taken as the estimate of the error having a 50% expectation of being
exceeded, and the ‘worst error which could reasonably be expected’ is taken as
corresponding approximately to the ‘95% confidence limit’.
K.1.1.4
The result is expressed as the logarithm (to base 10) of the overturning probability for
a given high-risk curve.
K.1.1.5
The uncertainty analysis is conducted using the input data for at least eight of the
highest-risk curves on the route. The uncertainties, bias and sensitivities for these
curves are then assumed to be representative of all curves making a significant
contribution to the total route section probability.

K.1.1.7
Overturning probability calculations for the route are made in accordance with Part 3.
The results are to be reviewed to identify at least eight relatively high-risk curves on
the route.

K.1.1.9
Assessments of the magnitudes of these biases and uncertainties are required for the
uncertainty analysis. Generally, these assessments will have to be based on the

K.1.1.10
The bias errors are assessed as ‘best estimates’ of the likely bias (with a 50%

underestimate of the likely true value. The uncertainties are assessed as ‘the worst
error which could reasonably be expected’ and are treated as ‘95% confidence
limits’. These assessments are obtained in general terms; that is, either as percentage
bias on the actual value, as an absolute magnitude, or as a multiplier or factor on the
actual value.

The assessed biases are applied to the input data and intermediate data derived from
the models. This will give, for each curve, an estimated bias and estimated
uncertainty for each input variable or intermediate model data.

K.1.2.5
The expected probability differs from the best estimate of the probability. This is
because the error distribution for log10 (overturning probability) is approximately
symmetric, with the consequence that the error distribution for overturning
probability is asymmetric. It was demonstrated in the West Coast Route
Modernisation Project for Class 390 operations that, for an overall uncertainty of two
orders of magnitude, the effect of this asymmetry is that the expected overturning
probability can be taken as 25 times the best estimate probability. In general, this
factor will be somewhat less than the overall uncertainty and, for cases where this
uncertainty differs significantly from two orders of magnitude, it has to be
recalculated.

Appendix L
Modified Method Applied to the Introduction of the
Class 390 Tilting Train on the West Coast Main Line
L.1
Introduction
L.1.1
The methodology for the determination of enhanced permissible speeds contained in
an early draft version of GCRC5521 was reviewed by WS Atkins who proposed a series
of revisions in a report in February 2001. These were incorporated into issue one of
the standard published in June 2001, which is the subject of this revision.
L.1.2
The West Coast Route Modernisation Project agreed a criterion for the acceptable
probability of overturning, based on the risk of fatality to an individual, to be applied
to operations of the Class 390 . The method of determining overturning probabilities
according to GCRC5521 was then applied to the Class 390 and to a Mark 3 coach; the
latter was to be considered as a potential reference vehicle for the Class 390 in the
comparative method as described in C.2.
Note: The acceptability of overturning risk can no longer be based on the concept of risk to
an individual according to the CSM RA.
L.1.3
It was found that the calculated overturning probabilities for the Class 390 on two
WCML route sections were approximately 104 times greater than the absolute
acceptability criterion. Furthermore, the calculated probabilities for the Class 390
were approximately 10 times greater than the estimated probabilities for the Mark 3
coach (which in turn were also greater than the absolute acceptability criterion).
These results led to use of the comparative method being dropped, and a series of
revisions being made to the method to remove conservatism and improve accuracy.
Refinements made to the method of GCRC5521 issue one
L.1.4
The following are some of the key refinements made to the method as the results of
studies made in the West Coast Route Modernisation Project; others have already
been included in this document.
L.1.5
The whole of this alternative methodology was accepted as ‘appropriate to meet the
mandatory requirements for calculation of enhanced permissible speeds set out in
GCRT5021, and the requirement set out in GMRT2141 for a risk assessment to
demonstrate that the probability of roll-over is broadly acceptable when trains are
operating under gale conditions’. If followed, it can therefore be considered as an
alternative to the methodology set out in other sections of this document, as long as
the risk acceptability is assessed according to the principles of the CSM RA as set out
in Appendix C:
a) A rigorous method was used to determine the aerodynamic rolling moment
coefficients for the Class 390 vehicles. This included atmospheric boundary layer
wind tunnel tests to determine the coefficients, and validation against full-scale
tests.
b) The rolling moment coefficients from the wind tunnel tests were determined as
functions of the relative wind angle instead of using a linear approximation.
c) Rolling moment coefficients were determined for trains on embankments, which
relate directly to the undisturbed wind upstream of the embankment, rather than
to an estimated wind over the embankment.

d) The results of the atmospheric boundary layer wind tunnel tests were also

e) A modified method was used to determine the wind climate along the route. This

of the Lake District), which were not accounted for in the method used in
GCRC5521 issue one.
f) The approximate wind probability model given in the code of practice was
modified. The standard hourly maximum gust type of analysis would
underestimate the probability of overturning, as it excludes the contribution to
probability from gusts that exceed the overturning wind speed, but are less than
the hourly maximum. The probability contribution from such gusts can be
substantial for trains vulnerable to winds which are significantly below the 50 year
value.
g) The probability calculations included the assessment of only curves with cant
deficiencies greater than 150 mm, defined to be enhanced permissible speed
curves.
h) The safety margin equivalent to no less than 50 mm of cant deficiency, allowance
for loss of cant and reduction in radius were not applied. Instead, all the input
variables and models were assessed for bias and uncertainty and the propagated
bias and uncertainty in the nominal probability of overturning was determined as
set out in K.1.

L.2
Modified probability assessment for the Class 390
Assessment of Class 390 aerodynamics characteristics
L.2.1
The aerodynamic rolling moment coefficients for the Class 390 were determined
according to GMRC2542. This involved undertaking atmospheric boundary layer wind
tunnel tests on models of the leading and trailing Class 390 vehicles on level ground
and on an embankment.
L.2.2
The wind tunnel tests provided discrete values of the aerodynamic rolling moment
coefficients for the tested combinations of embankment height, cant, tilt and yaw
angles. These data were analysed to provide functions of yaw angle for the
coefficients in order to determine values for combinations of these parameters that
did not correspond to the tested combinations, but did occur on the curves analysed
in the probability analysis of overturning.
L.2.3
The measurement of aerodynamic coefficients on an embankment means that it is
unnecessary to correct local wind speeds for the speed-up effect on embankments
and escarpments using the factor KL.

Definitions
Bias
A systematic difference between measurements of a variable and
its true value. The estimate of bias in a variable is taken here to be
the value having a 50% expectation of being exceeded.
Critical vehicle
The critical vehicle of a tilting train is the vehicle having the lowest
overturning wind speed. This will be either the vehicle having the
lowest roll-over resistance, or the vehicle having the smallest
aerodynamic cant deficiency coefficient calculated in accordance
with the aerodynamic cant deficiency coefficient.
Enhanced Permissible
Speed
The speed permitted over a section of line that applies to a specific
type of train operating at cant deficiencies in excess of those
permitted at the permissible speed. There may be more than one
enhanced permissible speed applicable to a given section of line.
For the purposes of RIS-7704-INS, this applies to all those curves
whose cant deficiency exceeds 150 mm.
Infrastructure Manager
(IM)
Any ‘body’ or undertaking that is responsible in particular for
establishing and maintaining railway infrastructure, or part thereof,
as defined in article 3 of Directive 91/440/EEC, which may also
include the management of infrastructure control and safety
systems. The functions of the infrastructure manager on a network
or part of a network may be allocated to different bodies or
undertakings. Source: Article 3 (b) of Directive 2004/49/EC.
Maximum Design Service
Cant Deficiency
The maximum cant deficiency at which a train is designed to
travel.
Overspeed
The amount by which the actual speed of a train could exceed the
enhanced permissible speed for any reason.
Overturning
‘Overturning’ has the same meaning as ‘roll-over’.
Permissible Speed
The authorised maximum speed over a section of line, either for all
trains or (where differential or enhanced permissible speeds are
applied) for specific types of trains, as set out in the Sectional
Appendix.
Railway Undertaking (RU)
Any private or public undertaking the principal business of which is
to provide rail transport services for goods and/or passengers, with
a requirement that the undertaking must ensure traction; this also
includes undertakings which provide traction only. Source: Article 3
(a) of Directive 2004/49/EC.
Risk
The combination of the likelihood of occurrence of harm and the
severity of that harm (specifically defined in CSM RA regulation as:
the frequency of occurrence of accidents and incidents resulting in
harm (caused by a hazard) and the degree of severity of that
harm).

Roll-Over
The situation reached when all the wheels on one side of a vehicle
reach 100% unloading and the whole weight of the vehicle is
supported by the wheels on the other running rail.
Route Section
A section of a route over which a regular long-distance commuter is
assumed to travel (in one direction) for the purposes of calculating
the risk to the commuter from overturning of the train in which
they are travelling. The route section extends between stations or
junctions at which the commuter joins and leaves the route. Route
sections for long-distance commuters are in the order of 100 miles
long.
Switches and Crossings
(S&C)
No definition.
Tilting Train
A train having a system which tilts the train body to reduce the
lateral acceleration experienced by passengers when operating
around curves, allowing the train to run at higher speeds through
curves than non-tilting trains.
Turnout Route and Through
Route in S&C
A turnout route in S&C is one having a set in the stock rail at the
switch toes. A through route has no set in the stock rail at the
switch toes. Note that an equal split turnout has a set in both stock
rails and that therefore both routes are turnout routes.
Uncertainty
The range of values of a variable likely to contain the true value.
The estimate of uncertainty in a variable is taken here to be the
worst error which could reasonably be expected and corresponds
approximately to the 95% confidence level for normally
distributed quantities.
Yaw angle
The angle of the resultant wind speed relative to the train. This
wind speed is caused by the combination of the forward movement
of the train and naturally occurring wind.
