Part 1
Purpose and introduction
1.1
Introduction
1.1.1
In the Great Britain (GB) mainline railway, car stop markers provide a fixed reference
point to drivers when stopping a train at a station platform where the train is
scheduled to stop. Also, car stop markers provide an indication for the train driver to
stop their train in accordance with the length and type of train they are operating.
1.1.2
RIS-3782-TOM builds upon the duty of co-operation that ROGS places on transport
operators. It sets out a framework to improve the consistency on the design,
positioning and use of car stop markers across the GB mainline railway. The overall
framework – known as the five-step approach – is described in section 2.1, subsequent
sections of the standard explain in more detail each part of it.
1.1.3
Sign AK104 supplements this standard. It contains the specification of the design for
car stop markers.
1.1.4
RIS-3782-TOM and sign AK104 incorporate the findings of the research RSSB carried
out during 2019 with drivers and operations managers. Therefore, both documents
contain the industry-agreed principles to determine the optimum train stopping
positions and car stop markers provision.
1.1.5
This standard can be adopted and implemented when a change to car stop markers
(or a change that may affect a car stop maker) is being proposed.
Purpose
1.1.6
The purpose of RIS-3782-TOM is to provide transport operators in the GB mainline
railway with a common framework that improves the consistency on the design,
positioning and use of car stop makers and that:
a) reduces the amount of route knowledge that drivers have to retain due to a
reduction in the number of stopping positions on platforms;
b) contributes to increased levels of safety due to improved usability of the car stop
marker design; and
c) reduces the costs and effort associated with designing, trialling, installing car stop
markers.
1.2

1.2.1

Introduction to a common approach to the provision of car stop markers

concludes with a review of their impact on the route.

2.2
Collaboration
2.2.1
Before implementing a change to a car stop marker, the proposer of the change shall:
a) Consult with the affected parties; and
b) Undertake a collaborative assessment of the provision and position of car stop
markers at each affected platform and on the route.
Rationale
G 2.2.2
ROGS places a duty on transport operators to cooperate to make sure the railway is
safe. Changes to train stopping positions and associated lineside operational signs
(such as car stop markers) may impact on other transport operators who share the
infrastructure.
G 2.2.3
A collaborative approach to the assessment of acceptable train stopping positions
and the design and position of car stop markers involves the participation of affected
transport operators. The assessment process encourages all transport operators to
carry out their assessments in a way that will allow them to compare and consolidate
their findings. Undertaking a collaborative assessment aids with the identification of
hazards and control of shared risks.
Guidance on changes to car stop markers
G 2.2.4
Changes to car stop markers can include:
a) The alteration of car stop markers’ locations;
b) The installation of new car stop markers;
c) The withdrawal of existing car stop markers; and
d) Modifying what is displayed on existing car stop markers.
G 2.2.5
Examples of changes that may affect the position of a car stop marker:
a) Introduction of a new train service, or changes to existing trains;
b) Variations to train length;
c) Modification to dispatch arrangements. For example, modes of dispatch, the
position of staff, new equipment;
d) Changes to legislation, policies, procedures or industry good practice;
e) Alterations to lineside signalling, driver only operation (DOO) or driver controlled
operation (DCO) equipment; and
f) Alterations to the station, platform or track layout.
Guidance on identifying affected transport operators
G 2.2.6
To identify transport operators affected by the proposed change, establish:
a) The platforms that are affected. This depends on what is driving the change, such
as the introduction of new trains or a station upgrade;
b) The station facility owner (SFO) at affected platforms; and
c) The train operators who have services stopping at the affected platforms.

Guidance on assessing the provision and position of car stop markers
G 2.2.7
Assessing the provision and position of car stop markers requires experience and
knowledge in:
a) Signal sighting factors, if signals or indications are viewed as part of station duties;
b) The station and platform;
c) The trains stopping at the platform;
d) Train dispatch arrangements;
e) The risks at the platform-train interface (PTI); and
f) Driving.
G 2.2.8
The assessment may benefit from additional experience and knowledge in:
a) Risk assessment and management;
b) Health and safety, for example, from local health and safety representatives;
c) Electrical safety; and
d) Human factors, such as understanding and influencing passenger and staff
behaviours.
G 2.2.9
Transport operators determine collaboratively:
a) What marks the beginning and end of the platform length - that is, the points
from which measurements should start (a ‘0 m’ datum point) and end (maximum
position), for example, the top of the platform ramp; and
b) Activities in the five-step approach that affected transport operators could carry
out collaboratively, to avoid duplication of work or inconsistencies.
G 2.2.10
A consistent system of measurements helps transport operators to determine
commonalities in train stopping positions and locations for car stop makers.
Therefore, longitudinal positions along a platform length are expressed in metres
from an agreed ‘0 m’ datum point at one end of the platform. It is good practice to
place the ‘0 m’ datum point at the start of the platform in the normal direction of
travel. If the platform is bi-directional, then it will be associated with a ‘0 m’ datum
point at each end in order to facilitate assessments for both directions.
2.3
Acceptable train stopping positions
2.3.1
Transport operators shall assess the acceptable train stopping positions on a station
platform for each of their train classes and lengths stopping there and for each
direction on bi-directional platforms.
Rationale
G 2.3.2
The assessment of the acceptable range of train stopping positions for each train
class and length stopping at that platform identifies all the possible suitable positions
that a train could stop at. This allows transport operators to find commonalities to
consolidate into the lowest possible number of stopping positions.
G 2.3.3
At some platforms, it is possible to either continue in the same direction as the train
arrived or to change ends and depart in the opposite direction. It is preferable for
trains of the same formation to stop in the same position regardless of which

2.4
Guidance on minimising train stopping positions and car stop markers
Guidance
G 2.4.1
To minimise the number of train stopping positions and car stop markers positions:
a) First, collate the acceptable ranges of train stopping positions for all train classes
and lengths stopping at the platform;
b) Map those acceptable ranges on a platform diagram;
c) Assess the commonalities between trains to identify the minimum number of
unique stopping positions required to accommodate all trains; and then
d) Identify the minimum number of car stop marker positions required to
accommodate all trains (see G 2.5.13).
G 2.4.2
Appendix C contains a worked example showing how to implement steps a) to d).
2.5
Car stop markers
Specification of car stop markers
2.5.1
Car stop markers shall conform with sign specification AK104.
Rationale
G 2.5.2
Using a distinctive and consistent sign design helps drivers to detect and distinguish it
as a car stop marker. AK104 contains the industry agreed sign design and was derived
from the research underpinning the development of this standard.
Guidance
G 2.5.3
GIRT7033 sets out the requirements for lineside operational signs for the GB mainline
railway, it includes requirements for size, shape, retroreflectivity and colour.
Information displayed on car stop markers
2.5.4
Car stop markers denoting the stopping position for all trains shall indicate so with
the letter “S”.
2.5.5
Car stop markers denoting the stopping position for trains with specific numbers of
cars shall indicate so either with a number, or two numbers separated by a hyphen.

2.5.6
Car stop markers specific to one or more train classes shall incorporate train class
identifiers above the number of cars element of the sign.
Rationale
G 2.5.7
Drivers use the information shown on car stop markers in combination with their
route knowledge to determine the stopping position for the train they are driving.
They derive from the car stop marker that it applies to the train they are driving in
terms of the train class and length.
G 2.5.8
GB mainline drivers think of train length in terms of the number of vehicles that form
a train rather than the absolute length of the train (for example, in metres). Therefore
car stop markers present train length information as the number of cars.
G 2.5.9
In the GB mainline railway, using the letter “S” to denote the stopping position for all
trains is a well-established practice.
G 2.5.10
The number of cars element in a car stop marker is larger than the class identifier and
remains prominent. Placing train class identifiers above it reduces the space required
to provide information and avoids the repetition of common information.

G 2.5.12
Other signs such as AK209 are used to display supplementary information, such as
selective door operation (SDO) values and platform numbers. Such signs may be co-
located with car stop markers.
G 2.5.13
Applying the following principles helps determine the minimum number and
complexity of car stop markers. They aim to reduce the complexity of the information
presented to the minimum required for the driver to interpret the correct stopping
position for their train.
a) If all trains, regardless of train class or length, stop at the same position, use a
single car stop marker.
b) Where all train classes with the same number of cars stop at the same position,
use a single car stop marker.
c) Where trains with a range of numbers of cars (for example, between four and
eight cars) stop at the same position, use a single car stop marker which presents
this range.
d) When different train classes with the same number of cars have different stopping
positions, car stop markers require train class identifiers.
e) When two or more train classes use the same car stop marker, only present the
number of cars once. Use supplementary identifiers above the number of cars
element to present the train classes (see 2.5.6 and Figure 2).

2.6
Whole route assessment of car stop markers
Guidance on Step 5: Whole route assessment of car stop markers
G 2.6.1
After Step 4 of the five-step approach (Figure 1), it is vital to review whether the
variability in the car stop markers drivers encounter along a given route introduces
any significant risks.
G 2.6.2
If changes are being made along a whole route or a significant part of a route, it is
beneficial to consider route-based issues at the start of the five-step approach.
G 2.6.3
There are two key considerations in reviewing the variability in car stop markers:
a) How much does the information displayed on car stop markers that drivers need
to identify vary along the route? For example, if drivers need to identify the 12-car
stopping position, does the way that the number 12 is grouped with other
numbers significantly increase the likelihood of driver error? If drivers see '4' and
'8 - 12' car stop markers at most stations, and then '4 - 8' and '10 - 12' at a later
station, this may increase the risk of stop short incidents due to drivers stopping at
the '4 - 8' car stop marker instead of the '10 - 12' one.
b) How much does the need to look for a train class identifier vary along the route?
For example, if drivers need to identify an 8-car stopping position, how frequently
does the car stop marker's design change between a class-specific '8' car stop
marker and a universal '8' car stop marker? Is there a station where the inclusion
or exclusion of a train class identifier would be different from all other stations
along the route? Does this significantly increase the likelihood of driver error?

G 2.6.4
The objective is to avoid any increase in the route knowledge burden on drivers that
results from changes to car stop markers’ positions compared with existing
arrangements. Consider the benefits that minimising the number of train stopping
positions can have on drivers’ route knowledge and the benefits of reducing the
variability in car stop marker designs across the network.

Installation

Rationale
G 2.7.2
Height and lateral position – Car stop markers provide drivers with a fixed target to
aim for when bringing a train to a stand. Drivers need to be able to see the car stop
marker from the driving cab.
G 2.7.3
Order - The car stop marker with the highest number is mounted at the top (that is, in
the most prominent position) as the longest train has the highest risk of not stopping
with all passenger doors on the platform. It also maximises consistency in how
information is presented and supports drivers in locating the target car stop marker.
G 2.7.4
Platform side - Minimises the likelihood of wrong side door release as it attracts the
driver’s attention to the correct side.
Guidance
G 2.7.5
Good practice is to avoid positioning car stop markers where they obscure
passengers' views of the information displays from a safe position on the platform.

Redundant car stop makers
2.7.6
Transport operators shall remove redundant car stop makers at the same time as

Rationale
G 2.7.7
Removing car stop markers that are no longer in use minimises the confusion created
by sign clutter on drivers as they will encounter fewer distracter objects.

Appendices
Appendix A
Implementation and use of car stop markers
A.1
Records of car stop markers
A.1.1
It is good practice for the infrastructure manager of the station to retain records of
the car stop markers installed at the station, including the design details, layout and
positioning of the signs. This is to ensure that the signs can be appropriately replaced
if temporarily removed during engineering works.
A.2
Briefing staff
A.2.1
It is good practice to share any changes to car stop markers with the industry as part
of the National Electronic Sectional Appendix (NESA). Changes include:
a) Changes in the position of car stop markers;
b) The installation of new car stop markers;
c) The withdrawal of existing car stop markers; and
d) Changes to what is displayed on existing car stop markers.
A.2.2
It is good practice for all affected parties to brief all staff concerned (such as drivers,
guards, train dispatchers) on changes to car stop markers even if the changes are
aimed at staff of other transport operators, so they know when to expect to
encounter them and that no action is required.
A.2.3
When a transport operator is undergoing lots of changes that require significant staff
briefings, it is good practice to judge whether their staff would benefit from a
detailed briefing on car stop markers that do not apply to them, or whether such a
briefing would be burdensome. In the latter case, transport operators may let their
staff know that they will not receive a detailed briefing on changes that do not
directly affect them.
A.3
Investigating the underlying causes of incidents
A.3.1
The most common human error that contributes to stop short incidents is the driver
recalling the train length wrongly, which means they stop at a position for a shorter
train than the one they are driving. This is often due to habits, previous experience or
distraction. For example, if a driver drives an 8-car train for multiple consecutive trips
or shifts, and then switches to driving a 12-car train, they may continue to think they
are driving an 8-car train.
A.3.2
Identifying the underlying causes of and contributors to errors is important for
learning from incidents and implementing appropriate mitigations. It is good practice
to integrate human factors principles into the investigation process (see RIS-3119-
TOM) to understand and mitigate against the errors that lead to stop short incidents.

A.4
Personal protection strategies for drivers
A.4.1
Errors like recalling the train length wrongly may occur long before drivers approach a
station platform, and before car stop markers are visible. This is because drivers
usually recall their train stopping position on the approach to the station.
A.4.2
Drivers may apply non-technical skills when approaching a train stopping position.
They use personal protection strategies, which are simple techniques that can be
integrated into the driving task to help prevent errors. One personal protection
strategy is to display the number of cars that form the train on "formation reminders"
(on cards or on electronic systems) in the driving cab and check them on the
approach to the platform to help target the correct stopping position.
A.4.3
To reduce the likelihood of driver error, transport operators can support drivers with:
a) Formation reminders. By positively framing their use to assist drivers in performing
their role, drivers may be less reluctant to use them and less likely to perceive them
as punishment after an incident. Formation reminders work best when actively
used on the approach to each station to check the number of cars. To aid recovery
from error, transport operators can also integrate into the drivers’ procedures a
step for checking that the number of cars matches the car stop marker they have
stopped the train at before releasing the doors.
b) Other personal protection strategies that complement formation reminders such
as point-and-call and risk-triggered commentary.
c) Additional development support if drivers have stop short incidents when
formation reminders are already in place in the driving cab. Simply having
formation reminders in place may not be sufficient. Post-incident, transport
operators may focus on understanding where a formation reminder was placed in
the driving cab, when it was checked, and what behaviours accompanied the
check. It is recommended that development focuses on the integration of
formation reminders into the driving task, so that drivers use them proactively and
regularly rather than displaying them passively in the cab.
d) Treating positively the use of personal protection strategies to promote their
continued use. For example, transport operators may praise drivers for taking the
time to check the car stop marker in between stopping the train and releasing the
doors. It is important that drivers are supported to perform such checks rather
than to rush the task, especially under operational pressures such as driving to the
timetable.
e) Briefings to drivers on the potential for incorrectly recalling the train length or
stopping position, and encourage the use of personal protection strategies at
crucial times, such as:
• When changing train length, particularly if changing after numerous shifts or
days; and
• When coupling or uncoupling.

The driver has a clear and unobstructed view of the relevant car stop marker when

Factor A7. Lineside infrastructure and level crossings
The train stopping position is compatible with the operation of the signalling system
and the road-rail interface.
Considerations
Avoid positioning the train:
a) with the cab above the AWS magnet;
b) with the wheels directly on the axle counter mechanism;
c) on insulated rail joints defining the end of a track section, as this will
lead to the signalling system showing two track sections as occupied.
In particular, if a train stopping position is on these insulated rail
joints, and beyond the end of the platform, it may cause the starting
signal to be replaced to danger or cause a level crossing in the next
track circuit to initiate a warning cycle;
d) over the TPWS loops, if the train is not fitted with TPWS Mk4 (or
higher level) equipment or the driver will change ends or shut down
the cab and re-open it later; or
e) over crossovers, points, level crossings and their controls. It is
particularly relevant when using SDO and ensures other routes are
not blocked when the train is on the platform (including the road-rail
interface).
If operating SDO with PIBS, the train needs to pass over the beacons before
stopping.
The effectiveness of any TPWS when deciding the stopping position for a short
formation train at a location with a platform starting signal fitted with train stop
sensors. This ensures that following a start against SPAD, the train will not reach a
speed that would breach the safe overrun distance available and allow the train to
enter the point of conflict beyond the signal. Additional overspeed sensors may be
used to mitigate against this risk.

The preferred dispatch position for the person undertaking the train safety check.

Definitions
Affected Party
A railway undertaking or infrastructure manager responsible for
assets on the other side of a physical interface with the potential to
be affected by a proposed change.
Automatic Warning System
(AWS)
A system that gives train drivers in-cab warnings of the approach
to signals, reductions in permissible speed and temporary/
emergency speed restrictions, and to apply the brakes in the event
that a train driver does not acknowledge cautionary warnings given
by the system within the specified time. Source: GERT8075
class identifier
The initial two digits of a locomotive number (for example 60) or
the initial three digits of a multiple unit set identification number
(for example 465).
Driver Controlled Operation
(DCO)
A method of working where the train driver is in control of the
opening and closing of the train's doors.
driver only operation (DOO) A method of working which permits trains to operate without the
need of a guard.
GB mainline railway
Mainline railway has the meaning given to it in the Railways and
Other Guided Transport Systems (Safety) Regulations 2006 (as
amended) and the associated exclusions. GB Mainline Railway is
the mainline railway network excluding any railway in Northern
Ireland, the Channel Tunnel, the dedicated high-speed railway
between London St Pancras International Station and the Channel
Tunnel, and any other exclusions determined by the member state.
good practice
A process or method that has been shown to work well; succeeds in
achieving its objective(s); is widely accepted; and therefore can be
recommended as an approach.
indicator
A lineside signalling asset that is capable of displaying a signalling
indication.
infrastructure manager
(IM)
Any ‘body’ or firm responsible in particular for establishing,
managing and maintaining railway infrastructure, including traffic
management and control-command and signalling; the functions
of the infrastructure manager on a network or part of a network
may be allocated to different bodies or firms. Source: Article 3(2) of
Directive 2012/34/EU.
platform
The structure forming the part of a station that provides access for
passengers to or from a train. Walkways used for staff only are not
considered to be platforms.
proposer
The railway undertaking (RU) or infrastructure manager (IM)
proposing a change that has a potential to affect physical
interfaces between vehicles and infrastructure.
