A FREE BOUNDARY ISOPERIMETRIC PROBLEM IN THE HYPERBOLIC SPACE BETWEEN PARALLEL HOROSPHERES
6 Nov 2008
In this work we investigate the following isoperimetric problem: to find the regions of prescribed volume with minimal boundary area between two parallel horospheres in hyperbolic 3-space (the area of the part of the boundary contained in the horospheres is not included). We reduce the problem to the study of rotationally invariant regions and obtain the possible isoperimetric solutions by studying the behaviour of the profile curves of the rotational surfaces with constant mean curvature in the hyperbolic space.2000 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 53A10, 49Q10.

Introduction

Geometric isoperimetric problems, (upper) estimates for the volume of regions of a given fixed boundary volume, or the dual problems, play an important role in Analysis and Geometry. There are both isoperimetric inequalities , common in Analysis, and actual classification of optimal geometric objects, like the round ball in Euclidean Geometry. We will be interested in the study of a relative free-boundary isoperimetric problem in Hyperbolic 3-space between two parallel horospheres. A survey of recent results about the geometric isoperimetric problems is [13].

For a Riemannian manifold M n , the classical isoperimetric problem assumes the following formulation: to classify, up to congruency by the isometry group of M, the (compact) regions Ω ⊆ M enclosing a fixed volume that have minimal boundary volume. The relevant concepts of volume involved are those of Geometric Measure Theory: regions and their boundaries are n-rectifiable (resp. (n − 1)-rectifiable) subsets of M (cf. [11]).

If M has boundary, the part of ∂Ω included in the interior of M will be called the free boundary of Ω, the other part will be called the fixed boundary. One may specify how the fixed boundary of Ω is included in the computation of the boundary volume functional. In this paper, we will assume that the volume of the fixed boundary of Ω is not considered in the boundary volume functional. We will see in Section 3 that this implies that the angle of contact between the interior boundary of Ω and ∂M is π/2 (when such contact occurs). Such relative problems are related to the geometry of stable drops in capillarity problems (the angle of contact depends, as mentioned, on how one considers the volume of the fixed boundary in the computation of the boundary volume functional). For a discussion about that, we refer to [7].

The motivation for our work is the well-known result of Athanassenas [2] and Vogel [14] which implies that between two parallel planes in the Euclidean space R 3 , a (stable) soap-bubble touching both walls perpendicularly is a straight cylinder perpendicular to the planes (enclosing a tube), and may only exist down to a certain minimal enclosing volume depending on the distance between the planes. Below that value, only half-spheres touching one of the planes or whole spheres not touching either plane occur, the cylinders becoming unstable. A newer proof can be found in [12], where the authors study the analogous problem in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces.

In this paper we study the analogous relative isoperimetric problem between two parallel horospheres in hyperbolic space H 3 (−1). We will use the upper halfspace model R 3 + . The parallel horospheres are then represented by the horizontal Euclidean 2-planes of R 3 + . We will present in this paper a detailed classification of the possible isoperimetric solutions.

The existence of isoperimetric regions in the manifold with boundary (B, g), the slab composed by the two horospheres and the region between them, may be obtained by adapting a result due to Morgan [11] (B/G is a compact space, where G is the subgroup of the isometry group of H 3 (−1) leaving B invariant, so that Morgan's result applies). Regarding the regularity of the free boundary, well-known results about the lower codimension bounds of the singular subset imply that it must be regular, in fact analytic.

In Section 2 we give some basic definitions in the model R 3 + like geodesics, totally geodesic surfaces, umbilical surfaces and rotational surfaces. We also give a more precise formulation for the isoperimetric problem considered using area and volume functionals.

In Section 3 we discuss briefly the rotationally invariance of isoperimetric regions and some of their basic geometric properties, since their (free) boundaries must have constant mean curvature and, when touching the bounding horospheres, the contact angle must be π/2. We will also discuss in more detail the existence of isoperimetric regions and the regularity of the free boundary part.

In Section 4 we investigate the tangency of profile curves for the rotational surfaces with constant mean curvature to determine the possible isoperimetric regions between the two parallel horospheres in R 3 + . We define what we mean by catenoid, equidistant and onduloid type surfaces and prove the following result.

Let c 1 , c 2 be positive real constants, c 1 < c 2 , and F c 1 ,c 2 = {(x, y, z) ∈ R 3 + : c 1 ≤ z ≤ c 2 }. Let V > 0 and C c 1 ,c 2 ,V be the set of Ω ⊂ F c 1 ,c 2 with volume |Ω| = V and boundary volume (area) A(Ω o F c 1 ,c 2 ) < ∞, where we suppose Ω to be connected, compact, 3-rectifiable in F c 1 ,c 2 , having as boundary (between the horospheres) an embedded, orientable, 2-rectifiable surface.
Theorem 1.1. Let A c 1 ,c 2 ,V = inf{A(Ω o F c 1 ,c 2 ) : Ω ∈ C c 1 ,c 2 ,V }. Then (1) there exists Ω ∈ C c 1 ,c 2 ,V such that A(Ω o F c 1 ,c 2 ) = A c 1 ,c 2 ,V .
As already mentioned, the free boundaries are actually analytic surfaces; (2) if Ω has minimal boundary volume, between the horospheres, the free boundary of Ω, is either (a) of catenoid type or umbilical with H = 1, or (b) of equidistant type or umbilical with 0 < H < 1, or (c) of onduloid type or umbilical with H > 1.

The details of the description above are included in Section 4. We observe here that this result shows how the situation in hyperbolic geometry is different from the one in the Euclidean 3-space, where we also have rotationally invariant surfaces of catenoid and of onduloid type, but they cannot appear as boundaries of optimizing tubes (even though, in higher dimensions, hypersurfaces generated by onduloids in Euclidean spaces are known to occur as boundaries of optimal tubes connecting two parallel hyperplanes (cf. [12])). Acknowledgements. The author Márcio Silva would like to thank CAPES and CNPq for support during his PhD studies, which included part of the work presented in this paper.


Preliminaries

In this section we will introduce some basic facts and notations that will appear along the paper.

Let L 4 = (R 4 , g) the 4-dimensional Lorentz space endowed with the metric g(x, y) = x 1 y 1 + x 2 y 2 + x 3 y 3 − x 4 y 4 and the 3-dimensional hyperbolic space
H 3 (−1) := {p = (x 1 , x 2 , x 3 , x 4 ) ∈ L 4 : g(p, p) = −1, x 4 > 0}.
We use the upper halfspace model R 3
+ := {(x, y, z) ∈ R 3 ; z > 0} for H 3 (−1), endowed with the metric <, >= ds 2 = dx 2 + dy 2 + dz 2 z 2 .
Let φ : Σ → R 3 + be an isometric immersion of a compact manifold Σ with boundary ∂Σ = ∅ and Γ be a curve in R 3 + . If φ is a diffeomorphism of ∂Σ onto Γ, we say that Γ is the boundary of φ and if φ has constant mean curvature H, we say that Σ is an H-surface with boundary Γ. We identify Σ with its image by φ and ∂Σ with the curve Γ.

The plane z = 0 is called the infinity boundary of R 3 + and we denote it by ∂ ∞ R 3 + . The geodesics of R 3 + are represented by vertical Euclidean lines and half-circles orthogonal to ∂ ∞ R 3 + , contained in R 3 + . The totally geodesic surfaces have constant mean curvature H = 0 and are represented by vertical Euclidean planes and hemispheres orthogonal to ∂ ∞ R 3 + , contained in R 3 + . The horizontal Euclidean translations and the rotations around a vertical geodesic are isometries of R 3 + . We have two family of isometries associated to one point p 0 ∈ ∂ ∞ R 3 + : the Euclidean homotheties centered in p 0 with factor k > 0, called hyperbolic translations through a geodesic α perpendicular to ∂ ∞ R 3 + in p 0 , and the hyperbolic reflections with respect to a totally geodesic surface P .

When P is a hemisphere orthogonal to ∂ ∞ R 3 + centered in p 0 and radius r > 0, the hyperbolic reflections are Euclidean inversions centered in p 0 that fix P and, when P is a vertical Euclidean plane, they are Euclidean reflections with respect to P .

The umbilical surfaces of R 3 + are described as follows (see for example [8]):

(1) Totally geodesics: These surfaces were already described (H = 0).

(2) Geodesic spheres: represented by Euclidean spheres entirely contained in R 3 + , they have H > 1 (mean curvature vector points to the interior). If ρ is the hyperbolic radius of a geodesic sphere then H = coth ρ.

(3) Horospheres: represented by horizontal Euclidean planes of R 3 + and Euclidean spheres of R 3 + which are tangent to ∂ ∞ R 3 + , they have H = 1 and the mean curvature vector points upward in the case of horizontal planes and to the interior in the case of spheres. (4) Equidistant surfaces: represented by the intersection of R 3 + with the planes of R 3 that are neither parallel nor perpendicular to the plane z = 0 and by (pieces of) Euclidean spheres that are not entirely contained in R 3 + and are neither parallel tangent nor perpendicular to the plane z = 0. They have 0 < H < 1 and the mean curvature vector points to the totally geodesic surface they are equidistant to.

In our study, the (spherical) rotational surfaces of R 3 + play an important role since the solutions of the isoperimetric problem must be rotationally invariant regions. They are defined as surfaces invariant by a subgroup of isometries whose principal orbits are (Euclidean) circles.

Let Π 1 and Π 2 be horospheres represented by distinct parallel horizontal Euclidean planes, Π = Π 1 ∪ Π 2 , F = F(Π 1 , Π 2 ) the closed slab between them and φ : Σ → F an immersion of a compact, connected, embedded and orientable C 2 -surface with boundary Γ = ∂Σ and such that φ(Γ) ⊂ Π (we will see later that the image by φ of the interior of the surface Σ will not be allowed to touch Π if Σ is the boundary of an optimal domain in our variational problem, but this is not part of the general situation at this point).

We fix, now, the notation for some well-known geometric invariants related to isometric immersions. We identify (locally) Σ with φ(Σ) and X(p) ∈ T p Σ with dφ p (X(p)) ⊂ R 3 + . We have, as usual, the decomposition T p (R 3 + ) = T p (Σ) ⊕ N p (Σ) into the tangent and normal spaces to Σ in p, respectively. Choose an orientation for Σ and let N be the (positive) unitary normal field along the immersion φ.
If X(p) ∈ T p (R 3 + ), we may write X(p) = X(p) T + X(p) N = X(p) T + αN (p), where α ∈ R.
Let < ·, · > be the metric induced on Σ by the immersion φ, making it isometric, ∇ be the Riemannian connection of the ambient space R 3 + and ∇ the induced Riemannian connection of Σ. Let X, Y ∈ X(Σ) be C ∞ -vector fields, then ∇ X Y = (∇ X Y ) T and B(X, Y ) = (∇ X Y ) N are, as usual, the induced connection on Σ and the second fundamental form of the immersion given by B. We also have the Weingarten operator
A N (Y ) = −(∇ Y N ) T so that < A N (X), Y >=< B(X, Y ), N > . Finally, the mean curvature of the immersion φ is H = 1/2 trace(A N ). Definition 1. A variation of φ is a smooth map F : (−ǫ, ǫ) × Σ → R 3 + , such that for all t ∈ (−ǫ, ǫ), φ t : Σ → R 3 + , defined by φ t (p) = F (t, p), (a) is an immersion; (b) φ 0 = φ.
For p ∈ Σ, X(p) = ∂φ t (p)/∂t| t=0 is the variation vector field of F and the normal variation function of F is given by f (p) =< X(p), N (p) >. We say that the variation F is normal if X is normal to φ at each point and that F has compact support if X has compact support. For a variation with compact support and small values of t we have that φ t is an immersion of Σ in R 3 + . In this case we define the (variation) area function A : (−ǫ, ǫ) → R by
A(t) = Σ dA t = Σ det[(dφ t ) * (dφ t )] dA,
where dA is the element of area of Σ. A(t) is the area of Σ with the metric induced by φ t . We also define the (variation) volume function V : (−ǫ, ǫ) → R by
V (t) = − [0,t]×Σ F * d(R 3 + ), where d(R 3 + ) is the element of volume of R 3 + and F * d(R 3 + ) is the pull-back of d(R 3 + ) by F . V (t)
is not, actually, the volume of some region with φ t (Σ) as boundary, but of a "tubular neighborhood" along φ(Σ) between φ(Σ) and φ t (Σ). The sign is related to the net change with respect to the normal field defining the orientation (for example, contracting a sphere in R 3 , which means moving it in the direction of the mean curvature vector, gives a negative sign for V (t), as expected).
Definition 2. Let F : (−ǫ, ǫ) × Σ → R 3 + a variation of φ. (i) F preserves volume if V (t) = V (0)(= 0), ∀t ∈ (−ǫ, ǫ); (ii) F is admissible if F (∂Σ) ⊂ Π, ∀t ∈ (−ǫ, ǫ).
Definition 3. We say that the immersion φ is stationary when A ′ (0) = 0, for all admissible variations that preserve volume.
Remark 2.1.
If Ω is a (compact) regular region in the slab F between the horospheres Π, taking Σ in Definition 2 as the (embedded regular) free boundary of Ω, we may extend the above variational approach to produce a variation Ω(t) of Ω by embedded domains (for small t), such that the condition V (t) = 0 in Definition 2 is equivalent to holding the measure (volume) |Ω(t)| of Ω(t) constant (the same as that of Ω = Ω(0)) along the variation. This justifies saying that the variation "preserves volume" in the above definition.

We end this section by stating again our problem. Let Π 1 and Π 2 be two parallel horospheres in R 3 and F = F(Π 2 , Π 2 ) the (closed) slab between them.

Isoperimetric problem for F(Π 1 , Π 2 ): fix a volume value and study the domains Ω ⊂ F with the prescribed volume which have minimal free boundary area.


Definition 4. A (compact) minimizing region Ω for this problem will be called an isoperimetric domain or region in F.

In more detail, one is looking for the classification and geometric description of isoperimetric regions (as a function of the volume value), in as much detail as possible, aiming at the determination of the isoperimetric profile (minimal free boundary area as a function of prescribed volume) for F.
