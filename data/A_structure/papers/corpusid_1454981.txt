Cell turnover time and organ mass loss 90 We searched the published literature for reported cell turnover times 91 for various whole organs and tissues, or for their major and most rapidly 92 replaced cell component. Data in humans were preferentially selected 93 where available; in the absence of data in humans, we used data from 94 rats or, if there were no data from rats, from mice. As organ turnover 95 times vary with age, those tabulated were of younger, rather than 96 older, adults. When turnover times were not available, they were esti-97 mated as the reciprocal of the fractional turnover rate (fraction per 98 day) or from half-life (day) data by dividing by the natural logarithm 99 of 2. 100 Reported turnover times for different parts of the respiratory tract, 101 dominated by endothelial cells, are particularly divergent. The bronchi-102 oles, alveolar ducts and sacs, rather than the tracheobronchial tree, make 103 up the major part (N 90%) of the lung mass. No estimate was found of the 104 turnover time for Type I epithelial cells, which constitute N95% of the 105 alveolus surface. During the age-45 related involution of organs and tissues (hereinafter "organs" for brevi-46 ty), functional or fundamental tissue integrity is reduced, chiefly by 47 one of four means: first, mass loss due to lack of replacement of cells 48 after apoptosis, necrosis, mitotic catastrophe and perhaps autophagic 49 cell death; second, irreversible cellular senescence; third, inflammatory 50 cell infiltration; and fourth, conversion of a functional tissue to another 51 form, such as fatty or fibrous tissue. Conversion to fat occurs in most 52 organs, but particularly in the bone marrow, breast, muscle, pancreas 53 and thymus. Both fibrous material and fatty infiltrates are associated 54 with chronic inflammation, a biomarker of ageing (Chung et al., 2009; 55 Richardson, 2011b; Schaffler et al., 2006). 56 Multiple interwoven mechanisms promote age-related involution, 57 including increased oxidative stress levels, loss of sex hormones, insulin 58 resistance (e.g., fatty liver disease), lowered production of growth 59 hormone, and tissue energy metabolism (Almeida et al., 2007; Krems 60 et al., 2005). In the case of sex steroids (hormones), their loss due to 61 gonadectomy or ageing is associated with involution of reproductive 62 organs, osteoporosis and cognitive decline (Lin et al., 2011).

Cell turnover, mass loss and implications for cancer and ageing 342 This work provides experimental evidence that the loss of functional 343 mass in tissues with ageing may be related to the mitotic rate or rates of 344 tissue turnover. This observation probably involves longevity regulator 345 SIRT1, the so-called "skinny gene", down-regulation of Q12 which is associ-346 ated with cellular senescence of endothelial cells and accelerated ageing 347 (Bai et al., 2014). It is particularly relevant to our findings that the 348 decline of SIRT1 protein with ageing is greatest in mitotically active 349 tissues (Sasaki et al., 2006). Similarly supportive is that highly prolifer-350 ative tissues exhibit greater telomere erosion with ageing, hence a 351 higher potential for replicative senescence (Ishii et al., 2006). The latter 352 state is present at considerable levels (N15%) in mitotically active skin 353 fibroblasts of aged baboons, whereas fewer (b3%) senescent cells are 354 present in their mitotically inactive muscle (Jeyapalan et al., 2007). 355 In young adults, tissue maintenance involves the removal of old, 356 damaged cells and their replacement by stem cells providing progeni-357 tors. In adults approaching old age, we have shown lack of homeostasis 358 and mass loss to be more prevalent in the most proliferative tissues. Two 359 means of losing functional mass involve the p53 protein, namely pro-360 grammed cell death (without cell replacement) and cellular senescence.361 Inactivation of these processes can have severe consequences. On the 362 one hand, one of us (Richardson, 2013) showed that p53 gene mutations 363 are involved in approximately one-third of the ageing-related rise in the 364 incidence of all cancers worldwide. On the other hand, mice with 365 increased p53 activity had a cancer incidence only 1/8th that of normal 366 mice, yet aged prematurely, with a 23% reduction in median lifespan 367 (Tyner et al., 2002). By contrast, mice breed by Garcia-Cao et al. (2002) 368 with extra copies of p53 genes were cancer-resistant but showed no 369 accelerated ageing. These augmented p53 responses in mutant mice, 370 with unambiguous benefits concerning p53 and cancer yet ambiguous 371 effects on p53 and ageing, indicate that other factors also play a 372 prominent role in ageing.373The breast is a well-documented example of an organ in which a 374 slower turnover time and greater functional mass loss have been report-375 ed to influence the cancer rate(Milanese et al., 2006;Misell et al., 2005).

5134Implications of results on theories of ageing 514 Reproductive value and contributions to fitness decline monotoni-515 cally during the reproductive period as fecundity falls and mortality 516 rises (Hamilton, 1966). At the same time, ageing is accompanied by an 517 increased likelihood, and the participation of deleterious mutations. 518 This has generally been viewed in terms of gene functional trade-offs 519 and the evolution of pleiotropy (Guillaume and Otto, 2012): genome 520 evolution being most influenced when selective pressure is greatest, 521 before sexual maturity, and decreasing thereafter.

Extensive stem cell proliferation can 547 foster a telomere erosion crisis, leading to oncogenic development, espe-548 cially if accompanied by dysfunctional cell apoptosis or proliferation 549 genes. A case in point: the unusually short telomeres observed in the 550 bone marrow cells of myelodysplasia syndrome patients become shorter 551 still upon the development of acute myeloid leukaemia with associated 552 FLT3, RB1 or TP53 mutations (Sieglova et al., 2004). In this cancer, cells 553 also exhibit exceptionally high levels of methylation ageing. At moderate 554 or high doses, radiation is a telomere-eroding and ageing agent, which in 555 A-bomb survivors has lead to high relative risks of myelodysplasia 556 syndrome and acute myeloid leukaemia (Horvath, 2013; Ilyenko et al., 557 2011; Richardson, 2009).558 We have shown that major organs exhibit a loss of actual and func-559 tional mass, attended by physiological or cognitive decline, surprisingly 560 even in young adults, when reproductive ability and hence natural 561 selective pressure is close to maximum. This early decline in fitness is 562 contrary to Medawar's (1952) postulate that ageing occurs after the 563 reproductive period when selective pressure is lacking. This claim is 564 therefore refuted by our study, and also by a study showing the onset 565 of senescence in dogs at 2.2 ± 0.8 years old, when large dogs are not 566 fully grown (Kraus et al., 2013). The first report for an early onset of age-567 ing for animals living in the wild is for wolves, which found that the onset 568 of ageing handicaps their fitness to escape predators (MacNulty et al., 569 2009), and presumably their reproductive success. The study found that 570 the age of peak ability to attack, select and kill elk was at 1 to 3 years, 571 around the time of first reproduction at 2 to 4 years old. One-fifth of 572 wolves were still alive at 10 years, subjecting ageing to selective pressure. 573 Similar conditions apply to premodern humans, including hunter-574 gathers, whose modal lifespan (or peak mortality) was estimated at 575 about 72 years old, with a quarter of all adult deaths at or above mode 576 (Gurven and Kaplan, 2007). Accordingly, evidence in young adult, wild 577 animals and premodern humans compellingly suggest that ageing, 578 and genes and mechanisms identified as pleiotropic and complicit in 579 ageing (e.g., TP53, TGF-β and replicative senescence) are subject to 580 strong evolutionary pressures.581 Weismann (1889) suggested the controversial idea that ageing may 582 have an evolutionary benefit: advancing death, thereby reducing the 583 period of reproductive decline and post-reproductive senescence, 584 hence making the best use of limited resources. In general, a species' 585 reproductive ability declines with adult age, with death commonly 586 following shortly after reproductive senescence (Zhao et al., 2008). 587 There are few exceptions to a short post-reproductive period, such as 588 when the usually hermaphroditic Caenorhabditis elegans mates with 589 rarer males (Mendenhall et al., 2011). Whales and humans constitute 590 other exceptions, as long-lived, post-reproductive females (i.e., mothers 591 and grandmothers) provide valuable aid to their progeny (Johnstone 592 and Cant, 2010). Notwithstanding these exceptions, ageing may be 593 beneficial in reducing the post-reproductive period and providing 594 nutrients and resources for descendants either indirectly or directly 595 and altruistically, as in yeast (Mendenhall et al., 2011).596Ageing in animals involves multifaceted mechanisms, including 597 replicative senescence and epigenetic inheritance, which are generally 598 considered to be genetically programmed. However, telomere-maintenance mechanisms are fully operational in human germ cells, most neoplasms (clonal cells) and biologically immortal species such as Hydra vulgaris that reproduce asexuallywhen food is plentiful (Boehm et al., 2012; Bridge et al., 2010). The immortality (and lack of reported mass loss) of Hydra is assigned to FoxO stem cell maintenance gene variants, which are also found in human stem cells, albeit at levels insufficient to maintain stem cells. Interestingly, a genetic variant in the FOXO3a gene region is more common in German centenarians compared with younger controls 615 (Flachsbart et al., 2009).The fact that organ involution is first evident in young adults can be also interpreted to indicate that trade-offs and pleiotropic gene activities begin to be expressed well before actual mortality. Thymic atrophy, a form of immunosenescence, begins even earlier in human development:loss of thymic epithelial space commences at 1 year old in humans and proceeds at a faster rate before sexual maturity rather than after 622(Steinmann et al., 1985). This thymic involution is driven by the prepubertal release of glucorticords, or infective or psychological stress.This demonstrates complex trade-offs, with an innate immune response predominating over an adaptive one, whereas impaired thymus function can, later in life, result in antagonistic pleiotropic features such as height-deficit, delayed puberty and increased vulnerability to acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (Kinouchi et al., 2012; Richardson, 2011a).Another trade-off, in which human lifespan is extended at the cost of reproductive success, was reported by Westendorp and Kirkwood 632 (1998) using historical data from the British aristocracy. Conversely, according to the disposal soma theory, finite food energy may be preferentially used for reproduction, compromising repair during ageing(Kirkwood, 1977). Indeed, the involution we documented in non-reproductive tissues could be redistributing energy away from mitotically active tissues to reproductive organs; however, this is doubtful, as we found a parallel mass loss in reproductive tissues.The prevailing view is that antagonistic pleiotropy is the dominant influence on the genetic architecture of human ageing. According to this view, beneficial selective pressure on pleiotropic genes arises from early-stage survival and reproduction trade-offs, with ageing resulting from selective neutrality of late-stage deleterious mutations 644 (Guillaume and Otto, 2012). Nevertheless, our research shows that mass loss in human bone and some soft tissue organs is first apparent soon after maturity, during the highly fertile period of young adulthood, contrary toMedawar's (1952) assertion that ageing is not subject to evolutionary pressures. We therefore surmise that the mass loss processes involved are in place and operational even before maturity, yet are masked or effectively countered by growth. If this mass loss is linked to replicative senescence that suppresses cancer during a period of strong selective pressure, then it has proven a highly effective evolutionary adaptation, as evinced by the very low cancer incidence of late childhoodfor males only (Kyle et al., 2001; Marcus
