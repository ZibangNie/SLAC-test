To What Extent Are College Students' Delay Discounting and Systematic Responding Rates Related to Time of Semester?
1 October 2024AE2FA35C70B56890ABBF3A854EDC7F4710.1007/s40732-024-00609-wAccepted: 18 July 2024 /
Researchers who use undergraduate participants may be concerned about variation in data quality at different times of the semester.For instance, students who are more self-controlled may get their course credit earlier in the semester whereas those who procrastinate may wait until the end.Therefore, a potential time-of-semester effect may influence research data.This study sought to examine the relation between time of semester and delay discounting (DD)-a process that refers to an individual's sensitivity to delayed rewards.DD may also directly relate to the decision to participate in research.Undergraduate participants completed money (n = 149) and food (n = 150) DD measures.Patterns in DD were examined across the semester.The number of systematic responders (i.e., those that follow a hyperbolic discounting curve) was also examined to determine the extent to which inattentive or random responding might be related to time of semester.Regression analyses revealed that money DD was consistent and stable across the semester.Food DD, controlling for subjective hunger as a co-variate increased slightly, though significantly, at the end of the semester.The percentage of participants with systematic discounting remained high throughout the semester for money and food DD, however, food DD had more variability, particularly towards the middle of the semester.Therefore, for college students, monetary DD is consistent across time of semester, but food DD slightly increases as the semester ends.

Convenience samples used in psychological research are often comprised of college undergraduates enrolled in lower-division courses (Peterson & Merunka, 2014; see Peterson, 2001).However, the time of the semester (i.e., 16-week period) that an undergraduate participates in psychological research may relate to study results.For example, Nicholls et al. (2015) noted performance differences in a visual digit task for undergraduate students participating for class credit across an academic semester, such that those at the beginning of the semester had greater sustained attention and performance accuracy than those later in the semester.Likewise, Casa de Calvo and Reich (2007) found that participants who attended a study early in the semester showed greater persistence in solving difficult-to-impossible anagrams relative to individuals who attended later in the semester.

Some research shows that students who complete studies at different points in the semester have different behavioral traits that may be related to impulse control.In particular, those who complete studies later in the semester tend to report higher levels of impulsive sensation seeking, which is consistent with a tendency to put off activities that are viewed as "mundane," such as completing research for class participation (Zelenski et al., 2003, p. 554).Further, personality research suggests that students that complete research at the beginning of the semester tend to be more conscientious and have higher grades, whereas those that participate at the end of the semester are less conscientious and have lower grades, supporting the idea that participants at the end of the semester put less effort into tasks given within the laboratory (Witt et al., 2011).These studies may point to individual difference variables in which sensitivity to delayed and immediate outcomes, such as grades, may be important in predicting performance.


Delay Discounting

A behavioral process that is related to impulse control, especially the ability to delay gratification is delay discounting.Delay discounting (DD) refers to an individual's sensitivity to delayed rewards (Madden & Johnson, 2010;Rachlin et al., 1991).In DD tasks, individuals make a series of choices between smaller, sooner outcomes (e.g., $1 now) versus larger, delayed outcomes (e.g., $10 days in 1 day).Preferences for smaller, sooner outcomes indicate higher sensitivity to delayed outcomes (previously called "impulsivity," but see Strickland & Johnson, 2021) whereas preferences for the larger, later outcomes suggest less sensitivity to delayed outcomes (also called "self-control").

When examining time of semester effects on DD, the extent to which discounting data are systematic may also be important.Discounting rates are considered systematic or nonsystematic based on criteria set by Johnson and Bickel (2008).A typical discounting function is hyperbolic in nature, meaning that the subjective value of a reward plunges at short delays and levels out at larger delays (Mazur, 1987;Stein & Madden, 2021;Voon et al., 2010); the steeper the plunge, the more sensitivity to delay.Most individuals' discounting patterns are consistent with this hyperbolic pattern (i.e., systematic responding); however, there are some exceptions.Random responding may suggest a lack of attention to the questionnaire or insensitivity to the delays or outcome and would therefore result in a pattern different from a hyperbolic function (i.e., nonsystematic responding).A meta-analysis by Smith et al. (2018) suggested that nonsystematic responders may threaten the validity of discounting questionnaires, and therefore it may be important to capture systematic responders over the course of the semester to examine possible changes.A more recent study by Craft et al. (2022) showed that the variation in nonsystematic responders was no different from a pattern of random responding, suggesting that the variation from nonsystematic responders is consistent with a pattern of careless and inattentive response (i.e., pseudo-random patterns as opposed to a situation in which participants are insensitive to delays).

For discounting researchers, differences in discounting and the extent to which participants provide inattentive or random response patterns that are related to time of semester may lead to methodological flaws or limitations in a study.For example, if data collection only occurs within the latter half of a semester, an inadvertent bias limits the range of discounting values in that sample.A higher number of nonsystematic responders during a particular time in the semester may also affect the validity of data.

To date, there is one time-of-semester research report with monetary DD.A brief report by Cassidy and Kangas (2014) found that individuals (n = 38) participating at the end of the semester had steeper discounting rates for money than participants at the beginning of the semester, suggesting that end-of-semester participants showed preferences for smaller, sooner outcomes over larger, delayed ones.It is notable, however, that this study may be limited by an underpowered sample, though no power analysis was reported.In addition, this study only reported one measure of discounting, area under the curve (AUC), which is atheoretical and does not consider the hyperbolic nature of the discounting curve.Systematic responding was also not reported.Finally, this study only examined money discounting; more studies that incorporate nonmonetary rewards are being used in discounting research, so it is important to examine nonmonetary outcomes (see Odum et al., 2020).Delay discounting for food, for example, may be important to college samples, who experience stressrelated eating and weight gain during the college years (Groesz et al., 2017;Pelletier et al., 2016;Yau & Potenza, 2013; see review by Lyzwinski et al., 2018).Because food delay discounting is a behavioral process related to obesity and higher stress levels (Hendrickson & Rasmussen, 2013, 2015;Hendrickson & Rasmussen, 2017;Law & Rasmussen 2023: Rasmussen et al., 2010), determining the extent to which time of semester may relate to food discounting may be important.

The purpose of this study was to determine the association between time of semester and delay discounting for money and food.Systematic responding for food and money across the academic semester was also examined.Similar to Cassidy and Kangas (2014), we hypothesized that individuals would show higher DD later in the semester relative to earlier in the semester across both money and food outcomes.In addition, we hypothesized that systematic responding would be higher earlier in the semester relative to later in the semester.


Method


Participants

Participants' data (N = 289) from the baseline session of a two-part cross-sectional study of monetary and food DD were used.Participants were enrolled in lower-division psychology courses and were recruited through a webbased sign-up database (i.e., SONA) between a 2.5-year period that included five consecutive academic semesters.Because summer semesters are shorter than fall and spring semesters (4 and 8 weeks), summer participants were not included.Inclusion criteria for the original study required participants to be at least 18 years of age and proficient in reading and speaking English.The current study is a secondary analysis of a larger study (manuscript under preparation) examining test-retest reliability of money and food DD.

A post-hoc power analysis was completed for this study for both money and food DD using the effect sizes of the results.For money, a linear multiple regression with one predictor, an effect size of 0.1, α = 0.05, and an n = 144, the power was estimated as 0.96.For food, a linear multiple regression with two predictor variables, an effect size of 0.085, α = 0.05, and an n = 145, the power was estimated at 0.88.


Measures


Delay Discounting Tasks

Monetary and food DD were assessed using a computerized, adjusting-amount procedure consisting of approximately 110 questions.Participants assigned to the money DD task (Richards et al., 1999) made choices between smaller, sooner (SS) versus larger, later (LL) amounts of money across five delays (1, 2, 30, 180, or 360 days).For example, the program asked, "Would you rather have $1 now or $10 in 30 days?"Each option was enclosed in a square that appeared on the top or bottom of the screen; placement of the SS and LL option was counterbalanced across each question.Upon selection of a choice via mouse click, the participant was prompted to answer, "Are you sure?" selecting either "yes" or "no."Selecting "yes" would result in a new trial and selecting "no" would lead to the trial being presented again.To discourage random or inattentive responding, the placement of yes and no was randomized on the left and right side of the screen.Subsequent questions would increase or decrease the value of the immediate option by $0.50 within a range of $1 to $10 until a preference reversal from LL to SS was found (e.g., $1.50 now or $10 in 30 days?).The median value between the delays that flanked this preference reversal was the indifference point; that is, when the SS and LL were preferred equally.This process of determining indifference points was repeated for five additional delay values (1, 2, 180, and 360 days).Questions were presented in a pseudorandom order and inconsistent responding (giving two different answers to the same question) would result in the question being presented until consistency (when the same answer was given on at least two consecutive trials).The task ended when indifference points were established across each delay for money.The program also assessed responses for probabilistic monetary and food outcomes.However, given the focus of this study on DD, those data are not presented here.

For those assigned to the food DD task (Rasmussen et al., 2010), the DD procedure was the same as the money DD task except participants made choices between SS versus LL bites of food (e.g., 1 bite now vs. 10 bites in 1 hr) across five delays (1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 hr).The range of bites varied between 1 and 10.Bites of food were standardized first by presenting participants with a 5/8" cube and asking the participant to imagine the cube represented one bite of their favorite food.


Subjective Hunger Questionnaire (SHQ)

The SHQ is a three-item self-report questionnaire that asks participants the time of their last snack, last meal, and to rate their subjective hunger on a scale of 0 to 100.This measure is used as a control for hunger levels while completing food DD tasks (Hendrickson & Rasmussen, 2013, 2017;Rasmussen et al., 2010;Rodriguez et al., 2018).The SHQ was not provided to participants in the money DD condition as subjective hunger has not been shown to affect money discounting (Rasmussen et al., 2010).


Substance Use and Alcohol Use Measures

Self-reported substance use was measured using the Drug and Abuse Screening Test (DAST-10; Skinner, 1982; α = 0.67).The DAST-10 is a self-report measure that assesses illicit substance use across 10 "yes" or "no" questions.The higher the DAST score, the greater the drug use.Nicotine use was measured using the Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND; Heatherton et al., 1991, α = 0.14).The FTND is a 12-item measure that evaluates the quantity, the compulsion to use, and dependence with cigarettes.The higher the total FTND score, the greater the dependence on cigarettes.Vaping use was calculated using a 12-item measure based on the FTND, such that the word "vaping" replaced cigarette use; the higher the score, the greater the vape use.

Self-reported alcohol use was measured using the Alcohol Use Identification Test (AUDIT-C: Bush et al., 1998;α = 0.85).The AUDIT-C is a three-item self-report measure that queries current problematic alcohol consumption.Higher scores on each measure of substance or alcohol use are indicative of more problematic consumption.These measures were used as potential confounding variables, as they have been shown to influence discounting rates (Bickel et al., 1999;Johnson et al., 2007, Kollins, 2003;Odum & Rainaud, 2003;Petry, 2001).


Demographics

The demographics questionnaire consisted of basic demographics and health variables (e.g., age, gender, socioeconomic status), self-identified or reportedly diagnosed eating disorders, activity level, and household eating patterns.


Biometrics

Weight (kg) and percent body fat (PBF) were recorded using a Tanita 2201 Body Fat Scale, as obesity status has been shown to predict both money and food DD (see Amlung et al., 2016, for review).Waist circumference was measured using a standard measuring tape (cm).Participants were then measured for weight (kg) and height (m 2 ) and BMI was calculated by dividing the two, respectively.BMI was categorized based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2020) BMI categories.BMI less than 18.5 is considered underweight, 18.5-24.9 is normal weight, 25-29.9 is overweight, and 30 or higher is obese.


Procedure

Participants arrived at the laboratory and met a researcher.Participants were asked to abstain from eating any food for at least 4 hr and drinking liquids for at least 2 hr prior to participation in the study.If a participant reported that they had eaten or drunk anything within these time frames, they were rescheduled.If the participant reported that they abstained from food and drink, they completed the consent process and were asked if they had any questions.Once consented, participants placed their personal items in a bin and were seated individually at a desk with a computer.Participants were randomly assigned to either a food (n = 144) or money (n = 145) group where they completed demographics, substance use measures, and a baseline DD task for either food or money.After completing the assigned DD task, participants were measured for weight, PBF, and waist circumference.Once completed, participants were awarded with course credit.All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Idaho State University.


Data Analyses

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 27 and GraphPad Prism v8 statistical software.For analysis, days since onset of the semester were calculated as a continuous measure.That is, there are 16 weeks in the semester, therefore the day of participation ranged from 1 to 112 (weekends included).

DD curves were characterized for each participant using the hyperbolic discounting function (Mazur, 1987;McKerchar et al., 2009):

(1)
V = A∕(1 + kD)
The hyperbolic function was fit to each participant's indifference points for each delay to the LL option using nonlinear regression.In the hyperbolic function, V is equal to the subjective value (or indifference point), A is equal to the amount of the LL reward, D is equal to the delay (days for money, hours for food), and k is a free parameter used to index the rate of discounting.Higher k values, a measure of discounting, suggest a relatively higher sensitivity to delays or higher preference for SS outcomes whereas lower k values suggest less sensitivity to delays or higher preference for LL outcomes.Given that k-values are significantly skewed, a common feature of discounting distributions (e.g., Bickel et al., 1999), k values were log10 transformed to achieve normality.

Area under the curve (AUC) was also calculated for each participant was calculated using the equation: where x 1 and x 2 refers to the delays associated with the reward, y 1 and y 2 refer to the subjective value of the reward in conjunction with each delay (Myerson et al., 2001).AUC values range from 0 to 1, where steeper discounting results in lower values and less steep discounting results in higher values (Myerson et al., 2001).

Systematic versus nonsystematic responders were identified using the criteria described by Johnson and Bickel (2008).Participants' discounting patterns were classified as nonsystematic if: (1) an indifference point that was greater than 20% of the previous indifference point; or (2) the final indifference point was not less than 10% of the first indifference point.The percentage of participants who had consistent discounting patterns was calculated per week in the semester and compared across the semester.When examining days into the semester, analyses were conducted with (1) all participants and (2) with only those that were systematic responders.

Hierarchical regression was used to determine the extent to which days since semester onset predicted monetary and food DD and systematic responding when controlling for potential covariates.Covariates were determined by analyzing correlations between discounting and demographic variables such as income or substance use, which have shown to be positively associated with monetary DD in previous research (Bickel et al., 1999;Green et al., 1996;Hendrickson & Rasmussen, 2013, 2017;Kollins, 2003;Rasmussen et al., 2010).Percent body fat and subjective hunger are examples of variables positively associated with food discounting (Epstein et al., 2010;Hendrickson et al., 2015;Hendrickson & Rasmussen 2013, 2017;Rasmussen et al., 2010).Significant correlations were controlled in the hierarchical regression by entering them as the first step in the regression model and adding days since semester in the second step to determine unique variance that was added to the model.

(2)
AUC = x 2 − x 1 y 1 − y 2 ∕2
Income was assessed in analyses as a categorical variable and a median split was used to dichotomize the variable with incomes.Median incomes were calculated for both money and food discounting individually such that participants reporting equal to or greater than the median income was coded as a 1 and incomes less than the median were coded as a 0. This coding scheme was based on previous research suggesting that continuous variables may be split into "high" versus "low" categories to reduce issues associated with nonnormally distributed data (Iacobucci et al., 2015).

All assumptions were met for all of the analyses conducted.

Discussion

The current study examined the extent to which time of semester predicted delay discounting (DD) rates and systematic responding for hypothetical monetary and food outcomes.Results revealed that DD for money, as measured by discounting (k) values and AUC values, is quite stable across the semester.That is, no one time predicted more variation or trends in monetary delay discounting.This was the case also when only systematic responders were used in the analysis.

Although our findings did not replicate Cassidy and Kangas (2014), who found steeper monetary discounting toward the end of the semester, it is important to note that this study reported data using a split-half analysis, comparing the first half of their participants to the second half, based on when the participants attended the study.When analyzing data from the present study in this way, the relation between time of semester for both money (and food) DD were nonsignificant, even when nonsystematic responders were removed from the analysis.These differences may be accounted for   by variations in methodology such as a small sample, which may have underpowered the analysis.In addition, participants in the Cassidy and Kangas study were given verbal instruction that there was no guarantee of research availability and were encouraged to complete their research participation as soon as possible.In return, it was reported that the majority of participants signed up within the first few days of class, whereas the current study did not encourage students to sign up at any particular time throughout the semester.These variables may be more systematically examined in future studies that attempt to determine the relation between time of semester and discounting processes.

The relation between time of semester and food DD, however, was more nuanced.When controlling for subjective hunger, days into the semester was a significant predictor of DD, with higher discounting rates at the end of the semester.However, when only systematic responders were used, subjective hunger was not a significant predictor and days into the semester alone predicted food DD.The analyses both showed that, as time of semester increased, food discounting also increased.For AUC, when all participants were used in the analysis, subjective hunger accounted for significant variance, but days into the semester did not.However, when only systematic responders were used, subjective hunger did not predict food discounting, but days into the semester negatively predicted discounting, accounting for 6% unique variance.Therefore, the general trend in the food discounting data suggests that food discounting is slightly, though significantly, higher at the end of the semester compared to the beginning.Though the effect sizes were small in this relation (R 2 = 0.08), they are within the range of effects sizes described in a recent review that describes the extent to which state variables influence discounting (Rung & Madden, 2018).

Food DD may increase over the course of the semester due to end-of-semester factors related to stress such as final exams and end-of-semester projects.A number of studies show that stress is related to higher food intake, weight gain, and steeper food DD in college students (Choi, 2020;Law & Rasmussen, 2023;Sinha et al., 2019).Although this study did not measure perceived stress levels or timing of exams or other projects, future studies could examine the timing of these factors on food discounting at different times of the semester and the extent to which food discounting is related.

Taken together, these findings suggest that money DD is relatively stable whereas food DD is significantly, albeit slightly, higher at the end of the semester.Odum (2011) refers to discounting as both a state and trait variable.That is, although discounting may be manipulated through various interventions, it is relatively stable over time and test-retest studies have shown relative stability up to a year after baseline measurements (Baker et al., 2003;Black & Rosen, 2011;Kirby, 2009).However, this study also contributes to literature suggesting that food is discounted differently (i.e., more steeply) than monetary rewards (Hendrickson et al., 2015;Holt et al., 2013Holt et al., , 2016;;see Odum et al., 2020).

Examining systematic responding throughout the semester, 91% of the total participants exhibited systematic delay discounting for money.For food DD, the average was 80%.There was also more variation in these data compared to systematic responding for money DD, especially near the middle of the semester.This range of variation in money and food DD is consistent with other studies (Hendrickson et al., 2015;Robertson & Rasmussen 2018;White et al., 2015).Time of semester did not predict percentage of participants with systematic discounting when all participants were used for money and food.This finding suggests that systematic responding is also relatively stable across the semester for money and food DD.

Despite finding no time-of-semester relation for money DD and no systematic responding effects for both food and money, there were differences in the number of participants volunteering at different times in the semester.Twice as many participants signed up in the last quarter of the semester, regardless of group.These data suggest that though students may procrastinate volunteering for a research study, the discounting data they provide for money are still within the range of values observed from those who provide data in the beginning of the semester.However, additional measures such as subjective hunger may be useful for measuring food DD.It may also be beneficial to track how many participants come in over the course of the semester for food DD particularly to minimize potential biases in data as those attending at the end of the semester may have slightly steeper discounting rates.

One potential limitation to the present study was that this study used a cross-sectional design, which does not allow examination of how participants' behavior may change across the semester.A study that examines within-subjects differences across the semester (beginning, middle and end of semester, for example) would be needed to determine within-subject consistency.Second, the sample of this study mainly consisted of white female college students.Future studies should aim for a more ethnically gender-diverse sample.Third, this study specifically used an adjusting amount procedure to measure discounting.It is possible that other discounting questionnaires such as choice questionnaires may be more sensitive to time of semester effects.Lastly, when examining time of semester effects, it is possible that other variables, such as midterms or exams may have an effect on DD.Future research should examine specific time of semester details, including environmental stressors such as exams on DD.

Despite these limitations, this study suggests that money DD is fairly stable over the course of semester and identifies trends which may influence food DD rates.

Moreover, this study provides evidence that certain times of the semester do not predict greater variation in systematic responding in food and money discounting.Because there are more students who volunteered at the end (last 4 weeks) of a semester compared to other times, researchers may wish to prepare for larger influxes of students toward the end of the semester or design ways to keep a consistent flow of participants throughout the semester.
