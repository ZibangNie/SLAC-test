Within the sport context, critical social agents, such as coaches and peers have a significant impact on youth developmental outcomes as well as participation decisions.While youth's interpersonal relationships with coaches and peers in sport have received considerable attention, to-date, there remains a lack of research examining the impact of such dynamics for youth development and participation decisions according to sport type.the purpose of this study therefore was to explore youth's relationships with coaches and peers in individual and team sports and the perceived influence of such dynamics on their developmental experiences and participation decisions.Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 youth athletes (10 boys and 10 girls), 13-19 years of age and data were analyzed through a reflexive thematic analysis approach.regardless of sport type, all athletes highlighted the critical importance of coaches and peers for youth development and participation decisions.however, team sports were perceived to better satisfy youth's developmental outcomes, such as meaningful connections, fun and enjoyment, athletic worth, and motivation and interest.the lack of connections beyond the coach-athlete relationship in individual sports reportedly contributed to decisions to leave sport.to optimize positive developmental experiences and sport participation for youth, coaches are encouraged to prioritize opportunities for youth to experience interpersonal connections with peers; specifically, nurturing peer connections beyond competition outcomes in individual sports remains an important area of interest.recommendations for future research and practice are suggested.During late childhood and adolescence, youth have a developmental desire to experience meaningful connections (Newman & Newman, 2018).Youth's interpersonal relationships serve as a conduit for other psychosocial development outcomes including identity formation, competence, motivation, belonging, and overall well-being(Bukowski et al., 2020;holt-lunstad, 2018).researchers have examined social agents (e.g., teachers, instructors, peers) and their role in fostering or impeding positive development across various youth-populated domains (e.g., family, school;Branje, 2018;Juvonen et al., 2012).Sport, specifically, is highlighted as a domain through which youth may experience and develop positive relational dynamics with critical social agents such as coaches and peers (anderson-Butcher, 2019;holt et al., 2017).collectively, coach-athlete and peer-peer dynamics underpinned by shared power, trust, respect, open communication, commitment, and unconditional support have been linked to positive developmental outcomes for youth, including prosocial behaviours, enhanced motivation, self-competence, meaningful bonds, fun and enjoyment, and overall satisfaction (Battaglia et al., 2022;Falcão et al., 2020;Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020).Unfortunately, not all youth experience positive interpersonal dynamics with coaches and peers in sport.Youth who endure coach-athlete and peer-peer relationships that are unsupportive, controlling, and abusive in nature, report negative outcomes such as mental health concerns, diminished sense of self, rejection, isolation, and tarnished social bonds(Waldron, 2021;Wilinsky & Mccabe, 2021).coach-athlete and peer-peer relationships have also been identified as influencing the maintenance or cessation of sport involvement during youth development (Battaglia et al., 2022;Moulds et al., 2022).

While researchers have examined youth's interpersonal relationships with coaches and peers in sport and their developmental influences (e.g., Jowett, 2017;Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020), these associations according to sport type specifically has received little attention.Sport type includes team sports and individual sports.team sports are commonly considered those where successful team performance and dynamics depend on athletes working together, social skills, and collaboration of all members' efforts (e.g., hockey; Kim et al., 2021).conversely, individual sports are commonly considered those where participation involves athletes competing against others for personal goals or there is no clear group task requiring all athletes to work together interdependently (e.g., golf; Kim et al., 2021).

Sport type may uniquely impact the nature and quality of experiences for youth.For example, the relationship between sport participation and mental health is stronger among team versus individual participants (Murray et al., 2021a;pluhar et al., 2019).Specifically, the social climate and connectedness experienced when engaged in team sports reportedly combats isolation and reduces negative mental health consequences such as anxiety and depression during youth development and into adulthood, compared to individual sports (Sabiston et al., 2016;Vella et al., 2017).participation in team sports is also linked with improved social skills, support, teamwork, emotional regulation, social capital, and feelings of social identity (Bruner et al., 2011).individual sports participation may contribute to increased concentration, preparedness, resilience, self-reliance, and responsibility (ortega et al., 2017; pluhar et al., 2019). in terms of negative implications, participation in team sports may contribute to greater incidents of substance abuse, bullying, and hazing, compared to individual sports (Murray et al., 2021b;Waldron, 2021); whereas the increased accountability for (poor) performance outcomes inherent to individual sports competition may heighten failure attributions, diminish motivation, and reduce fun and enjoyment (Nixdorf et al., 2016;pluhar et al., 2019).Few researchers have examined youth participation decisions according to sport type, and those that have indicate youth may withdraw from individual sports at greater rates than those from team sports (e.g., Baron-thiene & alfermann, 2015).

although previous researchers have indicated that sport type may influence athletes' experiences, several gaps in our understanding remain.For example, the reliance on quantitative measures, such as pre-determined questionnaires or self-report surveys has limited a deeper understanding of how sport type may influence athletes' experiences and developmental outcomes (evans et al., 2017).team sports have often been proposed as a more suitable context for youth to experience positive development given greater opportunities for social interaction and connectedness (Denault & poulin, 2016;hansen et al., 2010), yet individual sport athletes may also experience connections and thus relational dynamics remain important for their experiences.For example, rhind et al. (2012) found that athletes who participated in individual sports were reportedly more satisfied with their coach-athlete dynamic than athletes who performed in team sports.Findings also suggest athletic experiences that involve engagement in both individual and team sports are associated with more benefits, such as higher quality of life and positive developmental outcomes (agans & Geldhof, 2012;evans et al., 2017), however, little research has concomitantly explored the experiences of athletes with exposure to both sport settings.considering relationships serve as a mechanism for youth to reap developmental benefits, questions remain about how the relational dynamics youth experience with coaches and peers in individual and team sports may influence developmental outcomes.

the purpose of this study therefore was to explore youth's relationships with coaches and peers in individual and team sports and the perceived influence of such dynamics on their developmental experiences and participation decisions.the research questions that guided the current study included: "What is the nature and quality of youth's coach and peer relational experiences in individual and team sports?"; "What perceived influences might the relational experiences with coaches and peers in individual and team sports have for youth development?";and "What perceived influences might the relational experiences with coaches and peers in individual and team sports have for youth participation decisions?


Methods

a qualitative research design informed by a social constructivist paradigmatic perspective was adopted in the current study.ontologically, a social constructivist paradigm assumes a relativist perspective, which suggests there are no singular truths as reality and meanings are socially constructed and represented in multiple ways between participants (Denzin et al., 2023).While perceptions of reality may vary between individuals, elements of these realties may yet be shared (Denzin et al., 2023).epistemologically, a social constructivist approach embraces a subjectivist/transactional view, which highlights the active and collaborative involvement of the researcher and the participants in the co-construction of knowledge (Denzin et al., 2023).this paradigmatic perspective was deemed appropriate for this study as the youth athletes were invited to share interpretations regarding their coach and peer relationships in individual and team sports and the impact of such dynamics for their broader developmental experiences and participation decisions.Further, this paradigm acknowledged our active role as researchers in the collective generation of knowledge.


Role of the researchers

the researchers received formal education in the fields of qualitative inquiry and youth sport, have conducted numerous research projects on the psychosocial aspects of youth sport, and have experience as educators, mental performance consultants, and former competitive athletes.considering the principal investigator conducted the interviews and engaged in the initial organization and interpretation of the data, it remained important for him to acknowledge how his sport and research background influenced discussions with participants and the co-construction of meanings regarding interpersonal dynamics in youth sport.to ensure reflexivity, the principal investigator recorded notes in a journal throughout the research process highlighting positionality, member reflections, discussions with a critical friend (co-author), and emerging interpretations (see methodological rigor).


Participants

twenty youth athletes (10 boys; 10 girls) between 13-19 years of age (M = 16), from a large canadian city, participated in the study.the youth had between 6-14 years of experience with organized competitive individual and team sports.the sample included participants who self-identified as: Black (n = 4), asian (n = 3), and White (n = 13).the following sports were represented: ice hockey, baseball, basketball, dance, figure skating, golf, gymnastics, mixed martial arts, skiing, soccer, softball, swimming, tennis, track and field, volleyball, ultimate frisbee, and wrestling.

a youth sample was chosen as during late childhood and adolescence, meaningful connections, specifically with coaches and peers, are critical for developmental experiences in sport and participation decisions (Battaglia et al., 2022;howie et al., 2020).as the purpose of the study was to examine youth athletes' relationships with coaches and peers as well as participation decisions based on sport type, youth athletes who had experiences with individual and team sports and the inclusion of youth who represented various sport participation patterns were required to enhance diversity and gain multiple perspectives (Denzin et al., 2023).criteria for participation patterns were broadly informed by previous research which classified youth sport experiences as active sport engagement (never withdrew from any sports), sport-specific termination (withdrew from a specific sport but remained involved in others), and organized sport termination (left all organized sport; Battaglia et al., 2022).


Recruitment

ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University of toronto health Sciences research ethics Board (protocol# 00042301).participants were recruited through a combination of purposive and snowball sampling techniques (Sparkes & Smith, 2014).to recruit participants who produce the best data to address the research purpose (purposive sampling), a finite group of known youth athletes from individual and team sports and/or their parents (for those who were under 16 years of age) were initially contacted for study participation.the names and contact information of potential youth athletes and/or their parents were acquired from referrals provided by participants who had been involved in the researchers' previous investigations of the youth sport context.if the athletes and/or their parents were interested in the study, an interview session was arranged.Snowball sampling was then used to recruit additional participants (Sparkes & Smith, 2014), specifically participants were asked to suggest other athletes and/or their parents with a request for recommendations of athletes of diverse backgrounds, sports, genders, and participation patterns.all participants were contacted through e-mail, whereby the purpose of the study was described, and questions were invited.prior to study commencement, all participants 16 years of age or over were asked to sign a letter of consent.Youth athletes under the age of 16 were required to sign a letter of assent and parents of these athletes were asked to sign a letter of consent.By signing these required documents, the youth athletes and/or their parents also consented that the data resulting from this research may be used for publication.Demographic details (e.g., age, competitive level, years of athletic experience) were presented as summary data and the findings were presented as themed results only, limiting identifiable information (e.g., no athlete/coach names or club affiliations).


Data collection

Youth athletes were invited to share their views and interpretations on their relational experiences with coaches and peers in sport via a one-on-one semi-structured interview.all interviews were conducted online, ranged between 55-75 minutes (M = 64.8)and with the participant's consent, each interview was audio recorded.Data were transcribed verbatim after interview completion.a pseudonym was assigned to each participant and all personal identifiers were removed to maintain participant confidentiality and anonymity.

to develop a trusting and authentic relationship with the participants and thus elicit detailed responses (Denzin et al., 2023), the principal investigator described his own sport background and invited participants to ask any questions about his sport career or the study, prior to study commencement.Further, to facilitate a sense of comfort with the interview process, the principal investigator began by asking introductory questions, such as: "What sport(s) have you participated in and what led you to want to get involved in sports?" and "Describe some of your most memorable experiences in sport?" the participants were then asked open-ended questions, which allowed them to discuss and reflect on their relationships with coaches and peers in sport.Given the critical importance of relationships during youth development, the question focused on the impact of their relational dynamics for developmental outcomes and participation decisions.erikson's (1994) psychosocial Stages of Development theory was broadly used to inform questions regarding relevant developmental needs and outcomes for youth (e.g., sense of belonging, competence, fun and enjoyment).Sample questions included: "tell me about your relationships with coaches and peers in sport."; "in your opinion, how do your relationships with your coach and peers influence your experiences of fun and enjoyment?"; in your opinion, how do your relationships with your coach and peers influence how you feel about your athletic abilities?";and "how do your relationships with your coach and peers influence your thinking about whether to stay in sport or not?please explain." to address the research questions, athletes were specifically asked to reflect on their relational experiences according to individual and team sports.


Data analysis

interview data were analyzed through a reflexive thematic analysis approach, which involves data familiarization and familiarization notes; systemic data coding; generating initial themes; developing and reviewing themes, refining, defining, and naming themes, and writing the report (Braun & clarke, 2019(Braun & clarke, , 2021)).Following interview transcriptions, the principal investigator read and re-read all the corresponding data to become familiar with participants' interpretations related to the research purpose (Braun & clarke, 2019(Braun & clarke, , 2021)). he made informal notes regarding emerging ideas, interpretations, and topics of interests about youth's relationships in individual and team sports.Several semantic, surface-level codes, such as "coach-athlete and peer interactions in team sports, " "coach-athlete and peer interactions in individual sports"; and "relationships and sport withdrawal" were identified.a reflexive thematic analysis approach requires focus to be directed to interpreting patterns of shared meanings and understandings from participant responses as the analysis progresses (Braun & clarke, 2019(Braun & clarke, , 2021)).through the phases of generating initial themes; developing and reviewing themes; refining, defining, and naming themes, it was interpreted that youth's relational experiences with coaches and peers served as a conduit for broader developmental outcomes (e.g., meaningful bonds, fun and enjoyment) and participation decisions.the initial codes, categories, and preliminary themes were organized and interpreted into the themes: meaningful connections and a sense of belonging, fun and enjoyment, motivation and interest, athletic worth, and participation decisions.Further, within these themes, unique insights regarding youth's relational experiences with coaches and peers according to sport type were highlighted.the themes and associated responses represent overarching interpretations of the nature and quality of youth's relational dynamics in individual and team sports relative to development and participation decisions.the final phase involved generating a final report; to represent the themes, rich data quotes were provided, and the data were situated for interpretation within relevant psychosocial development and youth sport literature.


Methodological rigor

to ensure the quality of the research, a relativist approach was adopted (Smith & McGannon, 2018;Sparkes & Smith, 2014).the significance of the research contribution was the first criteria used to assess rigor; while youth's interpersonal relationships with coaches and peers have received extensive research attention (e.g., Jowett, 2017;Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020), to-date the influence of such relationships for youth's developmental outcomes and participation decisions according to sport type remains unclear.Meaningful coherence was considered by ensuring study aspects, such as the research paradigm, purpose, methods, and results were aligned.considering the social constructivist paradigm adopted, it was also imperative that the researchers, specifically the principal investigator remained reflexive about how his research and sport experiences may impact the research process.reflexive notes regarding participants' responses, context of the interview, and gaps or similarities regarding interpretations and theme development were maintained, to ensure that such experiences helped to inform rather than confine the research process.Member reflections and a critical friend (co-author) were also utilized to highlight the collaborative construction of knowledge; upon interview completion, the principal investigator invited participant feedback on his interpretations of the youth's responses and the co-author critically examined any potential biases, encouraged reflection, and provoked discussions related to theme development as well as the representation of participants' responses (Smith & McGannon, 2018;Sparkes & Smith, 2014).Further, aesthetic merit was established by critically analyzing the data and ensuring the participants' responses were coherent and provided a width of information (Smith & McGannon, 2018;Sparkes & Smith, 2014).in-depth resonant quotes were included in a manner that allowed readers to understand youth's relations according to sport type and the perceived influence for their developmental outcomes and participation decisions.


Results

the purpose of this study was to explore youth's coach and peer relationships in individual and team sports with a particular focus on the perceived influence of these dynamics on their developmental experiences and participation decisions.While the athletes' responses support previous research highlighting the importance of coaches and peers for youth development and participation decisions (e.g., holt et al., 2017;Moulds et al., 2022), the results focus on the relationship between these dynamics and sport type.according to the youth, relational dynamics in team sports were more conducive to their development needs and sport maintenance.the athletes suggested that the opportunity in team sports to have relations beyond the coach with peers contributed to their developmental needs and sport maintenance.


Meaningful connections and a sense of belonging

While all athletes identified relational dynamics with coaches and peers as influential in nurturing meaningful connections and a sense of belonging, the lack of opportunity in individual sports to form meaningful connections beyond the coach was problematic.Mason reflected, "the one person i had to relate to and connect with was an older coach…it was hard to make personal connections, it felt like we were from different worlds…i often felt detached from the coach." Beth explained, "Most of the time the main relationship is with a coach who is 40-50 years old…the coach-athlete relationship is very important but that's not really someone you can share life experiences or develop a close friendship with." as Fuad indicated, the nature and quality of the coach-athlete relationship was of critical importance in individual sports:

less options for relationships in individual sports means the coach becomes more important for your experi-ences…there is more pressure for the coach-athlete relationship to be positive.When i didn't get along with the coach in swimming it was brutal, that was supposed to be my main connection, the person who made me feel needed and without it i felt lost…my love for the sport vanished.there are more avenues to make connections in team sports but in individual sports all your eggs are in the coach basket.

athletes suggested that desires for belonging and meaningful relational development were best satisfied in the team environment.Kofi stated, "as youth athletes we want to fit in…team sports require cooperative interactions with teammates which provides you with more opportunities to belong and feel connected to something bigger than yourself."anna commented, "the lack of opportunities in individual sports to develop relationships with kids my own age is becoming a problem.peer relationships are rooted in the goals of team sport…when i participate, i'm not alone, there's a sense of togetherness."the youth also suggested peer relations may help athletes manage unfavourable coach-athlete dynamics and that the potential opportunities for peer relations (even if negative) in team sports was preferred compared to the lack of peer dynamics afforded in individual sport settings.lana explained: peer relationships can buffer the negative stuff like if i don't get along with the coaches but have good teammates then we can go through that negative experience together.the lack of relationships is hard to deal with in individual sports.in team sports i can interact with coaches, but i also have a chance to make friends so the opportunity to have relationships is doubled.i understand there might be times when peers can be negative, but i would rather have the option to make relationships with people my age than not.

in situations where the youth athletes competed in individual sports but trained with peers, strong relational dynamics and a sense of belonging were reportedly difficult to achieve.Noah indicated that while you may have training partners in individual sports, "it's hard to be friends when you are always competing against each other.in team sports, the focus is on building relationships, supporting each other, and working together…these types of peer relationships are not prioritized in individual sports."Further, when youth participated in individual sports but worked together with peers for a competition (i.e., relay events), they also reported that such interactions lacked meaningful bonds; specifically, when positive interactions were experienced, these interpersonal relations were often viewed as transactional and linked to performance goals.chane commented on her track and field experience: i have three girls who i train with, we get along well when we compete in the team relay events but beyond that the relationships aren't great, they are more for show.performance objectives are what hold us together so when this goes away and its back to individual competition, the positive connections fade.there have been a few individual races this season where i kept beating them and i noticed they got annoyed and a little jealous.they would make backhanded compliments; they weren't happy for me…i was making them look bad so they wouldn't talk to me.


Fun and enjoyment

athletes' perceptions of fun and enjoyment in sport were commonly underpinned by their relational dynamics with coaches and peers, however, the athletes suggested the impact of these social agents for their fun and enjoyment differed according to sport type.Sonny said his fun and enjoyment in individual sports was linked exclusively to his coach-athlete relationship, "times when i was having the least fun were when i had coaches who treated me more like a number than a person…this was the worst in individual sports because my enjoyment is directly tied to the coach." Jamila stated, "a good relationship with the coach is a must in individual sports to have fun, they're all you got…in team sports the coach-athlete relationship is important but you are also with friends…as a teenager that's the real fun." Grant compared perceptions of fun and enjoyment in individual versus team sports: the coach controls the environment…whether i'm having fun in mixed martial arts has a lot to do with the coach.When i don't get along with the coach, i hate sport.No youth will want to be in an environment 4-5 days a week with someone they dislike.the coach is also very important in team sport, but they aren't the only one that influences my fun…sure there may be some teammates you don't get along with but in my experience most of these relationships have allowed me to laugh and experience enjoyment.

the athletes indicated that team sports were commonly viewed as more fun and enjoyable compared to individual sports.peter stated, "For me and other youth athletes one of the most enjoyable parts about sport is being around people i love and being able to share moments with teammates." Beth described, "team sports provides youth with everything they need…i get to interact daily with people my own age and be social, which means my odds of developing friendships with teammates is higher, that's the real fun!" Karina compared the dynamics in individual and team sports and the influence on fun and enjoyment: in individual sports, whether i'm having a good time or not has a lot to do with my coach…but the coach-athlete relationship is more of a professional relationship.relationships with teammates are different, i can joke around with them and have more fun.i enjoy both individual and team sports, but it's easier to have fun in team sports.i know every practice i get to hang out with my teammates and that makes me happy.


Motivation and interest

coaches and peers were identified as influencing athletes' motivation and interest in sport.With regards to individual sports, the athletes acknowledged that a positive coach-athlete dynamic was a requirement to experience motivation and interest.Violet stated, "there's no ifs, ands, or buts about it, the relationship with my coach in track needs to be good…my interactions with the coach will determine if i am motivated and interested or unmotivated and disinterested in sport.Kofi commented "it's just me and my coach…my coach keeps me motivated and interested.if i didn't get along with him that would be a big problem…i wouldn't want to or care to train." cara stated, "My coach for tennis has always motivated me and made me feel good about myself no matter what happens in training or competitions.it's just me and her, she plays a big part in my motivation and commitment." coaches' abilities to empower athletes through their sport experience were also described as critical for individual sports.Noah explained: the coach is in charge, but it should never just be a one-sided relationship with the coach telling me what to do or not asking for my input.My coach encourages collaboration but if she stopped and was controlling then i would think 'what's the point of doing individual sports?'With little power in my own sport experience, i won't be motivated.

Despite acknowledgement of the importance of coach-athlete interactions for motivation and interest in individual sports, the athletes suggest how the peer dynamics inherent to team sports were better able to sustain interest and motivation.lana noted, "i've made some relationships with peers who i am still friends with to this day…those relationships got me through hard times in sport and kept me interested when i was ready to throw in the towel." Jamie indicated, "i want to play hockey every year not because of my coaches but because of my teammates…having good friendships can pick you up when you're down and help with your self-belief…those relationships impact my drive and motivate me to stay." Dylan stated:

Not having social interactions for teenagers can be a huge turnoff…i enjoyed participating in individual sports, but you need a constant drive for that type of sport.When my motivation started to go down it was easier to lose interest.in team sports, times where my motivation and interest dipped, friends kept me going.


Athletic worth

the athletes acknowledged that their interactions or lack thereof with coaches and peers impacted their self-perceptions about their ability.inadequate performance displays exacerbated negative self-perceptions in individual sports; specifically, the athletes suggested that the absence of collaborative interactions and efforts in individual sports meant that poor performances were more likely to be attributed to personal failures.Mason stated, "When you aren't performing well that's when you start to think less of yourself and question your skill.these thoughts and feelings were unavoidable when i played tennis and badminton." cara reflected, "it's difficult for me to find a good way to see a bad performance.i'm the one at fault no one else… it's an obvious reality of my failure." Jamie commented on the sense of personal achievement and satisfaction afforded to him when he displayed athletic competence in individual sports.however, when his performance faltered, the lack of peer connections made positive self-perceptions less likely, "When i achieved success in wrestling, i took all the glory and had high self-esteem…when i didn't it was very damaging for how i viewed myself.Not having peers meant i had to deal with these struggles on my own." the opportunities for relationships and collaborative efforts required for success in team sports reportedly buffered athletes' negative perceptions of their athletic worth.Jade commented, "i built relationships with teammates, worked together, and trusted them…it was easier to maintain positive thoughts about myself and have fun when i wasn't at my best because i had teammates to support me." peter stated, "in hockey, i never feel like i always need to be the best to feel good about myself or have fun because my successes and my failures are tied to the team…we deal with them together."

Given the lack of peer connections, the athletes further explained that negative self-perceptions in individual sports were exacerbated if coaches were critical of their performance.Fuad explained, "When the coach was unsupportive and hyper-critical it made it even harder to bounce back, my main source of support was bringing me down.i didn't have friends to pick me up and help drown out the coach criticism." likewise, edith commented: in track and field or dance when i messed up a sprint or didn't execute a solo routine, i always thought i sucked.it's one thing to think i suck but when my coaches also pointed out my mistakes it was worse…these are the people i relied on to pick me up, but they did the opposite, their comments were confirmations that i was inadequate.


Participation decisions

coaches and peers were prominent contributors to youth's participation decisions.Given the elevated importance of the coach in individual sports, the consequence of having a poor relationship with the coach significantly impacted withdrawal relative to team sports.Jade stated, "My outlook was shaped by the coach…he would make humiliating comments when i made mistakes…he impacted my decision to leave. in team sports you can have a bad coach, but you are going through it with your teammates." Steve commented, "i never felt like it was my sport experience it was more about what the coach wanted, so i quit.poor coach-athlete relationships are a deal breaker in individual sports because they're all you got." Jamila suggested: the coach doesn't even have to be bad…but if you can't connect on a deeper level with the one person you need to have a relationship with then there will come a point when you lose motivation, are having less fun, and just become disinterested in the sport because it feels like you are doing it alone, so you will quit.

Further, the lack of peer connections was identified as prominent precursors to withdrawal in individual sports.edith reflected, "team sports maintain its appeal because it satisfies youth's social needs.When i started high school, i made the decision to leave individual sports because they were interfering and taking away from my social life." Sonny explained, "i might not have gotten into team sports because of peers but as i've made friends, they have become the reason why i stay…in individual sports, friends are harder to come by so a lot of youth leave." anna acknowledged the importance of peers for sport maintenance and the lack of opportunities for relationships with peers in individual sports as a significant contributor to youth sport withdrawal, "i've become less interested in my individual sports as i've gotten older…a big reason is because it gets harder to go to practices and games when i don't get to be with friends…this is why many youth eventually leave." likewise, Grant stated: if you're in an individual sport especially during the teenage years, yeah, you still have a coach but that's not really a true friendship.You also might have training partners, but you won't achieve those strong friendships or a team bond…that's not appealing to many athletes.peer relationships are what give you that 'social life' and are a huge selling point that draws many kids back and makes sport fun.


Discussion

the purpose of the current study was to explore youth's coach and peer relations in individual and team sports and the perceived influence of these dynamics for their developmental experiences and participation decisions.all athletes in the current study acknowledged the importance of coach and peer relationships for their development and participation decisions, but they viewed the environment of team sports as more conducive to developmental needs satisfaction and maintenance of sport participation than that associated with individual sports.

the quality of the coach-athlete dynamic has been characterized by closeness (e.g., trust, respect, care), commitment (e.g., desire to maintain the relationship), complementarity (e.g., collaboration), and co-orientation (e.g., shared interests; Jowett, 2017).the extent to which these relational elements are achieved influences whether youth experience caring versus controlling coaching (Battaglia et al., 2022;pill, 2018), findings supported in the current study.previous researchers found that individual sport athletes felt more closeness and commitment to their coach compared to team sport athletes (rhind et al., 2012); however, when discussing sport type, the participants in the current study suggested that the lack of opportunities to form relationships beyond the coach in individual sports hindered their developmental needs for connection and belonging and fun and enjoyment.the athletes referenced difficulties with coach relatability; specifically, coaches were cited as being older, and representing an authority figure, thus not someone with whom the youth could share life experiences or form close personal bonds.researchers examining today's generation of athletes (i.e., GenZ athletes) found that trust, open communication, and the ability to create meaningful connections were highly valued relational needs required for youth to experience effective coach-athlete dynamics and positive outcomes (parker et al., 2012;Vance, 2019).coaches working with Gen Z athletes reported that communicating, connecting, and personality characteristics (e.g., entitlement) were challenges to forming relationships (Gould et al., 2020).although forming a meaningful bond is important across all coach-athlete dynamics, this may be most important for younger athletes and those in individual sports, who rely heavily on their relationships with coaches for growth and development (pill, 2018; rhind et al., 2012).

the athletes reported that positive developmental experiences were associated with coach interactions that involved being treated with respect, concern for their well-being, and listening to their voices.these descriptions are indicative of athlete-centred coaching -a leadership style that prioritizes athletes' needs and enables them to learn and have control of their sport participation-which has been linked to enhanced fun, competence, and motivation for youth athletes (Falcão et al., 2020;pill, 2018).Unfortunately, coaches may fail to provide unconditional support and engage in negative feedback (horn, 2019;Morbée et al., 2020), a notion that is particularly problematic in individual sports.individual sport athletes report greater negative attributions after failure, and thus reduced motivation and fun, compared to team sport athletes (Nixdorf et al., 2016;pluhar et al., 2019).the participants in the current study indicated the lack of relations beyond the coach-athlete dynamic in individual sports exacerbated the influence of the coach for their development; specifically, if the coach (their main source of connection and support) was controlling and provided negative feedback, this further contributed to their perceptions of incompetence as well as diminished motivation, fun, and enjoyment.

During youth, peer dynamics increase in importance as youth desire interactions with peers who share similar interests, values, and beliefs (Bukowski et al., 2020;Newman & Newman, 2018).Sport for many youth is viewed as an avenue to form and maintain peer bonds (anderson-Butcher, 2019).the collaborative nature of team sports reportedly provided opportunities to satisfy such connections; participants referred to desires to fit in and how their fun, motivation and interest, and competence perceptions were influenced by peers.Unlike coach-athlete relations which were described as more rigid and professional in nature, peer dynamics offered experiences of companionship and pleasant play, acceptance, relatability, intimacy, and support, common features of positive peer relations in sport (Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020).the notion that team sports better satisfied youth's developmental needs was also demonstrated in reflections of how peer relations served as a protective mechanism for poor coach-athlete relationships. the youth in the current study reported that connections formed with peers in team sports sustained their satisfaction and interest, whereas in individual sports when such peer connections were lacking, they reported diminished fun and interest.evidently, peers may help youth cope with detrimental experiences (e.g., poor coaching; Kavanagh et al., 2017) and contribute to motivation, fun, and sustained sport participation (Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020).

the athletes in the current study suggested that perceptions of athletic competence were better fostered in team sports given the collaborative efforts needed to achieve success.also, supportive peer dynamics served as a protective buffer when performance was sub-par.While peers may serve as critical figures for youth's support networks in sport (Smith & Ullrich-French, 2020), the youth's responses seem to contradict the growing body of research indicating that in the performance driven context of sport, relationships are heavily influenced by performance outcomes (Gould, 2019).Youth who are viewed as lower tier players or lacking skill often report poor relationship quality with peers; specifically, inadequate performances are one of the main precursors to youth experiencing rejection, isolation, and harm from peers and thus negative self-perceptions (Battaglia et al., 2022;Mishna et al., 2019).responses suggest that the youth in the current study may have experienced peer interactions in team sports characterized by unconditional support regardless of performance outcomes or, at the very least, perceived their peer interactions to be positive.

While responses suggest team sports and the associated opportunities to form peer relations is more conducive to positive developmental outcomes for youth, youth athletes may still experience (positive) peer connections within individual sports.researchers have indicated that individual sports may also contain group dynamics with differing levels of interdependence.For example, members of a gymnastics team may compete for both individual and team standings (collective team); members of a wrestling team may compete in different weight groups and contribute to an overall team score (cooperative team); members of a tennis team may train together but compete in individual tournaments, against one another, that do not consider team standings (contrient team); and members of a ski team that do not compete against each other and/or there is no team goal, yet they may identify as a group (independent team;evans et al., 2012;Kim et al., 2021).When individual sport athletes are required to work together, interdependence is high, contributing to positive developmental outcomes (e.g., enjoyment) and stronger intentions to maintain involvement (Donkers et al., 2018;evans & eys, 2015).Several athletes in the current study were involved in individual sports where they trained or worked collaboratively with others on certain events, however, such relationships were described as fragile and performance oriented.When the collective goal was removed (e.g., relay competition), relational dynamics diminished and thus were not interpreted as highly valued by the youth.conversely, in team sports, interdependence is a requirement which is known to foster more positive outcomes, such as teamwork, emotion regulation, initiative, and identity development (Bruner et al., 2017;Bruner et al., 2011).providing opportunities for youth to develop peer dynamics in individual sports beyond a collective competition outcome (e.g., relay race) is needed when considering the importance of meaningful peer social connections and their protective effect for youth mental well-being (anderson-Butcher, 2019;Murray et al., 2021a).

interactions youth experience with coaches and peers are commonly cited as influencing youth sport maintenance or withdrawal (Moulds et al., 2022), notions supported by the participants in the current study.extending this line of research, the participants suggested that youth withdrawal from individual sports was more prominent than team sports.the lack of relations beyond the coach in individual sports reportedly enhanced the power of the coach and the consequences associated with poor coach-athlete dynamics; to avoid interacting with a coach who lacked care or provided negative feedback, athletes withdrew.While youth withdrawal from sport because of harmful coaching has been well-documented (Battaglia et al., 2022;Moulds et al., 2022), the athletes also acknowledged that their interactions with the coach in individual sports did not have to be harmful per se to stimulate withdrawal.instead, if they lacked connection with the coach-the individual they interact with on a daily basis-experiences of fun, motivation, and interest were likely to be reduced, contributing to withdrawal.alternatively, the participants reflected on how the ability to form peer connections in team sports sustained their fun, enjoyment, motivation, and competence within the sport context.team sports provides social connections youth crave; in fact, youth sport participation decisions are often linked to external factors, such as peer groups (howie et al., 2020).

collectively, it may be interpreted that team sports best satisfies youth's needs because of the reported peer influence; peers may buffer negative experiences (e.g., detrimental coach-athlete dynamics) as well as maximize positive outcomes (e.g., sense of belonging, sport maintenance).Further, youth in the current study acknowledged that while peer dynamics may at times be negative, the benefits of having the opportunity to form connections with peers in team sports outweigh any potential costs.it remains important for coaches to find ways to improve coach-athlete and peer-peer connections in sports and individual sports more specifically.coaches should prioritize a culture of care through positive role modelling, open communication, shared power, and encouraging interactions beyond competition outcomes (cronin & armour, 2018).Further, to foster optimal peer dynamics, it is suggested that coaches deliberately implement activities such as those focused on enhancing interpersonal relations (e.g., personal disclosures, social events), group goal setting (e.g., outlining collective expectations and standards), problem solving (e.g., obstacle courses), and role involvement (e.g., peer-mentoring or peer assisted learning opportunities; Kim et al., 2021).these strategies may help to not only cultivate stronger coach-athlete dynamics but also peer connections, maximizing the benefits youth may achieve while actively involved regardless of sport type.


Limitations and future directions

the current study advances the youth sport literature by providing insight on the perceived influence of coach and peer relations in individual and team sports for youth's developmental experiences and participation decisions.although the sample for the study was exclusive to youth participants from a large city in canada, it is speculated that the rich description of responses presented regarding participants' relational experiences according to sport type may also resonate with other youth athletes and sport stakeholders, more broadly (i.e., naturalistic generalizability; Smith, 2018).participants from diverse intersectional identities, sports, and competitive levels were included in the current study, however focus was not directed towards comparing the impact of youth's sport relations in individual and team sports for development and participation decisions according to such characteristics.Future research would benefit from adopting an intersectional lens to compare identity characteristics on youth's relational experiences in individual versus team sports.For example, researchers might explore if the emphasis on performance excellence at more competitive levels of sport undermines relationship development and contributes to more fragile coach-athlete and peer-peer bonds.Future research would also benefit from exploring the perspectives of athletes from equity-denied groups (e.g., Bipoc, 2SlGBtQi+) as these individuals reportedly endure more negative interpersonal dynamics characterized by exclusion, denial, and harm (Gurgis et al., 2022).

the exploration of athletes who have persisted in individual sports to understand their perspectives and experiences with coach-athlete and peer-peer relational dynamics remains an area of interest for future research.athletes in the current study were required to retrospectively recall their relational experiences in sport and thus future prospective longitudinal designs are needed.For example, it would be helpful to explore the relational experiences of athletes in team and individual sports overtime (e.g., season, stage of development).Such research efforts may help to provide a deeper understanding regarding the process by which youth's interpersonal dynamics influence developmental outcomes as well as contribute to sport maintenance or withdrawal.Further, given the critical importance of relationships for youth's experience, it would be beneficial to conduct applied intervention research which educates coaches and athletes on ways to cultivate and maintain meaningful relationships in sport.


Conclusion

the athletes in the current study highlighted the critical importance of coaches and peers for youth development and participation decisions, regardless of sport type.however, when reflecting on developmental outcomes (e.g., meaningful connections, athletic worth) and sport participation decisions, team sports were perceived to best contribute to positive youth development and sport maintenance.central to the athletes' perceptions was that individual sports provided little opportunities for connections beyond coach-athlete dynamics.team sports provided youth opportunities to form coach-athlete bonds as well as bonds with peers, an important developmental need for youth and contributing factor to sport participation decisions.Given the critical role of coaches, it remains important to educate coaches on ways to foster cultures and experiences that provide opportunities for youth to develop healthy meaningful relationships with coaches as well as peers in both individual and team sports.
