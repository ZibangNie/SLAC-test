CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies in AML: breaking barriers and charting the future

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), characterized by aggressive relapse and dismal survival, remains a formidable challenge despite conventional therapies.Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered T and natural killer (NK) cells have emerged as groundbreaking immunotherapies, offering targeted eradication of leukemic stem cells (LSCs) and resistant blasts.CAR-T cells, leveraging antigens like CD123 and CD33, demonstrate early clinical success, with complete remission rates up to 66% in refractory/relapsed (R/R) AML.CAR-NK cells complement this approach through inherent tumor surveillance, reduced toxicity, and "off-the-shelf" feasibility.However, barriers such as antigen escape, heterogeneous immunosuppressive microenvironments (including intratumoral microbiota variations), and on-target/off-tumor toxicity persist, limiting durable responses.Innovations in dual-targeting CARs, cytokine-armored constructs, and CRISPR-edited universal cells aim to overcome these hurdles.Emerging strategies integrating checkpoint inhibitors, metabolic modulators, and AI-driven antigen selection promise to enhance efficacy and safety.This review synthesizes the evolving landscape of CAR-T/NK therapies, critically analyzing preclinical breakthroughs, clinical trial outcomes, and persisting challenges.By addressing manufacturing scalability, cost barriers, and long-term safety, cellular immunotherapy holds transformative potential to redefine AML management.As the field advances, interdisciplinary collaboration and biomarker-guided personalization will be pivotal in translating laboratory innovations into life-saving therapies for AML patients.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the most common acute leukemia in adults, remains a formidable clinical challenge despite decades of therapeutic advancements.Characterized by rapid clonal proliferation of immature myeloid cells, AML is associated with high relapse rates and a dismal 5-year survival rate of less than 30%, particularly in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease [1][2][3].Conventional therapies, including intensive chemotherapy and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), often fail to eradicate leukemic stem cells (LSCs), which drive chemoresistance and disease recurrence [4][5][6][7].While molecularly targeted agents such as FLT3 and IDH inhibitors have expanded treatment options, their utility is limited to specific genetic subsets, leaving many patients without durable responses [2,4].This unmet need has catalyzed the development of innovative immunotherapies, particularly chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered T and natural killer (NK) cells, which promise to redefine the therapeutic landscape for AML.

CAR-T cell therapy, a paradigm-shifting approach in hematologic malignancies, has demonstrated remarkable success in B-cell cancers by leveraging genetically modified T cells to target antigens like CD19 [8].Beyond hematologic malignancies, CAR-T therapy has shown promise in solid tumors, including sarcomas.For instance, clinical trials targeting sarcoma-associated antigens (e.g., HER2, GD2, NY-ESO-1) have yielded partial responses, though efficacy remains limited by tumor heterogeneity, immunosuppressive microenvironments, and poor T-cell infiltration [9].Similar challenges are observed in other solid tumors like glioblastoma and pancreatic cancer, where physical barriers (e.g., bloodbrain barrier, dense stroma) further impede CAR-T cell efficacy [9,10].Translating this success to AML, however, poses unique challenges due to the lack of a universally expressed, leukemia-exclusive antigen and the immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment [5,11].Despite these hurdles, preclinical and early clinical studies have identified promising targets such as CD123, CD33, CLL-1, and FLT3, which are overexpressed on AML blasts and LSCs while sparing normal hematopoietic progenitors to varying degrees [4,5,11,12].Concurrently, CAR-NK cells have emerged as a complementary strategy, offering advantages such as reduced risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), "off-the-shelf" availability, and innate cytotoxicity against AML cells [13,14].

Recent clinical trials of CAR-T/NK therapies in AML have yielded cautiously optimistic results.For instance, CD123-directed CAR-T cells achieved complete remission (CR) in 50-66% of R/R AML patients, albeit with transient efficacy and manageable cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [1,4].Similarly, CD33-targeting constructs have shown antileukemic activity, though myelosuppression remains a critical concern [1,5].These outcomes underscore the dual imperative of optimizing target specificity and mitigating on-target/off-tumor toxicity.Innovations such as suicide gene systems, transient mRNA-based CAR expression, and tunable platforms like UniCAR-which allow precise control over T cell activation-are being explored to enhance safety [4,5].Furthermore, advances in single-cell sequencing and epigenomic profiling are unraveling AML's clonal heterogeneity, enabling the design of multitargeted or adaptive CAR approaches to counter antigen escape [1,11].

The immunosuppressive AML microenvironment, enriched with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and inhibitory cytokines, further complicates CAR-T/NK efficacy [11,14,15].Recent studies highlight that tumor microenvironment heterogeneity extends beyond cellular components to include intratumoral microbiota, which influences immune evasion and therapy resistance in AML.Variations in microbial communities within leukemic niches can modulate local inflammation, alter checkpoint ligand expression, and impair CAR-T/NK infiltration [16].Furthermore, chronic disease models reveal that immune microenvironments operate through complex regulatory networks involving cytokine cascades, metabolic crosstalk, and microbiota-immune interactions.Targeting these networks could overcome immunosuppression in AML and enhance CAR efficacy [17].

Strategies to overcome this barrier include combining CAR therapies with checkpoint inhibitors, metabolic modulators, or microenvironment-targeting agents to reinvigorate antitumor immunity [11,14].Meanwhile, allogeneic CAR-NK cells, which resist exhaustion in hostile niches, are advancing through clinical trials, with early data suggesting durable responses and a favorable safety profile [14].

As the field evolves, the integration of next-generation CAR designs, biomarker-driven patient stratification, and synergistic combinatorial regimens holds transformative potential.This review synthesizes the current landscape of CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies for AML, critically evaluates their clinical progress and limitations, and envisions future directions to harness cellular immunotherapy as a cornerstone of AML management.


CAR-T cells in AML

CAR-T cells represent a groundbreaking advancement in cancer immunotherapy, with their development tracing back to the early twentieth century attempts to harness the immune system against cancer.The modern era of CAR-T-cell therapy began in the 1990s when researchers engineered T cells to express synthetic receptors that target specific tumor antigens, leading to the first CAR-T-cell therapy approval in 2017 [18].Structurally, CAR-T cells are engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) comprising three functional domains: (i) an extracellular domain (typically a single-chain variable fragment, scFv, derived from an antibody) for antigen recognition, (ii) a transmembrane domain for membrane anchoring, and (iii) an intracellular signaling domain that includes the CD3ζ chain for primary T-cell activation and costimulatory domains (e.g., CD28 or 4-1BB) to enhance proliferation and persistence [19].This design allows for enhanced T-cell activation and proliferation upon encountering tumor cells [19].Despite their success in treating hematological malignancies, particularly B-cell malignancies, challenges remain in applying CAR-T-cell therapy to solid tumors due to issues such as the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and T-cell exhaustion, prompting ongoing research into optimizing their structure and function for broader applications in oncology and beyond [20].

T cells interact with the immune system to target cancer cells by leveraging engineered receptors that specifically recognize antigens present on tumor cells.When CAR-T cells are infused into a patient, they seek out and bind to these antigens, triggering a cascade of immune responses.The structure of CAR-T cells includes an extracellular domain derived from an antibody that recognizes specific tumor-associated antigens, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain that activates T-cell functions upon antigen binding.Upon antigen engagement, CAR clustering initiates signal transduction through immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) in the CD3ζ chain.Phosphorylation of ITAMs by Src family kinases (e.g., Lck) recruits ζ-chain-associated protein kinase 70 (ZAP-70), which activates downstream pathways including phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCγ1).PLCγ1 hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), leading to calcium influx and protein kinase C (PKC) activation.This cascade culminates in nuclear translocation of transcription factors (NFAT, NF-κB, AP-1), driving T-cell activation and proliferation.Costimulatory domains (e.g., CD28 or 4-1BB) amplify this signal: CD28 recruits PI3K and Grb2 to enhance metabolic reprogramming, while 4-1BB activates TRAF pathways to promote persistence and antiapoptotic gene expression (e.g., Bcl-xL) [21,22].

This activation leads to the proliferation of CAR-T cells and the release of cytotoxic molecules such as perforin and granzymes, which induce apoptosis in targeted cancer cells [23,24].Additionally, CAR-T cells can secrete cytokines that further enhance the immune response and recruit other immune cells to the tumor site (Fig. 1) [25].

CAR-T-cell therapy has emerged as a promising treatment modality for AML, a hematologic malignancy characterized by poor prognosis and high relapse rates.One of the principal challenges in applying CAR-T-cell therapy to AML is the identification of suitable target antigens [26].Unlike B-cell malignancies, where CD19 serves as a well-tolerated target, AML cells often express antigens that are also present on healthy hematopoietic stem cells.This overlap raises concerns regarding ontarget off-tumor toxicity, making it crucial to develop strategies that increase specificity while maintaining therapeutic efficacy [27,28].Recent advancements have focused on dual-targeting approaches, such as combining CARs that target CD33 (which is expressed in more than 88% of AML cases) with TIM3, which is associated with leukemic stem cells but not with normal hematopoietic progenitors (Fig. 1) [29,30].These strategies aim to improve the safety profile of CAR-T-cell therapies by minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues.

Moreover, the role of LSCs in AML pathogenesis has prompted researchers to explore CAR-T-cell therapies that specifically target these cells.LSCs are implicated in disease initiation, progression, and resistance to conventional therapies.Thus, targeting LSCs with CAR-T cells engineered against specific antigens such as CD33 or CD93 could lead to improved outcomes for AML patients [31,32].While preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials have shown promise, the heterogeneity of AML and its microenvironment presents ongoing challenges [33].Table 1 Future research will likely focus on refining CAR designs and exploring combinatorial approaches to enhance efficacy while reducing toxicity, ultimately aiming for more durable remissions in AML patients.


Advances in CAR-T-cell technology

In the field of immunotherapy, next-generation CAR-Tcell therapy has made some new advancements because of its increased efficiency in overcoming certain limitations, which has improved responses to tumor cells and reduced adverse effects.Progress in molecular technology is responsible for these advancements [34].In the tumor microenvironment (TME), numerous factors contribute to the decrease in therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cells, including the presence of immunosuppressive cellular populations such as Tregs and MDSCs [26,35], along with the secretion of soluble inhibitory mediators such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [5].Furthermore, there is increased expression of immune checkpoint ligands that interact with their respective receptors on the surface of CAR-T cells, among which PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIM-3 and LAG-3, CD70, B7-H3, and LILRB4 are reported to be expressed on AML bleats [36][37][38].Additionally, there is a relationship between the chemokines produced by tumor cells and the receptors present on CAR-T cells, which is attributed to their misdirection and the absence of appropriate navigation toward the tumor milieu [39].For this purpose, many scholarly investigations have introduced various methodologies aimed at addressing these constraints (Fig. 2)..For example, a unique variant of CAR-T cells, designated TRUCK (T cells redirected for universal cytokine-mediated killing), was developed, exhibiting the capacity to synthesize an array of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-18, IL-15, IL-12, IL-7, and IL-23.These cytokines orchestrate potent anticancer effects through distinct mechanisms: IL-12 polarizes the TME toward a Th1 phenotype, enhances T/NK cell cytotoxicity, and inhibits Treg activity [40]; IL-15 promotes CAR-T cell persistence by upregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and sustaining a memory-like phenotype [41]; IL-18 synergizes with IFN-γ to boost NK-like activity and tumor infiltration [42]; while IL-7/IL-23 support T-cell survival and Th17-mediated antitumor immunity [43,44].Through paracrine interactions with TME cells and autocrine mechanisms, could increase their functional efficacy, augment antitumor responses, and promote long-term survival; however, despite these benefits, it is imperative to acknowledge the concomitant drawbacks, such as the risk of systemic cytokine accumulation and the onset of severe neurological adverse effects [45,46].Furthermore, by incorporating a CD40 ligand into CAR-T cells, the therapeutic potential of these cells can be augmented through two distinct mechanisms.One mechanism involves interaction with dendritic cells (DCs), which promote the release of proinflammatory cytokines, whereas the other mechanism involves the induction of apoptosis in tumor cells that express CD40 [47][48][49].Nonetheless, this particular class of CAR-T cells, which modulate anti-inflammatory cytokines, is not devoid of risks and, when combined with CD40L, exacerbates cytokine release syndrome [50].

The PD-1/PD-L1 axis is postulated to play a crucial role in the phenomenon of exhaustion observed in CAR-T cells, and blockade of this axis has a pronounced effect on enhancing therapeutic efficacy.For example, one strategy employs PD-1 dominant-negative receptors (traps)      that bind PD-L1/2 without transmitting inhibitory signals [51,52].Conversely, alternative methodologies harness a chimeric PD-1 receptor where its extracellular domain is fused to an intracellular CD28 costimulatory domain, creating a 'switch receptor' that converts PD-L1 binding into an activating signal [53].Scientists have also attempted to use genome editing methods to knockdown PD1.Using a different technique, CAR-T cells expressing an anti-PD1 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) [8] or PD1-FC fusion protein-which is created by fusing the extracellular domain of PD1 with the FC component of the antibody-were generated [54][55][56].A type of CAR-T-cell that secretes the CD47-signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPa) checkpoint blocker has been designed by cell engineers and is able to inhibit the induction of "do not eat me" signals by tumor cells and better phagocytosis processes by macrophages [57].Moreover, there is an increase in CTLA4 and LAG3 in chronic inflammation; therefore, suppressing their expression to enhance remedial outcomes may be advantageous [58].

A range of chemokines produced by tumor cells or their stroma stimulate growth and survival and can activate immune-suppressive cells.It was suggested that CAR-T cells express chemokine receptors on their surface as a solution to this issue.Some of these receptors, such as CCR2, CCR4, CCR8, CXCR1, CXCR2, CXCR5, CXCR6, and CX3CR1, can draw CAR-T cells to tumor blasts [59].CXCR4 is particularly crucial for facilitating CAR-T cell homing to the bone marrow niche.Engineering CAR-T cells to express CXCR4 serves the practical purpose of enhancing their infiltration into leukemic sanctuaries, thereby improving antitumor efficacy [60,61].

Recently, advancements in the methodologies of genetic engineering have led to an increased focus on various forms of CAR-T cells [34].The designation of universal CAR-T cells pertains to a category of allogeneic CAR-T cells that have undergone targeted modification via CRISPR-Cas9 or TALEN methodologies aimed at disrupting MHC and TCR genes to prevent graftversus-host disease (GVHD) and host rejection [34,62].This class of genetically modified cells presents numerous advantages over traditional CAR-T cells, which are autologous.Universal CAR-T cells do not encounter the challenges associated with autologous cell therapies, such as the protracted duration required to implement modifications to these cells, the overall efficiency, and the limited yield of high-quality cells produced [34].However, allogeneic approaches face limitations including reduced in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells, potential for host immune rejection, and higher susceptibility to exhaustion compared to autologous counterparts [63,64].

To mitigate the cytotoxicity and severe neurological complications associated with CAR-T-cell therapy, a novel approach has been proposed to control and eliminate CAR-T cells when necessary [65].This method involves the transduction of genes related to specific surface antigens, and one of the most common approaches is the incorporation of a truncated form of the epidermal growth factor receptor (tEGFR) on the surface of CAR-T cells [66].By administering a monoclonal anti-EGFR antibody, such as cetuximab, these modified CAR-T cells can be inactivated or "switched off."This process is known as the "suicide switch" or "safety switch" approach.The presence of tEGFR on the surface of CAR-T cells allows their destruction through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) mediated by cetuximab [55,56,66,67].This innovative strategy enhances the safety profile of CAR-T-cell therapy by providing a mechanism to control and eliminate engineered cells in cases of adverse events or excessive activation [5,68].

One of the prevalent challenges in AML is the phenomenon of antigenic escape [69].To address this challenge, engineering CAR-T cells that can concurrently target two distinct antigens, which are typically significant and present a high frequency in AML, has been suggested, resulting in a marked increase in the therapeutic response [70].Numerous investigations have explored the therapeutic implications of dual-targeting CAR-T cells.For example, data reveal the better efficacy of CAR-T cells, which target CD33 and C-type lectin-like molecule-1 (CLL-1) antigens and yield exceptionally promising outcomes [1,71].Furthermore, when the target antigens are expressed at low levels and a robust antitumor response is essential, CAR-T cells designed to target CLL1 in conjunction with either CD33 or CD123 effectively fulfill these requirements [69].These studies further highlight the importance of this type of CAR-T-cell design.Despite the numerous advantages associated with this class of engineered CAR-T cells (Fig. 2), various challenges remain, necessitating further research to establish the optimal combination of target antigens while minimizing toxicity [1].


CAR-NK therapy in AML


Mechanism of CAR-NK cells

CAR-NK cells integrate the antigen-specific targeting characteristic of CAR-T cells with the inherent activity of NK cell adoptive cell transfer (ACT), establishing a platform that addresses numerous limitations associated with therapeutic approaches in AML [72].CAR-NK cells eliminate malignant cells through mechanisms involving the CAR, which explicitly involves TAAs and the intrinsic receptors of NK cells.The mechanism of NK cell function is one of the main pillars of CAR-NK therapy effectiveness.NK cells exhibit functional similarities to CD8 + T cells; however, they do not necessitate prior sensitization or express T-cell receptors (TCRs), resulting in responses that are not restricted by human leukocyte antigens (HLAs) [73].The functionality of NK cells is contingent upon the equilibrium established between activating and inhibitory signals produced by various germline-encoded receptors, including natural killer group 2D (NKG2D).Consequently, NK cells maintain their capacity for cytotoxicity independent of CAR mechanisms via these innate receptors, even in situations where tumors exhibit escape strategies such as the loss or downregulation of CAR antigens [74].NK cells can eradicate malignant cells through several mechanisms, including CD16-mediated antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), direct cytotoxicity facilitated by cytolytic granules such as perforin and granzymes, or the activation of death receptors such as FAS ligand (FASL) or TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) [74].Furthermore, NK cells are highly efficient at producing chemokines and cytokines, which are crucial in modulating other immune mediators involved in cytotoxicity [75].Consequently, the redirection of NK cells to express a CAR might increase the efficacy of tumor eradication in heterogeneous tumor populations and mitigate the probability of relapse associated with CAR-dependent mechanisms.Accordingly, CAR-NK-cell therapy presents promising opportunities to overcome the constraints associated with CAR-T-cell therapies.Without an optimal CAR target antigen, the innate and alloreactive capabilities of NK cells facilitate their efficacy against antigen-negative subclones in AML.In contrast to T cells, NK cells do not necessitate previous exposure to antigens for their activation.In addition, NK cells do not induce GVHD and identify malignant cells in an antigenindependent fashion through an inherent killing ability regulated by various activating and inhibitory cell-surface receptors [76].Unlike T cells, adoptive CAR-NK therapy does not result in significant adverse events, including on-target off-tumor toxicity, CRS, or immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), which can prolong hospitalization and increase therapy costs [77].The limited lifespan of NK cells in vivo, along with the diverse array of cytokines and growth factors released during NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity (such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, GM-CSF, and IL-3), likely accounts for these advantages and recent advances in NK-CAR therapy in AML patients (Fig. 3).


Current clinical trials and results

Despite the potential advantages of the NK cell approach, the range of CAR target antigens relevant to AML still needs to be developed.The evaluation of the expression of possible targets by NK cells is necessary to assess the risk of fratricide, which might limit its efficacy.CD33 represents a potential target antigen for CAR-NK cell therapy [78].In more than 90% of patients diagnosed with AML, this marker is present on the surface of leukemic cells [79].In the research conducted by Albinger  [80].CAR-NK cells exhibited significant cytotoxic activity in vitro and in vivo against AML cells.Pediatric AML often presents elevated CD123 expression, suggesting that it is a compelling target for CAR-NK-cell therapy.Caruso et al. developed second-generation CD123-CAR-NK cells employing 4-1BB costimulatory domains [81].These CAR-NK cells exhibited an enhanced safety profile relative to that of CD123 CAR-T cells while preserving significant anti-leukemia effects both in vitro and in vivo.CD38 serves as another promising target for immunotherapeutic approaches in AML.A recent investigation revealed that the integration of CD38 knockout CAR-NK cells, which express an affinity-optimized CD38, along with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), significantly improved the targeting efficacy and cytotoxic potential of NK cells directed against CD38-positive mature myeloid cells [82].

These preclinical findings have prompted several ongoing clinical trials to assess the effectiveness of CAR-NK-cell therapy, with some exhibiting encouraging preliminary outcomes.The ongoing clinical trials evaluating CAR-NK cell therapy for AML patients are presented in Table 2. Recently, phase I follow-up data related to a few patients have been released from NKX101 (NKG2D-CAR-NK, NCT04623944) in AML, indicating complete remission with an incomplete count recovery (CR/ CRi) rate as high as 67% (4/6).The phase 1 clinical trial NCT05008575, which used anti-CD33 CAR-NK cells in combination with fludarabine and cytoxan, represents a considerable advancement in AML therapy.On the basis of the initial phase 1 results of this clinical trial, which evaluated human umbilical cord-derived CD33 CAR-NK cells in patients with R/R AML, in 10 evaluated patients, only one patient developed grade 2 CRS, and no highergrade CRS occurred.There were no ICANS instances of any grade, and all R/R AML patients with grades 3-4 bone marrow (BM) suppression recovered within one month.With respect to antileukemia effectiveness, 60% (6/10) of the patients achieved a CR 28 days after CAR-NK cell infusion.Another clinical trial (NCT05574608), aimed at evaluating the infusion of allogenic CD123-CAR-NK cells following FC (F, fludarabine, C, cyclophosphamide) chemotherapy in R/R AML patients, is ongoing in early phase 1.These findings highlight the potential of CAR-NK cells for "off-the-shelf" accessibility and a decreased risk of CRS.Despite the considerable challenge of overcoming the suppressive tumor microenvironment in CAR design, particularly before the integration of CAR-NK cells with other therapies, findings from preclinical and clinical studies have facilitated the advancement of more effective CAR-NK cell research, aiming for a more potent antitumor response with fewer adverse effects than those of CAR-T cells.Notably, CAR-NK trials reported only 5-10% grade ≥ 2 CRS and no severe ICANS, attributed to reduced IL-6/IL-1 secretion [83].However, IL-12-driven neurotoxicity occurred in 2 patients receiving high-dose (> 10 8 cells/kg) CD123-CAR-NK therapy, necessitating protocol modifications [83,84].


Advances in CAR-NK cell technology

The advent of CAR-T cells has initiated a paradigm shift in the therapeutic approach to hematological malignancies; however, this modality is encumbered by several constraints, including the propensity for inducing a cytokine storm and the necessity for a substantial quantity of these cells, alongside the protracted production timeline and escalating costs associated with their development [85].Furthermore, these therapies necessitate prior exposure to specific antigens prior to administration, and they are occasionally linked to GVHD [86,87].The introduction of CAR-NK cells has garnered significant attention as a potential alternative, effectively addressing the limitations inherent in CAR-T-cell therapy [88].These cells possess an intrinsic capacity to engage and eliminate neoplastic cells, particularly in scenarios where the adaptive immune response is ineffective in recognizing tumors that employ evasion strategies, because NK cells do not rely on the identification of tumor-associated antigens for their activation [89].The cells in question lack an intrinsic response; consequently, the generation of CAR-NK cells necessitates no prior antigen exposure prior to administration.Thus, NK cell therapy is more time-efficient than CAR-T-cell therapy is [88].Furthermore, these cells exhibit a distinct cytokine secretion profile relative to that of T cells, thereby reducing the likelihood of adverse effects, such as cytokine storms, associated with this therapeutic approach [90].Nevertheless, certain constraints persist regarding the generation of CAR-NK cells and their therapeutic application; scholars are currently exploring novel approaches to surmount these limitations, thereby propelling advancements in the domain of CAR-NK cell therapy [91].

Along with the field of CAR-T cells, one of the hottest topics of discussion is the growth of CAR-NK cells.Cytokines can be useful tools for producing large quantities of self-produced CAR-NK cell batches; in particular, using feeder cells can be very successful in reaching this objective, but there are safety concerns associated with leaving some feeder cells in the culture medium [92,93].Consequently, different approaches have been proposed, such as the use of microspheres made of a dissolvent polymer that can release growth factors into the environment gradually [94].

Transfer of genes is required to apply modifications to NK cells; viral vectors are used for this purpose; however, there is a possibility of mutagenesis associated with this type of gene transfer, although it has not been demonstrated.Further research is necessary to fully rule out this risk, even though follow-up studies in the field of CAR-T cells did not reveal any issues of this type [95].Among CAR-T cells, the vesicular stomatitis virus-G protein (VSV-G) is the most frequently used receptor pseudotype [96].However, in CAR-NK cells, the Baboon envelope pseudotyped lentiviral vector (BaEV-LV) has been linked to better outcomes, particularly as it can also transfer CRISPR [97].

The loss of tumor antigens is another issue in the field of NK cell-mediated therapy [98].Unlike CAR-T cells, NK cells are not dependent on tumor antigen recognition; however, it has been demonstrated that in certain cases, tumors can be misdiagnosed by NK cells and escape [99].Dual-targeting CAR-NK cells constitute one approach used to address this issue; however, careful target selection is needed [100].Additionally, multifactorial engineering is highly effective.For example, iPSCderived NK cells engineered with an anti-CD19 CAR, a high-affinity non-cleavable CD16 (hnCD16) variant, and an IL-15/IL-15 receptor α fusion protein (IL-15RF) demonstrated enhanced persistence and efficacy in preclinical models [101].Reducing the likelihood of cytotoxicity for nontumor tissues that express the same antigen is a factor that should be taken into account when choosing the target.Data reveal that using CAR types that bind to target antigens with a lower affinity and are activated only when dealing with tumor cells that express these targets at high concentrations is the solution to this issue [34].The cytotoxic qualities of these compounds are evident.Moreover, the implantation of suicide switches in CAR-NK cells is another innovation that has been used to address this issue; the use of inducible caspase 9 has been linked to encouraging outcomes [102,103].

To mitigate the effects of the tumor environment and its byproducts, such as hydrogen peroxide, on CAR-NK cells, researchers have used genetic manipulation to induce the production of peroxiredoxin-1 in these cells, which in turn causes these cells to interact more with the tumor environment [104].These effects are caused by the low pH of the tumor environment, a lack of oxygen and nutrients, and the production of some of the metabolites in the TME [105].In addition, TGF-β is thought to be an additional immunosuppressive factor in the tumor microenvironment.By eliminating TGF-βRII, researchers were able to increase the antitumor effects of NK cells and their stability in the TME [106].Because tumor cells can suppress the immune system by activating the inhibitory receptors of NK cells, researchers have knocked out certain receptors, such as NKG2A, and are studying how well they work as treatments [107,108].The findings of an additional investigation demonstrated that CD19-CAR-NK-92 cells with knockout of the CBLB (Cbl Proto-Oncogene B) and TIGIT genes, as well as CD276-CAR-NK-92 cells with knockout of the CBLB, NKG2A, and TIGIT genes via the CRISPR-CAS9 methodology, exhibited significant cytotoxic efficacy against AML cell lines [108].

Compared with T cells, NK cells are less durable in the body, which is considered one of the limitations of NK cell therapy [106].To solve this problem, the treatment efficiency can be increased by engineering NK cells to constitutively express cytokines like IL-15 [109] or by coexpressing cytokine receptors such as the IL-2/15 receptor β chain (IL-2/15Rβ) to enhance responsiveness [110].

However, when these cytokines are administered, one should be careful because systemic administration of high amounts of these cytokines can cause vascular leakage syndrome [111].In addition, short-term exposure of these cells to IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18 can cause memorylike properties [112].Additionally, the preconditioning regimen that is prescribed for people before injection is very effective in increasing the longevity of the cells.For this purpose, the combination of fludarabine and cyclophosphamide is used, which has the role of draining the lymph node, and through reprogramming the tumor microenvironment, the possibility of cell transplant rejection is reduced [113].In some types of CAR-NK cells, a type of cell killing called fratricide has been observed.This event is related to activating CAR (aCAR) and is caused by the process of trogocytosis, in which antigens from the plasma membrane of the presenter cells are absorbed by lymphocytes [114].Data show that this process is one of the methods of regulating the immune system and that it is caused by antigen receptor signaling.The answer to this issue came with the development of AI CARs, a novel subset of CAR-NK cells.In these particular cells, inhibitory CARs are concurrently engineered on the same NK cells to prevent their fratricide properties, which also increases cell survival, in addition to activating CARs [115].

In conclusion, while CAR-NK cells offer a plethora of benefits that have effectively addressed some of the drawbacks of CAR-T-cell therapy, the therapy itself is not perfect and still has certain drawbacks that researchers are working to resolve.The search for new approaches is what keeps this field moving forward [91].


Challeng and limitation

Despite the transformative potential of CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies in AML, several critical challenges and limitations hinder their widespread clinical application.A primary obstacle lies in the identification of leukemia-specific antigens, as AML blasts and LSCs often share surface markers with healthy hematopoietic progenitors, raising the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity and myelosuppression [116][117][118].For instance, targeting CD33 or CD123 may inadvertently deplete normal myeloid cells, leading to prolonged cytopenias and compromised immune reconstitution [119].Furthermore, antigen escape-a phenomenon where leukemic cells downregulate target antigens-remains a persistent issue, with up to 40% of relapsed AML cases showing loss of CAR-targeted markers [120].

The immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment poses another formidable barrier.Notably, spatial heterogeneity in intratumoral microbiota correlates with immunosuppressive niche formation in AML, potentially contributing to CAR-T/NK dysfunction [16].Microbiota-derived metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) may further dampen antitumor immunity by promoting Treg expansion and inhibiting effector cell cytotoxicity [17].These findings underscore the need to map microbial-immune interactions within leukemic niches to design microbiota-modulating adjuvants for CAR therapies.MDSCs, Tregs, and inhibitory cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10 create a hostile niche that dampens CAR cell activity and promotes exhaustion [121,122].Even advanced CAR designs, such as those incorporating cytokine-secreting "armored" constructs, struggle to sustain long-term efficacy in this setting [123].Additionally, CAR-T cells face intrinsic limitations, including poor persistence in vivo and terminal differentiation after repeated antigen exposure, which curtails their durability [124].While CAR-NK cells exhibit reduced exhaustion compared to T cells, their shorter lifespan and limited expansion capacity restrict sustained antitumor responses [125].

Manufacturing complexities further impede progress.Autologous CAR-T therapies require personalized production, leading to delays (often > 2 weeks) that are untenable for rapidly progressing AML patients [126].Allogeneic "off-the-shelf" CAR-NK products, though promising, face scalability challenges due to donor variability, limited cell yields, and the need for genetic modifications to enhance persistence [127].Cost remains a critical concern, with CAR-T therapies exceeding around $500,000 per treatment, rendering them inaccessible in resource-limited settings [128,129].

Safety risks, though less pronounced in CAR-NK therapies, persist.CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) occur in 20-40% of CAR-T recipients, necessitating intensive monitoring and management [130,131].While CAR-NK cells exhibit milder toxicity profiles, fratricide-a phenomenon where CAR-NK cells attack each other due to shared antigen expression-has been reported in preclinical models targeting markers like CD33 [132].Finally, long-term safety data remain sparse, particularly for novel dual-targeting CARs and gene-edited universal cells, raising concerns about genotoxicity and unintended off-target effects [63].

CRS arises from rapid immune activation, triggering massive cytokine release (e.g., IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF-α) that induces systemic inflammation, endothelial activation, and multiorgan dysfunction [128,133].ICANS involves cytokine-mediated blood-brain barrier disruption, microglial activation, and neuronal toxicity, often manifesting as confusion, seizures, or cerebral edema [134].While CAR-NK cells secrete lower levels of IL-1/IL-6 and exhibit reduced CRS incidence, IL-12/IFN-γ-driven neurotoxicity remains a concern in high-dose regimens [83,88].The following can be considered to prevent, management and reduce the risk of CRS and ICANS, (1) Pharmacological prophylaxis: Prophylactic dosing of tocilizumab (an IL-6R antagonist) reduces the incidence of severe CRS by 60% without compromising efficacy [135].(2) CAR design modifications: Caspase-9-induced "safety switches" allow rapid clearance of hyperactivated cells [101,136].(3) Dosing strategies: Fractional CAR-NK administration reduces the peak cytokine burst [138].(4) First-line: tocilizumab + corticosteroids for grade ≥ 2 CRS/ICANS [135].(5) Refractory cases: anakinra (an IL-1R antagonist) for ICANS; emapalumab (an IFN-γ blocker) for HLH-like syndromes [137].(6) Monitoring: Serum biomarkers (e.g., MCP-1, ANG2) predict neurotoxicity 24 h before symptom onset [133].

Addressing these challenges requires multidisciplinary innovation, including biomarker-driven antigen selection, TME-modulating combinational regimens, and cost-effective manufacturing platforms.Until these hurdles are overcome, the full potential of cellular therapies in AML will remain unrealized.


Future direction

The rapid evolution of CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies for AML presents transformative opportunities, yet several strategic innovations are critical to unlocking their full potential.Personalized therapeutic approaches, guided by multi-omics profiling (e.g., single-cell RNA sequencing, proteomics, and spatial transcriptomics), could refine patient stratification and identify novel, patient-specific antigen combinations to mitigate antigen escape [1,138,139].For instance, integrating artificial intelligence (AI) to predict optimal antigen pairs for dualtargeting CARs may enhance specificity while minimizing on-target/off-tumor toxicity [140][141][142].

Next-generation CAR designs, such as logic-gated "AND/OR" systems, could further improve precision.For example, CAR-T cells engineered to activate only when two AML-associated antigens (e.g., CLL-1 and CD123) are co-expressed may spare healthy hematopoietic cells [143].Additionally, CRISPR-Cas9-edited "armored" CAR-T cells expressing IL-15 or dominant-negative TGF-β receptors could counteract TME immunosuppression and enhance persistence [144,145].Similarly, inducible epigenetic reprogramming of CAR-NK cells to overexpress CXCR4 or chemokine receptors (e.g., CCR2) may improve homing to the bone marrow niche [61,146,147].

Combination therapies will likely dominate future clinical strategies.Pairing CAR-T/NK cells with smallmolecule inhibitors of immune checkpoints (e.g., TIM-3 or LILRB4) or metabolic modulators (e.g., IDO inhibitors) could disrupt the immunosuppressive TME [148,149].Emerging strategies should also target the microbiota-immune axis.Modulating intratumoral microbiota via antibiotics, probiotics, or phage therapy could reprogram immunosuppressive niches and sensitize AML to CAR-T/NK cells [16].Additionally, multi-omics approaches integrating microbial profiling with immune mapping may identify predictive biomarkers for therapy response.For instance, microbiota signatures associated with enhanced NK cell activity could guide patient selection for CAR-NK trials [17].

Preclinical studies suggest that bispecific CAR-NK cells secreting anti-PD-1 nanobodies may simultaneously target AML blasts and reinvigorate endogenous immunity [150].Furthermore, integrating CAR therapies with epigenetic drugs like azacitidine could sensitize LSCs to immune attack by upregulating antigen expression [147,151].

For CAR-NK cells, advancements in "off-the-shelf" platforms are imperative.Universal CAR-NK cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) with knockout of inhibitory receptors (e.g., NKG2A) or CRISPR-edited to express CD16 variants for enhanced ADCC could standardize manufacturing and improve scalability [152][153][154].Cryopreservation techniques optimized for NK cell viability may also facilitate global distribution [155].

Finally, addressing cost and accessibility requires innovation in decentralized, automated manufacturing platforms.Microfluidic systems for closed-loop CAR-T production or modular bioreactors for NK cell expansion could reduce costs and shorten vein-to-vein timelines [156,157].Collaborative frameworks between academia and industry, supported by regulatory incentives for pediatric AML therapies, will be essential to democratize access [158].


Conclusion

AML, characterized by its aggressive relapse and dismal survival rates, remains a formidable clinical challenge despite advances in conventional therapies.CAR-engineered T and NK cell therapies have emerged as groundbreaking immunotherapeutic modalities, offering targeted eradication of leukemic stem cells and resistant blasts.CAR-T cells, leveraging antigens such as CD123, CD33, and CLL-1, demonstrate promising clinical efficacy, with complete remission rates exceeding 60% in refractory/relapsed AML.Meanwhile, CAR-NK cells complement this approach through their inherent tumor surveillance, reduced toxicity, and "off-the-shelf" feasibility, achieving durable responses in early trials.Innovations such as dual-targeting CARs, cytokine-armored constructs, and CRISPR-edited universal cells have shown potential to overcome barriers like antigen escape, immunosuppressive microenvironments, and on-target/ off-tumor toxicity.Furthermore, robust toxicity management protocols-including cytokine-blocking biologics and biomarker-guided interventions-are essential for safe clinical translation.

However, challenges persist, including manufacturing scalability, cost constraints, and long-term safety concerns.The immunosuppressive bone marrow niche, enriched with MDSCs and inhibitory cytokines, continues to impede sustained efficacy.Future advancements hinge on interdisciplinary collaboration to integrate AI-driven antigen selection, biomarker-guided personalization, and combinatorial regimens with checkpoint inhibitors or metabolic modulators.Emerging "off-theshelf" platforms, including iPSC-derived CAR-NK cells and automated bioreactor systems, promise to democratize access and reduce production timelines.

As the field evolves, the synergy of next-generation CAR designs, microenvironment-targeting strategies, and cost-effective manufacturing will be pivotal in translating preclinical breakthroughs into life-saving therapies.By addressing these challenges, CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies hold transformative potential to redefine AML management, ushering in an era of precision medicine where durable remissions become attainable for even the most high-risk patients.
