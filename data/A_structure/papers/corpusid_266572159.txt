Introduction

Cancer stands as a formidable public health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of death in the United States.Breast cancer remains as the most common cancer type among women, contributing to nearly 30% of new cases each year [57].While various therapies have been introduced, mortality rates remain undesirably high, underscoring the need for more efficacious treatment options.To address this critical issue, our research involves the use of lapachol, a naphthoquinone compound initially isolated from the bark and heartwood of the Tabebuia avellanedae tree [48].Lapachol has garnered attention for its diverse biological activities, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, and leishmanicidal properties mainly for reactive oxygen species (ROS) [4,12,23,35].Over the years, numerous studies have explored lapachol's potential as an antitumor agent across a spectrum of cancer types ([4]; av; [52]).However, a significant limitation hampers its clinical applicability -its poor solubility.This limitation not only restricts its therapeutic potential but also raises concerns about bioavailability and the potential for adverse side effects [42,53].Natural compounds such as lapachol and its derivatives demonstrate diverse biological properties, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor effects, attributed largely to their capacity to generate reactive oxygen species.While lapachol has previously demonstrated anti-tumor activity, including a clinical study conducted in the 1970 s at the National Cancer Service Center, which achieved a 30% success rate [4,41,52], the study had to be terminated prematurely due to adverse effects such as anemia, prolonged prothrombin time, and issues related to drug bioavailability [42,53].To overcome this limitation, nanoemulsion systems offer an attractive strategy, encapsulating hydrophobic pay-loads, enhancing stability, improving pharmacokinetics, and enabling targeted delivery [9,55,67].Key advantages of nanoemulsions over other nanocarriers include ease of scaleup, kinetic stability, and payload capacity for solubilizing larger hydrophobic drug amounts [20,31].In this work, we developed a nanoemulsion designed to leverage key benefits for breast cancer therapy.Specifically, our nanoemulsion displays (1) suitable size under 200 nm to enable passive tumor targeting, (2) near 100% drug encapsulation efficiency to maximize lapachol payload delivery, (3) over 6 months kinetic stability for practical storage and use, and (4) enhanced cellular uptake versus free drug.

In addition, the development of novel lapachol derivatives with different lipophilicity and activity has been proposed [6].To bridge the gap between lapachol's therapeutic promise and its practical limitations, we have synthesized a novel lapachol derivative with an optimized hydro/lipophilic ratio.This alteration is intended to augment drug encapsulation and improve bioavailability compared to the parent compound.Collectively, these tailored design properties promise to circumvent lapachol's solubility limitations while improving its bioavailability, tumor delivery, and cytotoxic antitumor efficacy.We hypothesized the nanosystem could mitigate lapachol's solubility issues while augmenting delivery and antitumor efficacy.In our investigation, we perform a comprehensive characterization of the nanoemulsion, including assessments of mean diameter, zeta potential, polydispersity index, drug encapsulation, storage stability, and cellular uptake.Furthermore, we evaluated cell viability and systemic toxicity, assessing the effects on tumor cells and healthy mice, respectively.Finally, we assessed the in vivo antitumor activity of NE-F-LapA using a 4T1 breast cancer model.


Materials and methods


Chemicals and reagents

Ethoxylated sorbitan monooleate (SuperRefined ™ Polysorbate ™ 80; Tween 80 ™ ), soybean oil, glycerol, and lapachol were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany).RPMI 1640 Medium, fetal bovine serum, penicillin, streptomycin, and trypsin EDTA 0.25% were sourced from Gibco-Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, USA).Xylazine solution (Dopaser ® 2%) was acquired from Hertape Calier (Juatuba, Brazil), and Ketamine hydrochloride solution (Dopalen ® 10%) was supplied by Vetbrands Agroline (Campo Grande, Brazil).All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.The subcutaneous tumor model was established using 8-week-old female BALB/c mice procured from CEBIO-UFMG (Belo Horizonte, Brazil).All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (CEUA/UFMG) under protocol # 06/2018.


Synthesis of peracetylated lapachol L-fucoside

The fucoside of lapachol was obtained by the reaction with lapachol and the glycosyl halide from L-fucose under phase transfer catalysis (PTC) conditions: 10% w/v sodium carbonate aqueous solution, dichloromethane and tetra-n-butyl bromide (TBAB) as phase transfer catalyst, using the peracetylated fucosyl bromide as glycosyl halide [43,46].Lapachol (1.0 mmol) was solubilized in 5 mL of dichloromethane and was added 5 mL of sodium carbonate 10% w/v solution and tetrabuty-lammonium bromide (0.3 mmol).Then a solution of the fucosyl halide (3.0 mmol) in 5 mL of dichloromethane was added.The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, and monitored by TLC analysis.A 6 mol L −1 HCl (50 mL) solution was added until pH= 4 and the mixture was transferred to a separatory funnel.The organic layer was separated and the aqueous phase was extracted with 3 × 50 mL of dichloromethane.The combined organic layers were washed with water (3 ×25 mL), dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated under reduced pressure, the scheme synthesis of peracelylated lapachol L-fucoside was represented in Fig. 1.The crude product was purified by recrystallization from methanol.


Chemical characterization of F-LapA

The compound was characterized by IR NMR and ESI-MS spectroscopy, and spectra are presented in Supplementary section.The infrared spectra of peracetylated lapachol L-fucoside showed bands at 3030-2858 cm −1 (C-H alkane and aromatic), 1748 cm −1 (C=O, ester) and 1216 cm −1 (C-O, ester).The 1 H NMR of peracetylated lapachol L-fucoside showed signals at δ H 1.65-1.83ppm (methyl groups at C-14′ and C-15′ of the lapachol moiety), δ H 1.94-2.20 ppm (CH 3 C=O), δ H 3.30-3.44ppm (methylenic hydrogens at C-11′ of the lapachol), δ H 3.81-5.71ppm (pyranosidic protons), δ H 5.11-5.18ppm (olefinic hydrogens H-12′of the isoprenyl side chain) and 7.68-8.08ppm (aromatic hydrogens of lapachol).The signal of H-6 of the cL-fucose derivative is recorded at as a duplet at δ H 1.14 ppm (d,J=6.4 Hz;1 H,.The lapachol fucoside correspond to β anomer as confirmed by 1 H NMR spectrometry, wherein one observes signals at δ H 5.70, corresponding at H-1 (anomeric hydrogen), with J-coupling values around 8.0 Hz [29].These structural features were confirmed by the 13 C NMR spectra of this compound, which showed signals at δ C 15.6 ppm (CH 3 , C-6) δ C 18.0 ppm (CH 3 , C-14′), δ C 20.6-20.9ppm (CH 3 , ester), δ C 23.5 ppm (CH 2 , C-11′), δ C 25.9 ppm (CH 3 , C-15′), δ C 69.3-71.1 ppm (CH, C-2 to C-5), δ C 99.6 ppm (CH, C-1′), δ C 119.6 ppm (CH,C-12′),δ C 126.1-133.9ppm (CH, C-5′ at C-10′), δ C 137.9 ppm (CH,C-3′), δ C 152.9 ppm (CH,C-2′), δ C 169.8-170.6 ppm (C=O, ester) and, δ C 181.1-185.1 ppm (C=O, quinone).


Partition coefficient

For partition coefficient determination, 10 mg of F-LapA or Lap was added to tubes containing 6.0 mL of n-octanol/water (1:1).Samples were vigorously shaken for 30 min and then allowed to stand for 120 min for phase separation.Subsequently, 0.2 mL aliquots from each phase were collected and transferred to 10 mL volumetric flasks.The volume was adjusted with mobile phase (80:20; methanol: 5% acetic acid (v/v)) and quantified by HPLC, as described in Section 2.7.


Nanoemulsion preparation

Oil-in-water (O/W) nanoemulsions (NE) were prepared using the high-energy method.The oily phase (Soybean oil, 400 mg (4% w/v); Polysorbate 80, 115 mg (1.5% w/v)) and the aqueous phase (Glycerol, 224 mg (2.24% w/v); ultrapure water, 10 mL) were heated separately to 80 °C.With the temperature maintained at 80 °C, the aqueous phase was gently added dropwise onto the oily phase under constant stirring at 8000 rpm using an Ultra Turrax T-25 homogenizer (Ika Labortechnik, Germany).The resulting emulsion was immediately sonicated at high intensity at 21% amplitude for 10 min (CPX 500 model, Cole-Palmer Instruments, USA) [33].After cooling to room temperature with manual stirring, the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with ultrapure water.The pH of NE was adjusted to 7.0 with 0.1 M NaOH.NEs were stored at 4 °C.For NE-F-LapA, the drug was added to the oily phase at different concentrations (1.0 and 1.5 mg/mL, 0.2% and 0.3% w/v, respectively) prior to preparation.All prepared formulations were stored at 4 °C, and protected from light.


Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), Zeta potential, and morphological analysis

Mean particle size, polydispersivity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK) [62].Samples were diluted 100 times in ultrapure water.Nanoparticle tracking analysis was performed on samples diluted in ultrapure water (same as formulation) using a Nanosight NS300 (Malvern Instruments, England).Measurements were taken at room temperature for 60 s with automatic detection [45].

The nanoemulsion morphology was evaluated by transmission electron cryo-microscopy (cryo-TEM) (Tecnai G2-12 -FEI SpiritBiotwin 120 kV), the microscopy was performed at the Microscopy Center of the Federal University of Minas Gerais.It was used the plunge freezing technique to prepare the samples, they were spread into a thin film across an EM grid and then rapidly submerged in liquid ethane [41].The nanoparticles' mean diameter was measured using Image J software by analyzing 100 droplets [30].


Encapsulation efficiency (EE)

EE of NE-F-LapA was determined by purifying NE-F-LapA using 0.45 μm filtration, allowing encapsulated drug to pass while precipitating non-encapsulated drug get stuck in the filter.Samples of total and filtered F-LapA were quantified by HPLC (Agilent 1260) using a C18 column with UV detection at 278 nm [41].EE was calculated as:
%EE= filtrated F−LapA × 100 Total F−LapA

Storage stability

The stability of NE-F-LapA (1.0 and 1.5 mg/mL) stored at 4 °C was assessed by measuring mean diameter, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency at 3, 7, 15,30,60,90,120, and 180 days post-preparation, as described above [33].


Polarized light microscopy (PLM)

The presence of F-LapA crystals in the nanoemulsion was assessed using an optical microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager.M2, Carl Zeiss, Germany) coupled with polarized light and equipped with an AxioCam digital camera (Model ERc 5 S, Carl Zeiss, Germany).The samples were prepared on microscope slides (undiluted).The detection of F-LapA crystals indicates the presence of non-encapsulated F-LapA and, therefore, a lack of stability [41].


Colloidal stability

Dilution stability of NE-F-LapA was investigated in different biological fluids to predict the in vivo behavior of the drug delivery system.NE-F-LapA was diluted 4-fold in NaCl (0.9% w/v), PBS buffer (pH 7.4), Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) or mouse plasma.

Samples were kept at 37°C under agitation (150 RPM), for 24 h [39].At different time points, aliquots were collected and the mean diameter was measured by DLS.


In vitro drug release

The release of F-LapA from NE was performed by the dilution method [1].One milliliter of NE-F-LapA was incubated with 10 mL of PBS + 10% of PEG400 + Tween 80 at 2%, at 37 °C, for 24 h, under magnetic stirring at 150 rpm.This solution was used to guarantee sink condition for F-LapA.At 0, 60, 120, 240, 360, 480, and 1440 min, aliquots were withdrawn, filtered in Amicon centrifugal devices (10 kDa Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and F-LapA concentration was analyzed by HPLC.The dilution ratio of 1:10 was selected to mimic the final dilution after intravenous injection for subsequent animal assays [45].


Cell culture

Murine breast cancer cell line 4T1 (ATCC ® CRL-2539 ™ ) and human MDA-MB-231 (ATCC ® HTB26 ™ ) were cultured in RPMI 1640 and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM), respectively, supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 IU/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL).Non-tumoral human fibroblasts (NTHF) from primary gingival tissue culture were grown in DMEM with the same supplements and kindly supplied by Prof. Cláudia Maria Oliveira Simões (Laboratory of Virology, Universidade Federal de Santa Catarina, Florianópolis, Brazil) according to the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal de Santa Catarina, protocol number 021/2009.Cells were maintained in a 5% CO 2 atmosphere at 37 °C.Cells were grown to confluence and harvested by trypsinization.


Cell viability

MDA-MB-231, NTHF, and 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 10^4 cells/well and 5 × 10^3 cells/well, respectively) 24 h prior to treatment.Cells were then exposed to a range of concentrations of buffered solutions of F-LapA, NE-F-LapA (80-0.16μmol), and blank NE for 48 h.Cell viability was assessed using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay as previously described [66].Briefly, after incubation, 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to each well to fix cells for one hour.Plates were then washed with water to remove TCA and stained with SRB for 30 min.Afterward, the plate was washed with 1% acetic acid to remove unbound SRB.Then, the protein-bound dye was solubilized in a 10 mM Tris-Base solution, and optical densities (OD) were read at 510 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer Spectra Max Plus 384 (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).


Cellular uptake

The cellular uptake of F-LapA from NE-F-LapA and free F-LapA was measured by HPLC.4T1 tumor cells were plated in 12-well plates (5 × 10^5 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h.Then, the cells were treated (n = 3) with 1 mL of fresh RPMI 1640 containing 25 mM of either F-LapA or NE-F-LapA and incubated again for 1 and 4 h.At each time point, the plates were washed to remove the treatments, and cells were harvested using trypsin.

The resulting cell suspensions were washed twice with PBS by centrifugation (2000 rpm, 5 min).The cell pellets were then resuspended in 1 mL of methanol:isopropyl alcohol (1:1) solution and transferred to an ultrasonic bath to promote cell lysis and F-LapA extraction.Finally, the suspension was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was used to determine F-LapA concentration by HPLC analysis as mentioned in Section 2.7.The F-LapA cellular uptake was calculated using the equation [44].

cellular uptake (%) = ([F-LapA] extracted from cells)/[F-LapA] total × 100


Acute systemic toxicity

A single dose of F-LapA or NE-F-LapA (5, 10, or 20 mg/kg) was administered intravenously to healthy female Balb/c mice (n = 6).The control group received a single saline injection.Over 14 days post-treatment, the animals were observed for behavioral/ clinical changes, body weight, morbidity, and mortality (OECD Guideline for testing of Chemicals, 2001).After 14 days, the mice were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg), and blood was collected via brachial plexus puncture into tubes containing anti-coagulant (EDTA).Hematological parameters including hemoglobin, red blood cells (RBC), total white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets were evaluated for each group.The parameters were measured automatically using the Hemovet 2300 (São Paulo, Brazil) [58].

For biochemical analysis, blood was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 15 min), and the obtained plasma was frozen at −70 °C.Analysis was performed using a Bioplus BIO-2000 semiautomatic analyzer (São Paulo, Brazil) with commercial kits (Labtest, Lagoa Santa, Brazil).Renal function was evaluated by measuring urea and creatinine; liver function by determining alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate amino-transferase (AST) activity [58].Coagulation was assessed by measuring prothrombin time (PT) and partial thromboplastin time (PTT).


Antitumor activity

Aliquots of 1.0 × 10 6 4T1 cells in RPMI medium (0.1 mL) were injected subcutaneously (SC) into the right flank of female BALB/c mice.Mice were kept in an area with light control, with free access to water and food.Tumor cells were allowed to grow in vivo for 7 days, once the tumor volume reached about 100 mm 3 [14].On the 7th day after 4T1 cell inoculation, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 7) as follows: group 1 received PBS + 10% of PEG400 + Tween 80 at 2% as the negative control; group 2 received buffered solution of F-LapA and group 3 received NE-F-LapA.For all treatments, the F-LapA dose was 10 mg/kg, administered in 5 doses every 2 days by tail vein injection.Throughout the study, tumors were measured with a caliper every 2 days.Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula:
V = (d1) 2 × d2 × 0 ⋅ 5
Where d1 and d2, represent the smaller and larger diameter, respectively [34].

At the end of the experimental period (D10), the relative tumor volume (RTV), and the tumor growth inhibition ratio (IR) were determined by the formulas: At the end of the experiment, the animals were euthanized, and blood was collected for toxicity evaluation [34].Toxicity studies were conducted through biochemical analyses as mentioned in Section 2.15.


Histological analysis

Liver, lung and tumor tissues were collected for histopathological analysis.Samples were fixed in 10% formalin for 48 h, dehydrated in alcohol and embedded in paraffin blocks.

Sections of 4 μm were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) [45].Slides were evaluated by a trained pathologist and images were captured using a camera connected to an optical microscope (Olympus BX-40; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).


Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD.Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM, version 6.00 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).Differences between the experimental groups were tested by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Tukey test, or Student's t-test when two groups were compared.All data showed normal distribution and homoscedasticity.P values < 0.05.were considered statistically significant.


Results


Partition coefficient

The partition coefficient for F-LapA was found to be 1.84 ± 0.93, demonstrating a successful alteration in the hydro/lipophilic balance compared to Lapachol, which has a Log P of 2.8 [63].The addition of fucose shifted the partition coefficient towards a less lipophilic value, which could enhance the biological properties of this derivative.[22,54,67].The cryo-TEM (Fig. 2) showed droplets with circular morphology and uniform sizes, with the majority of the globules near 170 nm, similar to the findings obtained by NTA and DLS.


Characterization


Storage stability

Storage stability at 4°C for up to 180 days was evaluated by measuring mean diameter, polydispersity index, and zeta potential through DLS and entrapment efficiency by HPLC (Fig. 3).No significant differences in size, PdI, and zeta potential were observed in either formulation concentration throughout the experiment.However, for the nanoemulsion at 1.5 mg/kg, encapsulation remained stable for only 90 days, decreasing to 84.2% and 81.1% at days 120 and 180, respectively.Encapsulation stability was confirmed by polarized microscopy, as illustrated in Fig. 3E-F.Crystals were observed in NE-F-LAPA (1.5 mg/mL) at day 120 (Fig. 3F), whereas no crystals were found in NE-F-LAPA (1.0 mg/mL) during the same time frame (Fig. 3E).


Colloidal stability

Colloidal stability was assessed by measuring the mean diameter and PdI in the presence of different media (Fig. 4).All compounds remained stable in the tested media.Although NE-F-LapA showed a slight increase in size and PdI in the presence of plasma after 24 h, both parameters remained within acceptable ranges for intravenous administration [5,8,11].


Cell viability

IC50 and selectivity index (SI) calculations were performed for two breast cancer cell lines (4T1 and MDA-MB-231) and a normal control cell line (NTHF).The results revealed a significant decrease (p < 0.0029) in IC 50 values for cancer cells when the drug was encapsulated in the nanoemulsion.However, no differences were observed in the IC 50 of healthy cells between the free and encapsulated drugs.Additionally, the selectivity index showed a substantial enhancement for nanoemulsions, indicating specificity for the nanosystem toward cancer cells..


Cellular uptake

The results of cellular uptake are presented in Fig. 5A.Similar uptake was observed at 1 h, but there was a substantial increase in the nanoemulsion group at 4 h, with 5% uptake compared to 1% in the NE-F-LAPA and F-LAPA groups, respectively.


Drug Release

The release profiles of diluted formulations are shown in Fig. 5B.A rapid release was observed in the first few minutes of dilution (up to 60 min), likely due to the release of the drug present on the surface of the nanoemulsion.After 120 min, a sustained drug release was observed, with approximately 50% of the drug remaining in the nanoemulsion after 24 h.


Toxicity evaluation

Body weight (Fig. 6), hematological (Table 3), and biochemical changes (Table 4) were evaluated.No significant changes in body weight were observed among the investigated groups, indicating an overall safe profile of the formulation.

Similarly, no significant hematological alterations were observed in the tested groups, even in the highest dose group (20 mg/kg).Although statistical differences were observed in thrombocytes for some groups, no clinical signs were noticed in the animals, and all values remained within the reference range.

The same pattern was observed for biochemical parameters, with no significant differences among the groups.Importantly, at the highest doses of NE-LAP, no alterations in coagulation parameters were found, which is an advantage over reports of changes in coagulation patterns after lapachol administration [42,53].


Antitumor activity

Antitumor activity was evaluated in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, and the results are presented in Fig. 7A.The encapsulated drug significantly slowed tumor growth compared to the free drug.Both control groups (saline and diluent) demonstrated the same pattern of tumor growth behavior and are presented in a single curve in Fig. 7A.

Relative tumor volume (RTV) drastically diminished in animals administered with nanoemulsions, with a decrease of up to 50% compared to the control and ~40% compared to the free drug.Additionally, there was a substantial increase in tumor inhibition ratio (IR) for the encapsulated F-LapA group, indicating the nanoemulsion's ability to improve anti-tumor efficacy. .Toxicity was also evaluated by monitoring bodyweight of the mice throughout the study.No differences were observed indicating the safety of the treatment regimen tested (Fig. 7B).Moreover, biochemical analyses did not present any alteration in the treated groups (Table 6).


Metastasis analysis

Breast cancer metastasizes to the liver and lungs.We evaluated the impact of free versus NE-F-LapA treatment on reducing metastatic load.While all groups showed multifocal metastasis in the liver with a similar number of foci (Fig. 8A-C), there was a substantial difference observed in the lungs among the groups, indicating the treatment's capacity to control the spread of tumor cells to this organ (Fig. 8D-F).Fig. 8G represents the number of metastases found in each group, indicating a reduction of ~5-fold compared to the control group and a 3-fold reduction compared to the free drug group.


Discussion

Nanoemulsions, characterized by droplet sizes ranging from 10 to 1000 nm, represent versatile systems primarily composed of oil, water, and a stabilizing surfactant [20,41,67].

Despite their inherent thermodynamic instability, these nanoemulsions exhibit kinetic stability and have found extensive applications in diverse fields such as pharmaceuticals, food, and cosmetics [3,26,59].Herein, we developed a novel nanoemulsion containing a fucoside derivative of lapachol, positioning it as a potential candidate for breast tumor therapy.This formulation presented a highly suitable diameter for intravenous administration, a zeta potential indicative of stability, and an excellent encapsulation efficiency.These results underscore the capacity of this nanoemulsion to efficiently entrap hydrophobic compounds.Nanoemulsions represent an important strategy to overcome poor water solubility of promising natural products, enabling enhanced bioavailability and retained biological function [9,27,55].Their capacity to incorporate hydrophobic compounds in a kinetically stable nano-scale emulsion can help unlock the potential of compounds otherwise hampered by delivery challenges.

We investigated the stability of our nanoconstruct in relevant biological fluids.The choice of Tween 80 as the surfactant in our formulation aligns with a well-established trend in the field of nanoemulsions for delivering poorly water-soluble drugs [20,31,55,67].Non-ionic surfactants like Tween 80 are favored in biological applications due to their propensity for inducing fewer adverse effects in comparison to cationic or anionic surfactants [50,60].Notably, our formulations exhibited a good stability profile, extending up to four months at higher concentrations and six months at 1.0 mg/mL.While we observed a minor increase in PdI and size in plasma after 24 h, these changes held limited significance as they remained within acceptable thresholds for intravenous administration.Surface properties are critical in determining nanoparticle stability and interactions within biological systems.

As evidenced by past research [36,67], the non-ionic surfactant Tween 80 is pivotal in minimizing electrostatic interactions between nanoparticles, proteins, and cells [36,67].By mitigating these attractive forces, Tween 80 reduces undesirable nanoparticle aggregation and biofouling, thereby enhancing stability and biocompatibility.Careful optimization of the nanoparticle surface using surfactants like Tween 80 is therefore essential to control behavior in complex biological environments.

A major roadblock in lapachol's clinical advancement has been problems with bioavailability after oral administration.Poor water solubility, metabolism by liver enzymes, and rapid systemic elimination are factors that likely reduce lapachol's plasma levels when given orally and have hampered clinical studies [4,42,53].While the precise oral bioavailability percentage in humans or animal models is not well quantified, these factors are thought to significantly diminish therapeutic exposure after oral dosing.Nanoemulsions are uniquely poised to enhance delivery of poorly water-soluble drugs compared to other nanocarriers, owing to high kinetic stability, payload capacity to solubilize larger hydrophobic drug amounts, and ability to increase circulation time while protecting drug cargo from degradation [9,27,41,55].Our nanoemulsion may help overcome these barriers in two key ways: 1) Enabling intravenous injection avoids first-pass liver metabolism and direct gut wall clearance, increasing plasma levels.2) Encapsulation into nanoparticles shields compounds from rapid metabolism/elimination during circulation [41,59].Applying these strategies allowed us to demonstrate higher circulating plasma levels of lapachol nanoemulsions versus free oral drug, increasing bioavailability and tumor localization [41].Building on this prior work, our current nanoemulsion integrates a novel lapachol derivative designed to further improve encapsulation and therapeutic index.We tailored composition and preparation parameters to achieve physicochemical stability exceeding our prior nanoemulsion, suggesting even better bioavailability potential [41].

We hypothesized that by retaining lapachol's capacity for reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, our nanoemulsion could optimize its selective antitumor cytotoxicity [19,56,69].We rationalized that encapsulating the lapachol derivative in a nanoemulsion could improve therapeutic activity against breast cancer through several key mechanisms assisting delivery.The ~200 nm particle size enables passive targeting of nanoemulsion to tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention effect.Excessive angiogenesis during tumor growth produces irregular vascular openings that allow preferential accumulation of nano-scale agents [13].The tween-80 surfactant facilitates nanoemulsion entry into cancer cells.Naphthoquinones like lapachol exert antineoplastic effects through both DNA/protein damage mediated by ROS and inhibition of topoisomerases required by cancer cells [7,18,32,49,51,56,61,65].Once internalized, retention of the redox-cycling quinone lapachol derivative leads to heightened oxidative stress selectively in malignant cells already burdened with reactive oxygen species [21].This tips them beyond sustainable threshold, triggering cell death.Sustained release of the compound from nanoemulsion ensures successive rounds of redox damage.Additionally, our lapachol derivative inhibits topoisomerase enzymes vital for regulating DNA supercoiling essential for tumor proliferation.Collectively, these interrelated mechanisms of enhanced selective delivery, subcellular retention of redox-cycling drug, elevated and longstanding oxidative stress, and concurrent enzyme inhibition empower our nanoemulsion for combating breast malignancy.

Subsequent cell viability assays tested whether our tailored nanoemulsion could enhance these cytotoxic mechanisms.Da Silva et al.,($year$) [7,28,32,40,49,51,68,69].

In fact, our nanoemulsion exhibited reduced IC 50 values-possibly attributable to enhanced drug delivery into cells.Calculation of the Selectivity Index (SI) further emphasized the nanoemulsion's potential as an antitumor agent (SI = 11.34)since values above 3 indicating promise and above 10 warranting more extensive antitumor studies [25].In summary, our results highlight the nanoemulsion's ability to leverage lapachol's antitumor activity while improving its delivery and therapeutic index.

Another key advantage of our nanoemulsion was its ability to sub-stantially improve cellular uptake of the drug by breast cancer cells.After just 4 h, the nanoemulsion enabled 5% drug uptake by the cancer cells, a major enhancement compared to only 1% uptake of the free drug.This demonstrates the nanoemulsion's effectiveness at facilitating drug entry into target cells.Past research has shown how nanosystems like ours can increase uptake through endocytosis, allowing more drug payload to reach cancer cells [17,67].

In addition, our release studies showed some drug remained in the oil phase even after a 10-fold dilution.This likely stems from an affinity between the drug and nanoemulsion components.The drug F-LapA has slight hydrophilicity, resulting in release patterns tied to dilution -similar to expected behavior when administered in vivo.Our toxicity studies showed no concerning effects on monitored parameters at varying intravenous doses.This suggests an absence of treatment-related toxicity, unlike previous clinical trials of lapachol that were halted due to side effects like anemia, nausea, vomiting, and prolonged clotting times [4,16,24,37].Of note, lapachol's structural similarity to vitamin K raised concerns about impacting coagulation and causing anemia [4,42].However, we saw no indications of such toxicity, likely due to our lower dosing compared to earlier studies (over 2 g/day).

Critically, we demonstrated our nanoemulsion's efficacy in inhibiting tumor growth in vivo.Lapachol derivatives have shown antitumor effects in breast and other cancer models [41,47,52] providing a basis for our study.Our nanoemulsion treatment significantly enhanced the inhibition rate compared to the free drug, aligning with past nanoemulsion research [10,59,64].This increased efficacy likely stems from improved cellular uptake, selective entrapment, and higher drug levels at the tumor site.Moreover, lapachol has exhibited potential to reduce metastasis in other models [2,15,38], which we also observed through decreased lung metastases in this study.

In summary, this proof-of-concept study provides the first evidence that formulation into a tailor-made nanoemulsion can optimize delivery and improve the therapeutic index of a novel lapachol derivative.Our data lays the groundwork for further optimization and toxicology to enable clinical translation, bringing us closer to realizing lapachol's long-held but unfulfilled promise for breast cancer treatment.


Conclusion

In this study, we successfully prepared and characterized a nanoemulsion incorporating a glycosylated lapachol derivative for intravenous delivery.Importantly, we demonstrate that the tailored properties of our nanoemulsion improved lapachol's solubility, bioavailability, tumor delivery, and cytotoxic efficacy for breast cancer treatment.Specifically, our nanoemulsion displayed a suitable size (under 200 nm) for passive tumor targeting via the EPR effect, excellent drug encapsulation efficiency (nearly 100%) to maximize lapachol payload, kinetic stability exceeding 6 months for practical storage and use, and capacity to enhance cellular uptake while reducing IC50 versus free drug.Our research also translated to positive outcomes in vivo, with improved tumor growth control and reduced lung metastasis in a murine breast cancer model.Overall, our findings reveal the nanoemulsion's promise as a viable, potent tool for breast cancer research and therapy.Proposal synthesis of peracetylated lapachol L-fucoside from lapachol.Peracetylated lapachol L-fucoside: yellow powder.Yield: 80%; mp 93.6 -95.1 °C.Body weight variation after intravenous administration of NaCl 0,9% (w/v); F-LapA (5; 10 and 20 mg/kg) and NE-F-LapA (5, 10 and 20 mg/kg).Table 1 Characterization of NE-F-LapA: Mean diameter, particle concentration, zeta potential, PdI, and entrapment efficiency (%EE) at two different concentrations (1.0 and 1.5 mg/mL) of F-LapA.
