A B S T R A C TThe sensitive detection of viruses is key to preventing the spread of infectious diseases. In this study, we develop a silica-encapsulated Au core-satellite (CS@SiO 2 ) nanotag, which produces a strong and reproducible surfaceenhanced Raman scattering (SERS) signal. The combination of SERS from the CS@SiO 2 nanotags with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) achieves a highly sensitive detection of SARS-CoV-2. The CS@SiO 2 nanotag is constructed by assembling 32 nm Au nanoparticles (AuNPs) on a 75 nm AuNP. Then the core-satellite particles are encapsulated with SiO 2 for facile surface modification and stability. The SERS-ELISA technique using the CS@SiO 2 nanotags provides a great sensitivity, yielding a detection limit of 8.81 PFU mL-1, which is 10 times better than conventional ELISA and 100 times better than lateral flow assay strip method. SERS-ELISA is applied to 30 SARS-CoV-2 clinical samples and achieved 100% and 55% sensitivities for 15 and 9 positive samples with cycle thresholds < 30 and > 30, respectively. This new CS@SiO 2 -SERS-ELISA method is an innovative technique that can significantly reduce the false-negative diagnostic rate for SARS-CoV-2 and thereby contribute to overcoming the current pandemic crisis.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease  pandemic, caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), is still ongoing worldwide. Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing of nasopharyngeal swabs or saliva specimens is the gold standard method for SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis [1][2][3]. However, this method requires approximately 3-4 h from the pre-treatment stage to the occurrence of PCR detection [4][5][6]. For more rapid detection, the lateral flow assay (LFA), an immunodiagnostic method that measures the SARS-CoV-2 antigen, has been developed. Although it reduces the detection time to < 15 min [7][8][9], its diagnostic accuracy drops to 50% for initial infections with a low virus concentration [10,11]. The limit of detection (LoD) of a commercial LFA strip for SARS-CoV-2 is estimated to be approximately 300-500 plaque-forming units (PFU) mL-1 [12][13][14]. This high LoD level makes it difficult to identify early-stage infections in people. Another immunodiagnostic method, i.e., enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), has a better LoD than LFA strips [15][16][17]. However, it does not have sufficient sensitivity to reduce the false-negative diagnosis rate in early infection. Therefore, developing a new immunoassay technique with high sensitivity that allows to detect low concentrations of SARS-CoV-2 is a significant advance in the diagnosis of infection in an early stage and preventing the spread of the virus.

Conventional ELISA monitors a change in absorbance that depends on the target concentration as enzyme-labeled antibodies are added to the immunocomplexes on the well. The absorption-based detection method inevitably results in insufficient sensitivity to diagnose earlystage infections. Alternatively, fluorescence emission was used to improve the detection sensitivity. Nevertheless, according to recent reports, the diagnostic sensitivity of this method is still insufficient to significantly reduce the false-negative diagnostic rate [18,19].

An effort to implement highly sensitive surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) to ELISA has been made to overcome the inherent sensitivity limitations of absorption and fluorescence ELISA [20][21][22][23]. In SERS-ELISA, SERS nanotags with Raman reporter molecules attached to the surface of metal nanoparticles (NPs) are used as detection labels instead of the enzyme or fluorescent labels that are used in conventional ELISAs. When SERS nanotags are exposed to a laser beam, plasmon excitation of NPs creates electromagnetic fields, which increase Raman scattering signal of the reporter molecules. The electromagnetic fields are particularly intensified in nanogaps between NPs or at sharp curvatures of NP surfaces, which thereafter are called hot spots. Using this phenomenon, the inherent problem of low sensitivity of conventional absorption and fluorescence ELISA could be solved [24][25][26][27]. In this respect, developing functional SERS nanotags with controllable hot spots is critical to achieving high sensitivity and reproducibility in SERS-ELISA [27][28][29]. Au nanoparticles (AuNPs) have been extensively used in SERS-ELISA. However, monomeric AuNPs yield very low Raman enhancement because of a lack of hot spots [30][31][32][33]. Previously, assays on Au-coated substrates have been explored, where hot spots could be created between AuNPs and the Au-coated substrate [21,34,35]. Recently, 96-well plates coated with Au were commercialized for nano-plasmonic bioassays. However, such Au-coated well plates are expensive and cannot accurately control the hot spots during the assay process. Therefore, a more effective method to generate hot spots, preferably within an individual particle unit, is highly desired for SERS-ELISA. Then, SERS nanotags can act as independent optical probes that do not require Au substrates, much like enzyme substrates or fluorescence dyes used in absorbance or fluorescence ELISAs [36][37][38][39].

In this study, we develop core-satellite (CS) SERS nanotags, in which multiple 32 nm AuNPs are assembled on the surface of 75 nm AuNPs. Nanogaps are formed between the core AuNP and satellite AuNPs to enable creation of hot spots within the individual CS NP [40]. Moreover, the surface of the CS NPs is encapsulated with silica (CS@SiO 2 ) to improve the stability of the CS NPs and facilitate the surface modification for anchoring antibodies [41][42][43][44][45][46]. We evaluate the plasmonic coupling characteristics and stability of the CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags by performing a SERS-ELISA on a prepared SARS-CoV-2 lysate. This newly developed SERS-ELISA platform outperforms the conventional lateral flow assay (LFA) strip or ELISA assay method based on UV/Vis absorption/fluorescence detection with a much better LoD. We also reduce the assay cost by implementing the SERS assay technology to a 384-well plate instead of the conventional 96-well-plate assay container. Finally, we conduct clinical trials using 30 real SARS-CoV-2 clinical samples (24 positives and 6 negatives) to evaluate the clinical efficacy of our SERS-ELISA platform. The most critical issue in performing immunoassay using the SERS-ELISA platform is the development of a SERS nanotag that can generate sensitive and reproducible hot spots. In this study, we developed a new SERS nanotag (CS@SiO 2 ) that can satisfy these requirements, and immunoassays were performed on SARS-CoV-2 lysates and clinical samples to verify their clinical validity. The assay results are compared with those of the RT-PCR and LFA assays. The proposed SERS-ELISA platform using CS@SiO 2 nanotags could significantly improve the false-negative rate of SARS-CoV-2 detection compared to the existing immunoassay methods.


Experimental section


Reagent and materials

Trisodium citrate dihydrate (≥99.0%), gold (III) chloride trihydrate, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid (35.0-37.0%), RBS 35 solution, methanol (≥99.8%), 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTMS, 97.0%), 1,4-benzenedimethanethiol (BDMT, 98.0%), (11-mercaptoundecyl)-N, N,N-trimethylammonium bromide (97%), sodium borohydride, cobalt (II) chloride hexahydrate, benzene thiol (BT), thiol-polyethylene glycolcarboxyl (i.e., HS-PEG3500-COOH with a molecular weight of ~3500), ethyl alcohol (≥99.5%), ammonium hydroxide solution (28-30%), tetraethyl orthosilicate, sodium chloride (NaCl), (3-mercaptopropyl) trimethoxysilane (95%, MPTMS), 4-(N-maleimidomethyl) cyclohexane-1carboxylic acid 3-sulfo-N-hydroxysuccinimide ester sodium salt (sulfo-SMCC), bovine serum albumin (BSA), glutaraldehyde solution (50%, GA), 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (≥98%, APTES), polyoxyethylene sorbitan monolaurate (Tween 20), and 3,3 ′ ,5,5 ′ -tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (10 ×, pH 7.4) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). TMB stop buffer was purchased from GenDEPOT (Katy, TX, USA). Mouse anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein monoclonal antibody clones 0659 (capture antibody) and 0658 (detection antibody) were purchased from Creative Diagnostics (Shirley, NY, USA). SARS-CoV-2 lysate was provided by the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology. All chemicals were used without further purification. Ultrapure water (high-performance liquid chromatography grade, J. T. Baker and Milli-Q water purification system) was used in all aqueous solutions. Commercial LFA strips (STANDARD™ Q COVID-19 Ag test) were purchased from SD BIOSENSOR (Suwon, South Korea). The LFA strip is the most commonly used antigen diagnostic kit for COVID-19 in South Korea.


Instrumentation

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were captured using JEM-1400 TEM (JEOL Ltd., Japan). UV/Vis absorption spectra were measured using Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) and Lambda 25 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA). DLS data were obtained using Nano ZS90 apparatus (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Raman spectra was measured using a Renishaw inVia Raman microscope system (Renishaw, New Mills, UK) with a 20 × (NA 0.4) objective lens. A He-Ne laser operating at λ = 632.8 nm was used as the excitation source with a laser power of approximately 18 mW. The Raman spectra were collected using a charge-coupled device camera at a spectral resolution of 1 cm − 1 . All spectra were calibrated to the Raman peak of a silicon wafer (520 cm − 1 ). Raman mapping images were measured with the same Raman microscope system using a 5 × (NA 0.12) objective lens.


Synthesis of CS@SiO 2 , AuNP@SiO 2, and HAuNP@SiO 2

Citrate-stabilized AuNPs with a diameter of 75 nm (32 nm) were synthesized via the seed growth method proposed by Bastus et al. [45]. The CS@SiO 2 NPs were prepared using a previous method reported by our group [42]. The 32 nm AuNPs were assembled on a 75 nm AuNP through Au-S bonds of the Raman reporter BDMT. The 58 nm hollow gold nanoparticles (HAuNPs) were prepared via the method that was previously reported by Liang et al. and Schwartzberg et al. [47,48].

Then, 1 μL of 10 − 3 M BT, which is a Raman reporter, was added to 1 mL of 40 pM AuNPs and HAuNPs. Silica encapsulation was performed using the modified Stöber method to stabilize the three different AuNPs [44]. This reaction was performed under mild stirring at 25 • C for 18 h. Finally, the produced AuNPs@SiO 2 was washed twice by centrifugation (4500 rpm, 20 min).


Conjugation of detection antibodies onto CS@SiO 2 , HAuNP@SiO 2, and AuNP@SiO 2 for SARS-CoV-2 assays

As previously reported [42], for the antibody conjugation onto CS@SiO 2 , AuNP@SiO 2 , and HAuNP@SiO 2 , 2 μL of 40 mM MPTMS was reacted with 1 mL of each silica-encapsulated AuNPs for 2 h and then centrifuged (3000 rpm, 20 min) twice. Then, 2 μL of 5 μM sulfo-SMCC linker was added and reacted for 20 min. Next, the solution was reacted overnight with 1 μL of 3.1 mg/mL detection antibodies for nucleocapsid proteins. Finally, 10 μL of 2% BSA was added and reacted for 30 min. Finally, the antibody-conjugated CS@SiO 2 , AuNP@SiO 2 , and HAuNP@SiO 2 SERS nanotags were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) and redispersed in 1 mL of PBS buffer.


SERS-ELISA assays of SARS-CoV-2 on a 384-well glass plate

The capture antibodies for SARS-CoV-2 were immobilized on glass substrates of a 384-well plate using the following procedure. First, the substrate was modified with 25 μL of 5% APTES for 30 min to change the hydrophilic nature of the surface; then, it was washed three times with 100 μL of distilled water (DW). Subsequently, 25 μL of 5% GA was added for 30 min to activate the surface with aldehyde groups, and then washed three times with 100 μL of DW again. Here, GA was used to covalently immobilize the capture antibodies onto the hydrophilic substrates. Then, 25 μL of 2 μg/mL capture antibodies were added onto the aldehyde-activated substrate and reacted for 1 h. Next, it was washed three times with 100 μL of PBST solution (i.e., 1 × PBS buffer with 0.02% Tween 20). Next, 50 μL of 2% BSA was added and incubated for 1 h to prevent nonspecific bindings. Thirty SARS-CoV-2 clinical samples (24 positives and 6 negatives) were provided with RT-PCR data from the Gyeongsang National University College of Medicine (IRB approval number: 2020-10-002) and Yonsei University Health Service Center, Severance Hospital (IRB approval number: 4-2020-0465). Viral lysates of clinical samples were prepared in the same manner as that provided by NCCP. SARS-CoV-2 viral lysates for clinical samples were prepared at KCDC-approved biosafety facilities at Gyeongsang National University and Yonsei University Hospitals. Fig. 1 shows a schematic illustration of the SERS-ELISA method using CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags. Instead of a 96-well plastic plate (Fig. 1a), a 384-well glass plate (Fig. 1b) was used as the assay template to reduce the required amount of the SARS-CoV-2 clinical sample. As a result, the volume of one well was reduced by 1/3 or less from 400 μL to 130 μL, thereby significantly reducing the amount of expensive capture and detection antibodies. It is also noteworthy that the SERS-ELISA could be more straightforward in the signal measurement process than absorption and fluorescence ELISAs because the SERS signals can be directly measured from the SERS nanotags (Fig. 1c). In contrast, conventional ELISAs require secondary antibodies and additional enzymatic reactions. This simplicity could be beneficial for the practical use of SERS-ELISA for biomedical analysis.


Results and discussion


Strategy for SERS coupled with ELISA using CS@SiO 2 nanotags

Commercial SARS-CoV-2 ELISA kits use enzyme substrates or fluorescent dyes as optical probes to measure absorbance or fluorescence signals. In the case of SERS-ELISA, SERS signals of sandwich immunocomplexes are measured using SERS nanotags. SERS-based immunoassay for N-protein of SARS-CoV-2 proceeds as follows. First, capture antibodies are immobilized on eight glass wells of a 384-well plate. Next, different concentrations of SARS-CoV-2 lysate are put into each well to bind different concentrations of target N-protein. Then, detection antibody-conjugated SERS nanotags are added to each well to form sandwich immunocomplexes. Finally, the target N-protein is quantitatively analyzed by measuring the SERS signal of the nanotags present in each well. In the SERS-ELISAs reported so far, monomeric AuNPs were used primarily as SERS nanotags, which results in poor sensitivity because of the limited electromagnetic enhancement effect at the individual-particle level. In contrast, the CS@SiO 2 nanotags possess multiple hot spots (i.e., nanogaps) within one unit, enabling greater Raman enhancement and thus provide higher sensitivity for SARS-CoV-2 assays (top in Fig. 1d). To evaluate the nanoplasmonic characteristics of the CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags, we prepared three types of AuNPs with similar sizes, all encapsulated with silica shells, namely, CS AuNPs (CS@SiO 2 ), hollow AuNPs (HAuNP@SiO 2 ), and regular AuNPs (AuN-P@SiO 2 ) (Fig. 1d). Fig. 2 shows the assembly method for CS@SiO 2 [42]. Briefly, APTMS was coated on a glass surface, on which 75 nm citrate-capped AuNPs were immobilized on this glass surface. Next, BDMT, a well-known Raman reporter, was attached to the surface of the AuNPs through an Au-S bond. Then, the 32 nm AuNPs were bound to the 75 nm AuNP through the formation of an Au-S bond with another thiol group of BDMT. The number of 32 nm AuNPs (satellites) adsorbing on a 75 nm AuNP (core) is determined by interplay between the available space on the core AuNP and the electrostatic repulsion among the satellite AuNPs [49]. Elimination of the APTMS coating using NaOH in the third step in Fig. 2 prevents the 32 nm AuNPs from adsorbing onto the glass substrates, leading to the formation of CS NPs with 100% purity [50]. The typical yield for the CS NPs per glass substrate was 4 × 10 9 particles or 1 pM in 5 mL solution [42]. We concentrated the sample by using 9-10 glass substrates. This sequential assembly method based on glass substrates offers several advantages over other assembly methods performed in suspension: (i) Washing and purification is easy and does not cause aggregation of NPs. (ii) Various building blocks can be used as constituents for CS nanoassemblies. (iii) Dithiol linkers provide well-defined nanogaps.

The BDMT molecules serve as both a Raman reporter and a linker that binds satellite AuNPs to a core AuNP using the two terminal thiol groups at both ends. Au-S covalent bonding is more robust in maintaining the assembly structure than assembling AuNPs through electrostatic interactions. Furthermore, nanogaps are uniformly defined by the self-assembled monolayer (SAM) formation of the molecular thiol linkers. The gap distance is also much shorter (~1.1 nm) than that of other linkers, such as DNAs, proteins, or polymers (~4 nm). A short interparticle distance creates more intense electromagnetic fields in the nanogaps, leading to a stronger SERS signal intensity.

Once the CS particles were formed on the glass substrate, they were separated from the glass substrate via sonication and encapsulated with SiO 2 for better stability and easier surface modification. Finally, the antibodies that aid in the detection of the nucleocapsid protein (Nprotein) were immobilized using sulfo-SMCC linker for the use of CS@SiO 2 as a SERS nanotag for the SARS-CoV-2 detection assays. For comparison, HAuNP@SiO 2 (Fig. S1a) and AuNP@SiO 2 SERS nanotags (Fig. S1b) were also synthesized using the previously reported method [44]. The SERS signal intensity and stability were compared and evaluated among those three types of SERS nanotags (CS@SiO 2 , HAuN-P@SiO 2 , and AuNP@SiO 2 ) under various conditions. Fig. 3 shows the TEM images for the synthesized CS@SiO 2 , HAuN-P@SiO 2 , and AuNP@SiO 2 , and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) images for CS@SiO 2 . These images indicate that the three types of SERS nanotags with uniform compositions were successfully synthesized. The EDS images clearly show that the SiO 2 shell surrounds the CS NPs. Corresponding UV/Vis spectra, DLS data, and SERS spectra are also provided in Fig. S2. CS@SiO 2 shows the two characteristic absorption bands: one at 551 nm and the other at 748 nm, assigned to the nonradiant (uncoupled) dipole plasmon mode of each constituent AuNP and the radiant plasmon coupling mode between the core and satellite AuNPs, respectively [42]. HAuNP@SiO 2 and AuNP@SiO 2 also have a characteristic localized surface plasmon resonance band at 590 nm and 543 nm, respectively. Fig. S2c shows the size distributions of the three particle types from DLS instruments. Fig. S2d shows the SERS spectra for three SERS nanotags. For CS@SiO 2 , a high-intensity Raman spectrum of BDMT is observed as the BDMT is in the nanogap between the core and satellite AuNPs as a linker. Fig. S3 shows the changes in the UV/Vis spectra, size distribution, and zeta potential upon antibody conjugation for the three nanotag types. These data confirm that we successfully immobilized antibodies on three types of SERS nanotags.


Characterization of CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags and stability tests

We also tested the aliphatic dithiol molecule, 1,8-octandithiol (ODT), as a Raman reporter, but it gave a very weak Raman signal intensity owing to its small Raman scattering cross-section. Additionally, we tested three Raman reporters: benzyl mercaptan (BM), thiophenol (BT), and 4-aminobenzenethiol (ABT), as shown in Fig. S4. In this case, we used ODT as the linker and inserted Raman reporters (X) in the SAM of ODT using a 1:1 molar mixture of ODT + X (X = aromatic monothiols, such as BM, BT, or ABT). As shown in Fig. S4, the SERS intensity of BDMT is much stronger than that of the other reporter molecules because of the larger number of reporter molecules in the nanogaps. For HAuNP@SiO 2 and AuNP@SiO 2 , BT was used as a Raman reporter instead of BDMT. When we used BDMT, it connected AuNPs, causing uncontrollable aggregation.

We determined the SERS enhancement factors (EFs) for the three SERS nanotags by comparing the Raman peak intensity with and without the corresponding nanoparticles. The Raman intensity I Raman of a solution with a known concentration (C Raman ) was compared to the SERS intensity I SERS obtained under identical conditions (20 × objective lens and 632.8 nm laser with a power of 18 mW). SERS experiments for the three SERS nanotags were performed with the analyte in solutions at known concentrations of C SERS (much smaller than C Raman ). Then, the enhancement factor (EF) is defined by the ratio of the Raman intensity per molecule as follows:
EF = I SERS ∕C SERS I Raman ∕C Raman
The EFs were calculated using the strongest Raman peak intensities at 1597 cm − 1 (BDMT) and 1574 cm − 1 (BT), as indicated with asterisks in Fig. S2d. Comparisons of the Raman peak intensities for the concentrations of Raman reporters in the SERS nanotags (4.17 × 10 − 7 M BDMT for CS@SiO2, 10 − 7 M BT for HAuNP@SiO 2 , and 10 − 7 M BT for AuN-P@SiO 2 ) with those from the solutions (1 M BDMT and 0.1 M BT) yielded the SERS EFs corresponding to 7.8 × 10 7 (CS@SiO 2 ), 7.43 × 10 4 , (HAuNP@SiO 2 ), and 3.36 × 10 4 (AuNP@SiO 2 ). In HAuNPs, pinholes in the Au nanoshell are capable of forming nanogaps, which increases the Raman signal by a factor of 2 compared with that of AuNPs with smooth surfaces [36]. The CS@SiO 2 produced the greatest SERS EF, ~1000 times larger than those of the other two SERS nanotags. The multiple nanogaps between the core and satellite AuNPs within the CS@SiO 2 play a significant role in enhancing the Raman signal.

We performed a finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulation to theoretically understand the plasmonic coupling effects between the two Au particles. Fig. 4 presents the simulated electric field intensity distributions and maximum intensities of each particle when 633 nm polarized light irradiates on CS, HAuNP, and AuNP. The calculations show that the E max /E 0 value for CS (192.3) is significantly larger than that of HAuNP (13.66) or AuNP (7.85), consistent with the experimental results shown in Fig. S2d.

The stability of SERS nanotags under various conditions (such as the salt concentration, pH, temperature, aggregation, and time) is critical when using them in immunoassays. Therefore, we tested the stability of our CS@SiO 2 nanotags by measuring the changes in the spectroscopic signals and comparing them with those AuNPs of a similar size in harsh conditions. Fig. 5a shows the TEM images of the CS@SiO 2 NPs and AuNPs used in this study. Figs. 5b and 5c show the changes in the normalized absorption intensity of the CS@SiO 2 NPs and AuNPs against the changes in NaCl salt concentration and pH. As the NaCl concentration increases, the AuNP particles begin to aggregate at 20 mM NaCl concentration, and the absorption intensity decreases sharply. In contrast, the absorption intensity of CS@SiO 2 NPs decreases slightly, but largely remains constant even with the addition of NaCl (Fig. 5b). The CS@SiO 2 NPs are also stable across a wide range of pH, whereas AuNPs become unstable in basic or acidic conditions (Fig. 5c).

Stability at high temperatures is also critical for the long-term storage of SERS nanotags in tropical regions [43,44]. Both CS@SiO 2 and regular AuNPs are stable at 25 • C over a time span of 48 h (grey bars in Fig. 5d). Upon heating to 45 • C, however, the AuNPs begin to deteriorate much faster (in 3 h) than the CS@SiO 2 NPs (in 12 h) (colored bars in Fig. 5d). The SERS intensity was reduced by 80% than that of AuNPs stored at room temperature for 48 h (bottom, Fig. 5d). As the temperature increased, Raman reporter molecules on AuNPs were released from the surface, and their desorption led to a rapid decrease in the SERS intensity. For CS@SiO 2 particles at 45 • C, however, the SERS intensity was only reduced by 30% after 48 h (top, Fig. 5d) because silica encapsulation prevents the release of Raman reporter molecules from the surface of CS. Such higher sensitivity and superior stability make the CS@SiO 2 NPs an excellent SERS label for SERS-ELISA applications.


Performance evaluation of the CS@SiO 2 -based SERS-ELISA platform

The proposed CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags were used for highly sensitive SARS-CoV-2 immunoassays. As specified previously, the low sensitivity of commercially available LFA strips (300-500 PFU mL − 1 ) causes the high false-negative diagnosis rate for early-stage infections [12][13][14], which has been a serious problem. Fig. S5 shows the SARS-CoV-2 assay results using (a) 96-well plastic and (b) 384-well glass plates based on absorbance measurements. Assays were performed in the 0-1000 PFU mL − 1 SARS-CoV-2 quantitation range, and calibration curves were determined from the absorbance measurement data. Error bars indicate the standard deviations from five measurements. The LoDs of SARS-CoV-2 that were chosen for the 96-and 384-well plates were 59.3 and 70.1 PFU mL − 1 , respectively. Although these LoDs are within the proper range of the best-performing ELISA (< 100 PFU mL − 1 ), lowering the LoD is still desired to reduce the false-negative diagnostic rate in COVID-19 diagnosis.

Fig. 6d shows that the SERS signal from HAuNP@SiO 2 or AuNP@SiO 2 nanotags was so weak that it was undetectable even when they bound to 1000 PFU mL − 1 SARS-CoV-2. In marked contrast, the Raman peak intensities for the on and off conditions were clearly distinguishable when CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags were used. Interestingly, a weak SERS signal is still observed from the off condition (0 PFU mL − 1 ) for the CS@SiO 2 nanotags, which indicates a small amount of SERS nanotags that was not removed during the washing process as a result of non-specific binding to the plate. Nevertheless, it could be confirmed that these non-specific binding effects do not affect the accurate quantitative analysis of SARS-CoV-2, as shown in Fig. 7. Fig. S9 and Fig. 7 show the assay results obtained using CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags for the 0-1000 PFU mL − 1 concentration range of SARS-CoV-2 in a 384-well glass plate. Fig. S9a shows how Raman mapping images are measured in each well after the formation of immunocomplexes for the various SARS-CoV-2 concentrations. Fig. S9b illustrates Raman mapping images obtained for three well lines at identical concentration conditions. The color-decoding bar on the right shows that the Raman intensity gradually increases from dark red to bright yellow. As the concentration of SARS-CoV-2 increases, the Raman peak intensity increases consistently in the Raman mapping image. Fig. 7 shows the results of the SARS-CoV-2 assay using three different SERS nanotags. Fig. 7a shows the average Raman spectra for 400 pixels (left) and the corresponding calibration curve (right) measured for each SARS-CoV-2 concentration (the first well line in Fig. S9b). The LoD was determined using the following four-parameter sigmoidal equation.
y = A 1 − A 2 1 + (x/x 0 ) p + A 2
From Raman spectral data, the parameters were determined to be A 1 = 142.8, A 2 = 1160.6, x 0 = 774.8, and p = 0.51. The LoD was determined using the standard method proposed by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, i.e., LoD = Mean blank + 3 SD blank , where Mean blank and SD blank were determined from the equation above, and the LoD was calculated to be 8.81 PFU mL − 1 , which is 10 times better than the conventional ELISA. In contrast, when SERS-ELISA was performed using HAuNP@SiO 2 (Fig. 7b) or AuNP@SiO 2 (Fig. 7c), the SERS signal did not change with the concentration of SARS-CoV-2. These results confirm that a highly sensitive SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay is only possible using a SERS-ELISA with CS@SiO 2 SERS nanotags.

SERS-ELISA assays were also performed for SARS-CoV-2 (1000 PFU mL − 1 ), influenza A/H1N1 (4032 HFU mL − 1 ), influenza A/H3N2 (5461 HFU mL − 1 ), and influenza B (241 µg mL − 1 ) viruses to verify the specificity for SARS-CoV-2. The average Raman spectra of the four different viruses obtained from the Raman mapping images for 400 pixels are displayed in Fig. S10a. The corresponding average Raman intensities are shown in Fig. S10b, and the assay results using SERS-ELISA show a strong SERS signal intensity only in the presence of SARS-CoV-2. These results demonstrate that only the target virus is detected using the proposed SERS-ELISA platform.


Clinical validation of SERS-ELISA platforms using CS@SiO 2 nanotags for SARS-CoV-2 assays

Finally, SARS-CoV-2 immunoassays were performed on 30 clinical samples (24 positive and 6 negative samples) to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of the SERS-ELISA method using CS@SiO 2 nanotags. As shown in Table 1, the positive samples were classified into four grades (< 20, 20-25, 25-30, and > 30) according to the cycle threshold (Ct) value for RT-PCR. The positive and negative discrimination results, obtained from our newly developed SERS-ELISA platform, were compared with those obtained from commercial LFA strips. Table 2 compares the sensitivity and specificity of those two assay techniques. For the positive samples with a Ct < 25, both the commercial LFA strips and SERS-ELISAs show a 100% sensitivity. For the clinical samples with low concentrations of SARS-CoV-2, however, the LFA strip assay results show poor sensitivity (71% for Ct = 25-30% and 22% for Ct > 30). In marked contrast, our SERS-ELISA platform performs with excellent sensitivity for the samples derived from patients with low virus concentrations (100% for Ct = 25-30% and 55% for a Ct > 30). In particular, SERS-ELISA can detect SARS-CoV-2 in clinical samples with Ct < 30 with 100% sensitivity, showing that this diagnostic technology has good clinical applicability for early infection patients. In the case of the six negative clinical samples, both methods showed 100% specificity. Our clinical assay data indicate that the false-negative rate for early infection patients can be significantly reduced if the CS@SiO 2 -SERS-ELISA platforms are used instead of commercial LFA strips for immunodiagnosis SARS-CoV-2.


Conclusion

A significant number of reports have shown the possibility that the SERS-ELISA method can overcome the sensitivity limitations of conventional absorption or fluorescence ELISA. AuNPs have primarily been used as a detection label in SERS-ELISA, however, they do not show good sensitivity and stability at the individual-particle level. Thus, the development of a new SERS nanotag that can solve this problem is highly desired. Here, we developed a CS nanotag with superior sensitivity and reproducibility. This nanotag could generate multiple nanogaps by adsorbing several 32 nm AuNPs (satellites) on the surface of 75 nm AuNPs (core) through BDMT dithiol linkers. Our CS nanotags offered significantly high SERS signal enhancement owing to the hot spots between the core and satellite AuNPs at the individual-particle level. Moreover, the stability of the CS NPs was significantly improved by encapsulating them with SiO 2 . The SERS signal enhancement and stability of the proposed CS@SiO 2 nanotags were evaluated by comparing their spectroscopic data with those of AuNP@SiO 2 and HAuNP@SiO 2 .

SERS-ELISAs were also performed to detect SARS-CoV-2 to verify the clinical diagnostic application of the proposed CS@SiO 2 . We constructed a calibration curve of the SERS-ELISA for various SARS-CoV-2 concentrations, and obtained the LoD that corresponded 8.81 PFU mL − 1 . This low LoD indicates that the sensitivity of SERS-ELISA assays using CS@SiO2 nanotags is better than the LFA and conventional ELISA by a factor of 100 and 10, respectively. Finally, we applied this new CS@SiO 2 -SERS-ELISA assays to the 30 SARS-CoV-2 clinical samples. The assay yields a sensitivity of 100% for the 15 samples with Ct < 30% and 55% for the 9 samples with Ct > 30. These assay results indicate that the false-negative diagnostic rate of SARS-CoV-2 could be considerably reduced using the SERS-ELISA with CS@SiO 2 nanotags when compared to that obtained using commercial LFA strips or conventional ELISA. We believe that this novel SERS-ELISA method will significantly contribute to the accurate immunodiagnosis of rapidly spreading infectious diseases.
