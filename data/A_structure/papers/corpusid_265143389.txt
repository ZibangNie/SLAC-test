At the local scale, microclimate variability is strongly influenced by biotic and abiotic characteristics, such as climatic, topographical, ecological, and land-cover factors (Aalto, Maeda, et al., 2022;Aalto, Tyystjärvi, et al., 2022;Beck et al., 2018;De Frenne et al., 2021;Niittynen et al., 2020;Stein et al., 2014;Ye et al., 2022).These microclimate drivers commonly modify surface hydrothermal conditions through heat budget and water redistribution (Garcia et al., 2014).Elevation and terrain roughness are well-known topographic elements that influence microclimate distribution.Many tropical mountains encompass a remarkable climatic volume, providing abundant cool refugia for local species to adapt to warming climates (Rahbek et al., 2019).Local warming along altitudinal gradients is likely to cause the homogenization of mountainous microclimates, which may jeopardize habitats and lead to biodiversity loss as a result of climate change (De Frenne et al., 2021).In addition, ecological and land cover factors such as canopy closure and biome type also influence local microclimates through the intertwining effects of albedo and evapotranspiration (De Frenne et al., 2019).For example, deforestation has been shown to produce cooling with increased albedo and warming with decreased evapotranspiration in tropical mountainous regions (Zeng et al., 2020).However, in tropical savanna bushlands in Kenya, clearance of bushlands to croplands has caused an increase in land surface temperature despite increased albedo and decreased evapotranspiration (Aalto, Maeda, et al., 2022;Aalto, Tyystjärvi, et al., 2022;Abera et al., 2020).Furthermore, temperature and precipitation fluctuations are viewed as direct drivers of microclimate (Aalto, Maeda, et al., 2022;Aalto, Tyystjärvi, et al., 2022).Collectively, these biotic and abiotic drivers alter hydrothermal conditions and microclimate distribution.However, current microclimate research continues to depend on the quantification of temperature variability at the local scale, ignoring the impact of other potential factors such as moisture and wind speed.In contrast, microclimates based solely on temperature quantification cannot fully describe the characteristics of microclimate variations in the tropics due to sufficient warmness.Therefore, defining microclimate variability in tropical mountains using appropriate ecological criteria is extremely important.

Climate niche modeling approaches usually presume that climate drives species range shifts (Atwater et al., 2018;Rahbek et al., 2019;Sexton et al., 2009;Suggitt et al., 2018).A bioclimatic classification scheme that connects climate and biomes is regarded as an effective tool for studying biological responses to shifts in microclimates.Kӧppen (1936) described a widely accepted climatic classification system, and various improved versions have since been published (Kottek et al., 2006;Peel et al., 2007;Rohli, Andrew Joyner, et al., 2015).Such a bioclimatic classification is easily coupled with fine-scale climate data sets as a first-hand index for tracking shifts in microclimates.Because bioclimatic information is assigned to distinct grid boxes, it provides a grid-based solution for interpreting the potential response of biodiversity to microclimate variability.Landscape morphological metrics provide a tool for measuring the spatial heterogeneity of microclimate patterns using the "patch-mosaic" concept (Guan et al., 2020(Guan et al., , 2022)).The spatial distribution of climatic patches under heterogeneous hydrothermal conditions can be used to analyze landscape pattern responses to climate variability, including area, shape, structure, and function (McGarigal & Cushman, 2005).However, few attempts have been made to investigate the spatial heterogeneity of microclimatic variability in tropical regions from a landscape perspective.Southeast Asia, in particular (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1), encompasses an area of approximately 3,000,000 km 2 (Lat: 7°-28°/Lon: 90°-113°).Both biotic and abiotic factors influence fine-scale microclimatic variability; however, their relative importance in Southeast Asia has not been thoroughly investigated.

The goal of this study was to measure fine-scale microclimate variability over Southeast Asia based on the Köppen-Geiger climate classification.Shannon's diversity index (SHDI) was used in the "patch-mosaic" framework to calculate the degree of microclimate heterogeneity in a specific Köppen climate landscape (3 × 3 cells).First, we used fine-scale temperature and precipitation data sets to map the SHDI distributions between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017.We then applied the gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) method to examine the contributions of microclimate drivers to SHDI.Thereafter, we investigated the potential drivers of temperature and precipitation changes in the SHDI for each mountain over Southeast Asia between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017.


Materials and Methods


Data Sets and Pre-Treatment

Monthly temperature and precipitation data sets  were collected from the dynamical downscaling product across the Third Pole Region (Sun et al., 2021), which was made available by the project "Climate Change and Water Resources in the Great River Regions in Southeast and South Asia" of the Strategic Priority Research Program of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Grant No. XDA20060402) (http://www.lancang-mekong.net/).The fifth-generation ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis of the global climate was downscaled to provide a high-resolution (3 km) temperature and precipitation data set over Southeast Asia using the Weather Research and Forecasting Model version 3.7.1.This data set has been proven to be highly reliable and meets high accuracy requirements (Ou et al., 2020(Ou et al., , 2023)).

Multiple environmental factors were used to interpret the potential influence of the SHDI on microclimate variability.The surface fractional vegetation cover (FVC) product V40  was obtained from the Global Land Surface Satellite product at a spatial resolution of 0.05° (Liang et al., 2021).Root-zone soil moisture products v3. 3 (1980-2018) were derived from the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model with a resolution of 0.25° (Martens et al., 2017).Farmland statistics for 1992-2020 were derived from a global land cover map with a spatial resolution of 300 m provided by the Land Cover CCI Climate Research Data Package (Harper et al., 2022).Topographical factors, including elevation and roughness (1 km) extracted from "Global 1, 5, 10, 100-km Topography," were used to explore potential influences on SHDI (Amatulli et al., 2018).All the data sets were interpolated to 3 km using bilinear interpolation to match the high-resolution climate data sets (Table 1).Additionally, the shapefile of mountainous areas in Southeast Asia was derived from the GMBA Mountain Inventory V1.2 (Körner et al., 2017).


Köppen-Geiger Climate Classification

We used the Köppen-Geiger climate classification updated by Peel et al. (2007) to categorize climate types over Southeast Asia between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017 (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1).This classification, based on annual and monthly mean temperature and precipitation, serves to indicate the potential distribution of biomes, given the substantial overlap between Köppen climate types and biome types (Rohli, Joyner, et al., 2015).Based on the different hydrothermal thresholds of the Köppen-Geiger climate classification, any grid in Southeast Asia was assigned a specific climate subtype.Although the tropical savannah climate (Aw) and temperate dry winter-hot summer climate (CWa) dominate most regions, various cool microclimates exist in the high mountainous areas of Southeast Asia, including Csa, Cwa, Csc, Cfb, and BSh (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1).In addition, a 5-year running average was employed to mitigate the fluctuations induced by natural variability as interannual variations include internal fluctuations that may or may not be related to climate trends (Mahlstein et al., 2013).


Landscape Diversity Index

A Köppen climatic landscape is seen as a collection of discrete climatic patches characterized by distinct temperature and precipitation thresholds within the framework of the "patch-mosaic" paradigm (Guan et al., 2022).These Köppen climatic patches delineate geographical regions that exhibit relatively consistent hydrothermal conditions and closed spaces.In this study, although there are similar spatial metrics that are highly correlated (McGarigal, 2002), we chose only SHDI to describe spatial heterogeneity in a specific Köppen climate landscape (Morris et al., 2014).This is because the SHDI takes into account not only climatic richness (number of climate types) but also evenness distribution.A landscape with more evenly distributed climate richness will have higher heterogeneity than a landscape with a few dominant climate types.In this study, we designed a moving window with a slide length of three cells as a small landscape to calculate the SHDI and returned the calculated values for each small landscape to the central cell.Each grid cell in Southeast Asia was allocated a predetermined SHDI value, with the exception of three cells at the outermost edge of the boundary (null value).Mountain-scale SHDI calculations, in contrast to grid-scale SHDI calculations, are based on their individual boundary ranges; that is, the calculation of microclimate heterogeneity in each mountain will have an SHDI value for a given year.The within-landscape SHDI increased with increases in distinct patch types (i ∈ I) and their evenness (P i ), defined by the proportion of the landscape occupied by climatic patch type i within a specific range.The formula for the

Attribution Analysis

We used GBDTs to examine the influence of multiple environmental factors on SHDI between 1992 and 2017 (Liu et al., 2022;Wei et al., 2019).This is an ensemble learning method that constructs and iterates utilizing multiple regression trees.It leverages the negative-gradient values of the loss function within the model to approximate the residuals by employing a lifting tree algorithm tailored for regression problems.The GBDT, being a tree model, has good interpretability capabilities for different environmental factors that influence SHDI changes.Using GBDT, we evaluated the relative "importance" of different environmental factors on SHDI across 39 mountainous areas in Southeast Asia (Figure S2 and Table S2 in Supporting Information S1).


Sensitivity Analysis

We designed a sensitivity experiment in 39 mountainous areas in Southeast Asia to isolate the potential impacts of temperature and precipitation on SHDI.Using the Köppen-Geiger climate classification algorithm, temperature and precipitation for each mountain were cross-combined between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017 to simulate and compare the actual SHDI during these periods.Two controlled experiments with moving windows of different sizes and spatial resolutions were designed to separate the potential influence of moving window size and spatial resolution on the SHDI.In the first experiment, statistical characteristics of the SHDI for the period 1982-2017 were compared using four different moving windows sizes: 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 7 × 7, and 9 × 9 cells.In the second experiment, we resampled the SHDI using bilinear interpolation at resolutions of 3, 6, 9, and 12 km to display the spatial heterogeneity of SHDI changes.


Results


Changes in SHDI

Figures 1a-1c show that the spatial grid-based SHDI changes between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017 were clearly linked to elevation, with changeable regions centered on high-elevation mountainous areas.Specifically, we identified a markedly high SHDI value in the mountainous areas (Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1), such as Qingshuiliang Shan, Gaoligong Shan, Ailao Shan, Laobie Shan, and Bangma Shan to the north of Southeast Asia; Arakan Yoma and Chin Hills to the west; and Truong San to the south.The heat maps (Figures 1d and 1e) for the periods 1982-1999 and 2000-2017 also show an apparent decrease in SHDI, particularly below 3000 m, which was confirmed by the statistical distribution plots for both periods.This suggests that over the past three decades, mountain microclimates have been susceptible to changes in temperature and precipitation, with decreases in SHDI presumably linked to increased homogenization of mountain microclimate types.In contrast, regions with low value were primarily found in low-elevation regions (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1), which is likely because of the relative climatic stability in these regions.Local climate type transitions do not occur in low-elevation regions if the increase or decrease in precipitation and temperature does not surpass the threshold for a particular climate type.Overall, a definite decrease in SHDI was observed in high-elevation mountainous regions during 1982-1999 and 2000-2017, most likely due to the combined effect of topographical and climatic factors.


Drivers of the Microclimate Heterogeneity

An apparent decreasing trend in the SHDI distribution over Southeast Asia between 1992 and 2017 is shown in Figure 2a and further confirmed by Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1.This suggests a tendency towards microclimate homogenization in high-elevation mountainous areas.To better identify the environmental drivers of SHDI, we used GBDT for an attribution analysis across the 39 mountainous areas in Southeast Asia (Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1).The GBDT model performed particularly well in these areas, with a root mean square error of ∼0.009 and an R-square of 0.96 (Table S2 in Supporting Information S1).Topographical factors, such as roughness and elevation (∼50.6%),contributed the most to SHDI changes, followed by climatic factors (∼21.5%), and other environmental factors (Figure 2b).Roughness emerged as the most significant factor influencing SHDI changes, accounting for 30.2% of the total, followed by elevation (20.4%),FVC (14.3%), temperature (11.4%), precipitation (10.1%), and other environmental parameters.Figure 2c further depicts a consistent negative correlation between roughness and SHDI across all 39 mountainous areas from 1982 to 2017.SHDI declined as roughness increased, confirming the ongoing trend of mountainous microclimate homogenization over the past three decades.

We further examined the effect of temperature and precipitation on SHDI in 39 mountainous areas between 1982 and 2017.Figures 3a and 3d clearly depict the marked fluctuations in temperature and precipitation around the year 2000.Specifically, the linear trend of temperature was weak before 2000, increasing at a rate of 0.0004°C/y; however, after 2000, the temperature increased substantially at a rate of 0.04°C/y (p < 0.01, MK test).In contrast, a sharp increase in precipitation was reported prior to 2000, followed by a significant decrease at a rate of 11.5 mm/y (p < 0.01, MK test).Due to the inconsistent temporal trends of temperature and precipitation, we compared the simulated SHDI with the actual SHDI between 1982-1999 and 2000-2017.The distributional results (Figure 3b) indicate that precipitation changes were highly aligned with the observed SHDI in the time 1982-1999 (T1/P1), and dominated the SHDI changes over Southeast Asia.In contrast, rapid warming emerged as a stronger driver of SHDI changes than precipitation during the period 2000-2017 (T2/P2) (Figure 3e).Furthermore, from 1981 to 1999, a substantially greater number of climatic patches were identified in the 39 mountainous areas than from 2000 to 2017 (Figure 3c).The mean area of climatic patches, on the other hand, was lower between 1982 and 1999 than between 2000 and 2017 (Figure 3f).These comparisons further show that the patch structures of these climate landscapes tended to congregate from 1982-1999 to 2000-2017.As precipitation increased and temperatures weakened prior to the year 2000, climatic and topographical factors jointly produced a large number of heterogeneous microclimates, resulting in a high SHDI.After 2000, as the temperature increased and precipitation decreased, the overall climate patches in the 39 mountainous areas tended to be homogenized; there were fewer numbers and larger mean patch areas, and the SHDI declined in comparison to 1982-1999.


Sensitivity of Microclimate Heterogeneity

We designed two controlled experiments with moving windows of varying sizes and geographical resolutions to characterize the spatial heterogeneity of SHDI changes.In terms of spatial resolution (Figure 4a), as the spatial resolution became coarser from 3 to 6 km, both the shape complexity and median SHDI values of the data distribution deteriorated, although the overall section of the data distribution appeared to be wider.This indicates that as the spatial resolution changes from 3 to 6 km, the spatial heterogeneity of the Köppen climate types decreases, and the grid SHDI values tend to be closer.As the spatial resolution continues to become coarser toward 9 km or 12 km, the sections of the data distribution appear to shrink.The spatial heterogeneity decreases substantially with coarser resolution, as seen in Figures 4c-4e.These unequivocally demonstrate that such grid-scale data sets cannot capture the fine-scale features of the microclimates over Southeast Asia.According to the grid-based SHDI calculation method, a moving window size of 3 × 3 cells is the minimum landscape for depicting the spatial heterogeneity of the SHDI distribution (Figure 4b).As the moving window size increases from 3 × 3 to 5 × 5 cells, the section of the distribution depicted by the violin plot generally broadens, whereas the shape complexity of the data distribution weakens.This is because the larger the window size, the more microclimate types may be involved in the small climate landscape, and the closer the SHDI values are between different grid cells.Figures 4f-4h also depict a wider and higher SHDI distribution than that of 3 × 3 cells.Overall, our sensitivity analysis suggests that changes in microclimate heterogeneity are remarkably regulated by fine-scale climatic data and the size of the spatial window.


Conclusion

Based on high-resolution data sets and the "patch-mosaic" model paradigm, we constructed a climate landscape model to examine the heterogeneity changes of microclimates over Southeast Asia from 1982 to 2017.

Our results revealed a decline in SHDI during this period, which was strongly correlated with an increase in elevation.According to the GBDT attribution analysis, these changes are primarily attributed to topographical (∼50.6%) and climatic factors (∼21.5%).From 1992 to 2017, the SHDI of the 39 mountainous areas generally decreased as roughness increased.Cross-combined experimental data show that precipitation changes dominated SHDI changes over Southeast Asia from 1982 to 1999, while rapid warming was the primary driving force of the SHDI decrease from 2000 to 2017.These changes were confirmed by the ebb and flow of the number and mean area of the climatic patches.In addition, as the size of the moving windows and resolution increased, the spatial heterogeneity of the grid SHDI distribution weakened.This is because such large moving windows and coarse grid-scale data sets weaken the fine-scale features of microclimate landscapes.Our results provide new insights into the regional climate variability and biodiversity conservation in response to climate change.
