Spectrometry of Captured Highly Charged Ions Produced Following Antiproton Annihilations
9 Oct 20253F6E3F8CC653CFCD982CFC2DE2EEA9BBarXiv:2510.08440v1[nucl-ex]
We report the first capture and time-of-flight spectrometry of highly charged ions produced following antiproton annihilations in a Penning-Malmberg trap.At the AEgIS experiment, we employed a multi-step nested-trap technique to isolate ions from antiproton annihilations with ultra-low-density helium and argon gas.The capture and identification of highly-charged argon ions in charge-states up to Ar 5+ demonstrates a new method for in-trap synthesis.This work establishes a clear path towards the direct capture and mass spectrometry of cold nuclear annihilation fragments, which will enable a complementary tool for exploring the neutron to proton density ratio at the extreme nuclear periphery.

Introduction.-Preciseknowledge of the spatial distributions of protons and neutrons in atomic nuclei is central to both nuclear physics and astrophysics.While electromagnetic probes can map charge densities with high precision [1,2], the neutron-rich peripheral region of nuclei remains experimentally challenging to explore.As an example, the neutron skin, given by the difference in neutron to proton rms radii, is a sensitive link to the nuclear forces [3], and its study allows us to explore the density dependence of the nuclear symmetry energy, which elucidates the properties of neutron stars [4].The ongoing CREX-PREX dilemma [5][6][7][8] further highlights the need for new techniques sensitive to the neutron distribution in the peripheral region of nuclei.

Annihilation of antiprotons (p) in atomic orbits around atomic nuclei provides a powerful probe of the neutronto-proton density ratio at the outer nuclear periphery, a region of low hadronic density 2-3 fm beyond the halfdensity radius [9,10].When the annihilation occurs on either a proton or neutron at the surface, the resulting mesons may escape the nucleus, leaving behind a 'cold' fragment with minimal recoil momentum [11,12].The production ratio of fragments from neutron versus proton removal directly mirrors the local n/p density ratio in this outer peripheral region ∼3 fm from the nuclear surface, where the nuclear density is up to 10 3 times lower than in the core [13,14].The study of these cold fragments offers a sensitive constraint on the neutron skin and the isospin structure of the extreme peripheral region of the nucleus [15].

Historically, the study of antiproton interactions with matter has been performed via beam-on-target (gas, liquid, or solid) experiments [10].These studies have provided crucial information on antiproton ionization crosssections and stopping powers in different targets [16].After being slowed by collisions, an antiproton is eventually captured into a high-lying circular Rydberg state near the atomic nucleus forming an antiprotonic atom [17,18].As it cascades to lower energy levels, the atom is being stripped of its electrons though the Meitner-Auger process [19,20].So far, the charge state after annihilation, remains unmeasured.While light, energetic fragments (pions, neutrons, protons, deuterons, tritons and helium nuclei) have been detected directly [21][22][23], much heavier fragments have only been studied using radiochemical means through gamma spectroscopy [13,24].These studies indicated that roughly 10 to 20% of the nuclear annihilation fragments only lose a single proton or neutron [12].Due to the limitations of the radiochemical approach, the fragment ratios could only be deduced for selected cases with appropriate decay properties [11,14,25,26].These measurements allowed systematic studies of the neutron skin by constraining models together with X-ray spectroscopy measurements of antiprotonic atoms and electron scattering data [15,27].However, cases involving * fredrik.parnefjord.gustafsson@cern.chnon-radioactive cold fragments, such as those from 208 Pb, had to be interpolated [28], introducing a significant systematic uncertainty for the determination of their neutron skin.

Trapping antiprotons in Penning-Malmberg traps is now routine at the AD-ELENA facility at CERN [29].The study of antiprotonic atom annihilations occurring within ion traps was first proposed by Wada and Yamazaki [30].Building on this idea, the PUMA project, currently under development, aims to probe the nuclear periphery of short-lived nuclei produced at ISOLDE by measuring the net charge of mesons from the annihilation events occurring in a portable trap.With this approach they will explore the region 2-2.5 fm from the half-density radius [31].A complementary approach, presented here, involves the direct capture and spectrometry of recoil-filtered cold annihilation fragments in a trap.The mass spectrometry of the captured cold fragments will enable a novel tool for exploring the extreme peripheral region 3 fm from the half-density radius, while filtering events where high energy mesons strongly interact with the residual nucleus, by the fragment recoil energy.The high charge state of annihilation fragments, resulting from the Auger cascade, enables their efficient capture within a Penning-Malmberg trap, as confirmed by recent simulations [32].Furthermore, these often radioactive highly charged ions (HCIs), if captured, are valuable systems for precision studies of fundamental interactions [33][34][35][36], with potential applications in quantum metrology [37].

In this work, we report the first experimental realization of this technique.We demonstrate the procedure by capturing highly charged ions produced from antiproton annihilations on a low-density gas target within a Penning-Malmberg trap.Using the AEgIS experiment at CERN [38], we employed time-of-flight spectrometry to identify the captured ion species.These results establish a new methodology for producing and trapping exotic HCIs and outline a clear path toward high-resolution spectrometry of cold annihilation fragments, enabling a complementary and powerful probe of the outer nuclear periphery.

A measurement procedure was developed to capture any positively charged ion species generated from antiproton interactions with the residual gas within the trapping region.The experimental cycle proceeded as follows for every captured antiproton bunch arriving from ELENA into the AEgIS trapping region (see Fig. 2). 1 Antiprotons are captured between the pulsed highvoltage (V HV = −14 kV) electrodes.Collisions with the buffer gas cool the antiprotons, resulting in the ionization of the atoms.

2 The low-voltage electrodes between HV1 and HV3

were then biased to a potential V floor (typically −190 V), forming a nested potential well that allowed for the capture of any positive species.Annihilations occurring within the trapping region are monitored using external scintillators that detect pions released during annihilation.

3 After a set antiproton trapping time, ranging from 20 s to 500 s, the potential of the HV3 electrode is ramped down to ground over approximately 12 s.This process ejects the remaining antiprotons axially.These ejection-induced annihilations are monitored using the external scintillators, providing a measure of the remaining antiproton number.Positive ions with insufficient axial energy to overcome the potential V floor remain trapped in the central region.

4 The potential on the low voltage electrodes is manipulated over approximately 10 s to axially compress the captured positive ions into a shorter trap (approximately 4 cm long) formed by three adjacent 1.5 cm long electrodes near the downstream HV3 electrode.

5 The potential of this short trap is raised over approximately 5 s to form a harmonic well with a set floor potential V launch (e.g., 180 V), confined axially by wall potentials V wall (e.g., 190 V).The launch potential V launch defines the potential energy of the ions ejected for the TOF measurement.This ion manipulation sequence took approximately 20 s.

6 Finally, the downstream wall electrode is rapidly switched (1 ns timescale) to 0 V, ejecting the trapped positive ions axially towards a microchannel plate (MCP) detector located at a distance of L TOF =1.05(1) m downstream within the 1 T magnetic field of the trapping region.The front face of the MCP was biased to 0 V.The MCP readout is amplified and recorded using a Teledyne LeCroy HDO6104 oscilloscope, triggered synchronously with the ejection pulse, yielding the captured TOF signal.The scintillator signals are processed using counting modules to monitor annihilation rates and timing throughout each experimental cycle.

In order to characterize the TOF spectrometry procedure, electron cooled antiprotons were prepared in a reverse-polarity trap and ejected towards the MCP (step 6 in Fig. 2).These measurements allowed systematic calibration studies at different V launch and V wall , indicating good agreement with the measured flight distance.An atomic mass resolution of M/∆M ≈ 45 was obtained when using the presented TOF spectrometry procedure (see Supplemental Material for more details A).

The present buffer-gas environment, used in this work simultaneously as a target for the ion formation and cooling of the species, limits sensitivity to the most interesting stripped annihilation fragments, HCIs are depleted by charge exchange, and primary fragments may scatter before capture.A direct path forward is to perform measurements without the presence of buffer gas in the trapping environment to preserve fragment charge states and kinematics.Refined approaches utilizing p −anion mixing would allow the laser-triggered synthesis of antiprotonic atoms and fragments in trap [32,45], and for species not forming anions, a Rydberg excited atomic beam could be injected on the trapped cold antiproton target.

The experiments reported here were constrained by a ∼190 V well depth set by available supplies and electrode geometries, furthermore, captured ions not cooled were ejected when reducing the trap depth before ion ejection for spectrometry.Employing positive bias on the HV electrodes will substantially increase capture efficiency of annihilation fragments to energies beyond 10 keV/q.This will allow the efficient capture of cold annihilation fragments for nuclear periphery studies, with the estimated complete capture of cold fragments with only a single proton or neutron lost from 80 Kr and heavier nuclei as predicted by GEANT4 simulations [32].

Achieving the mass resolution required for isotopic and isobaric separation will require efficient, non-intrusive cooling of the captured HCIs.Promising options include combined electron/positron cooling [46,47] and sympathetic cooling with laser-cooled ions [48].Once the fragments are cooled to thermal energies, a TOF resolving power comparable to that obtained with electroncooled antiprotons (M/∆M ∼ 170) should be reachable at AEgIS, using the demonstrated technique.For example, to identify A − 1 fragments of fully stripped p 80 Kr fragments a minimal mass resolution above M/∆M > 35 is necessary.To resolve multiple charge states of multiple isotopes and isobars, multi-reflection TOF (MR-TOF) mass spectrometry can reach resolving powers of M/∆M > 10 5 [49,50].Preliminary studies toward implementing MR-TOF functionality using the Penning-Malmberg trap electrodes are underway at AEgIS.As a complementary tool, mass-selective RF techniques could provide in-trap filtration of specific m/q bands [51].

TOF calibration measurements using cold antiprotons were performed with two trap configurations, V wall = −190 V and V wall = −160 V, while varying the applied launch potential V launch on the central electrode.We study the characteristics of the TOF procedure by plotting V launch against m/(2qT 2 TOF ) (see Fig. 6).While the measurements performed using V wall = −190 V are in good agreement with the proportionality expected from the constant-velocity assumption, the measurements using V wall = −160 V deviate slightly.This deviation is expected since the set voltage V set launch of the deeper trap differs from the actual potential experienced by the charged particles at the trap center, here denoted V real launch .The true launch potential is therefore parameterized as This correction restores consistency across both configurations and enables a unified determination of the effective flight length.From the global corrected fit we extract an effective distance of L eff = 1.1384(5) m, slightly larger than the measured geometric flight path of 1.05(1) m, that can be attributed to the constant-velocity approximation and the trap geometry.The raw TOF data from the four experimental runs were combined to create a single dataset.The resulting signal was binned into 70 ns intervals, and the mean voltage and standard error of the mean were calculated for each bin.As the MCP detector produces negative pulses, the spectrum was inverted to facilitate the analysis of positive peaks.

The intense He 1+ peak, arriving at approximately 12 µs, induced a significant electronic ringing artifact that manifested as a persistent oscillation across the spectrum.To isolate the smaller ion peaks for analysis, this artifact was modeled and subtracted.The model consisted of a composite function describing both the primary He 1+ peak and the ringing it produced.The peak itself was modeled with an Exponentially Modified Gaussian (EMG) distribution (defined in Eq.A2), while the ringing was described by a sum of two distinct damped sinusoidal functions of the form:
V ring (t) = 2 i=1 C i exp − t − t 0,i τ r,i sin(2πf i (t − t 0,i ) + ϕ i )
where for each component i, C is the amplitude, τ r is the decay time, f is the frequency, ϕ is the phase, and t 0 is the start time relative to the main peak.This full composite model was fitted to the binned spectrum using a least-squares algorithm.To ensure the model accurately captured the background artifact without being biased by the other ion peaks, the fit was constrained using time windows containing only the main He 1+ peak and representative regions of the oscillating background .After obtaining the best-fit parameters, the contribution from the two damped sinusoids was calculated and subtracted from the entire spectrum.The result of this procedure was a 'cleaned' spectrum with the ringing artifact removed, which was used for the subsequent multi-peak fit and calibration.


Multi-Peak Fit and TOF Determination

The cleaned spectrum was fitted with a global model consisting of a sum of seven EMG functions,one for each identified ion species, and a constant background.This 29-parameter function was fitted to the data using a weighted least-squares algorithm, where the weights were the inverse square of the propagated standard errors from the binning stage.To ensure conservative error estimates, the resulting covariance matrix from the fit was scaled by the reduced chi-squared (χ 2 ν ) of the fit if this value exceeded unity.

For an asymmetric EMG distribution, the peak maximum (the mode, t mode ) is shifted relative to the distribution's location parameter, µ.Therefore, the final TOF for each ion was determined by numerically calculating the mode of its corresponding fitted EMG function.The uncertainty on this TOF value was calculated by analytically propagating the errors from the relevant parameters (µ, σ, τ ) in the scaled covariance matrix.


Mass-to-Charge Calibration and Linearity Correction

The TOF values were converted to a mass-to-charge (m/q) axis using Eq.A1, with the precisely determined TOF mode of the He 1+ peak (T ref ) serving as a singlepoint reference.The statistical uncertainties on the calculated m/q values, as reported in Table I, were derived by propagating the uncertainties from the fitted TOF modes of the ion of interest (δT i ) and the reference ion (δT ref ) using the standard formula:
δ m q i = 2 m q i δT i T i 2 + δT ref T ref2
This single-point calibration assumes a perfectly quadratic relationship between TOF and m/q over the full range.However, the data in Table I presented in Fig 7 shows that this approximation works well for ions with m/q values close to the He 1+ reference, but systematically deviates for the slower ions.This behavior suggests a slight instrumental non-linearity, likely from small imperfections in the accelerating electric fields.

To characterize this deviation, a weighted linear regression was performed on the experimental m/q values versus their known literature values, see Fig 7.This fit provides a linear correction that can account for the observed offset and more accurately calibrate the mass axis across the entire detected range.
