Evaluating cell-free DNA integrity index as a non-invasive biomarker for neoadjuvant chemotherapy in colorectal cancer patients
2D52EE0834EC23A4A924C4DD0B324C8510.1186/s12885-025-14570-6Received: 11 January 2025 / Accepted: 23 June 2025Colorectal cancerLiquid biopsyCell-free DNANeoadjuvant chemotherapy
Background Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is gaining attention as a treatment for advanced colorectal cancer owing to its potential to improve surgical outcomes and prognosis.However, reliable biomarkers to predict the response to NAC are lacking.We aimed to investigate the predictive value of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) integrity index for NAC response, using machine learning to compensate for the small cohort size.Methods This retrospective study included 31 locally advanced colorectal cancer patients who underwent NAC and surgery at the Nippon Medical School Hospital between 2016 and 2020.Blood samples were collected pre-post-NAC to assess cfDNA levels using quantitative polymerase chain reaction.The cfDNA integrity index was calculated based on the ratio of long to short fragments in the long-interspersed element-1 repeat sequence.Statistical analyses, including random forest modeling, were performed to evaluate the predictive value of the cfDNA integrity index for treatment response.ResultsOf the 31 patients, 19 (61.3%) were classified as responders and 12 (38.7%)as non-responders.The post-NAC cfDNA integrity index was significantly different between the groups (P = 0.002, odds ratio = 16.0).Random forest analysis identified changes in the cfDNA integrity index as the most important predictor of NAC response (%IncMSE: 15.79; IncNodePurity: 2.21), while sex, age, tumor site, and pre-NAC cfDNA levels were not significant predictors.Conclusions Variability in the cfDNA integrity index shows promise as a biomarker for predicting NAC efficacy in colorectal cancer.

Background

In Japan, the standard treatment for locally advanced colorectal cancer consists of surgery followed by postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy [1].However, the outcome of this approach remains unsatisfactory.Recently, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has attracted attention in the treatment of advanced colorectal cancer owing to its potential for tumor shrinkage with advances in chemotherapy and good outcomes in other gastrointestinal cancers, such as esophageal and gastric cancers [2,3].

In the FOxTROT trial involving patients with cT3-4 N0-2 M0 colorectal cancer, the rate of perioperative major complications was significantly lower in patients in the NAC group compared to those in the surgeryfirst group.In addition, the R0 resection rate was 95.2% among patients in the NAC group, surpassing 90.9% in patients in the surgery-first group [4].While neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NACRT) remains the recommended treatment for high-risk recurrent rectal cancer in Europe and the USA, NAC has recently gained attention as an alternative treatment for intermediate-risk patients, primarily to avoid the adverse events associated with radiation therapy [5].

The GEMCAD 0801 trial, which studied cT3 rectal cancer, showed a 100% R0 resection rate, a 78% response rate, 69% 3-year recurrence-free survival, and a 6% local recurrence rate [6].Moreover, the FOWARC trial, which compared NACRT with NAC in stage II/III patients, found no significant difference in disease free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and local recurrence rate [7].Additionally, toxicity and post-operative complications were significantly lower in patients in the NAC group than in those in the NACRT group, suggesting the potential of NAC as an alternative to NACRT [7].

However, there is currently no consensus on biomarkers to predict response to NAC.As NAC is performed on resectable tumors, it is important to avoid delaying surgical intervention owing to a lack of response to treatment.This highlights the need for a simple, real-time method to assess and predict treatment responses.Liquid biopsy (LB) a non-invasive method that can be performed in real-time [8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15], has emerged as a promising tool for assessing NAC.

In our previous research, we reported that the length of DNA fragments in circulating free DNA (cfDNA) is associated with tumor cell death and is useful for evaluating minimal residual disease after surgery for metastatic colorectal cancer [16].The usefulness of cfDNA integrity indexes (ratio of long-and short-chain fragments) focusing on DNA fragment length in evaluating cancer treatment has been reported in various carcinomas [11,[17][18][19][20][21].Although genetic analysis has become the mainstay of LB research, particularly in colorectal cancer, the key driver gene, KRAS, remains wild-type in approximately half of the cases.One of the advantages of using the cfDNA integrity index in LB is that it allows for independent analysis irrespective of genetic mutations, making it a more cost-effective approach [17,19,22].

Clinical studies with small cohorts often face limitations in identifying reliable biomarkers.Machine learning algorithms, such as random forest, offer a powerful tool to overcome these challenges by identifying key predictors and enhancing predictive accuracy [23,24].As this was a single-center study and a small sample size was expected, we used random forest with a five-fold crossvalidation method to ensure the robustness of the model, and we supplemented the reliability by evaluating the prediction performance using the variable importance index.

This study aimed to investigate the predictive value of the cfDNA integrity index for NAC response using machine learning to address the limitations of small cohort size.


Methods


Patients

From April 2016 to June 2020, 76 patients with locally advanced colorectal cancer were treated with NAC at Nippon Medical School Hospital.Of these, 31 patients met the inclusion criteria, resulting in a final selection of 31 patients.The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma, (ii) cT4a or cN (+) * , (iii) no distant metastasis on chest, abdominal, and pelvic CT, (iv) good physical fitness for elective surgery, (v) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0-1, (vi) age 20-80 years and (vii) adequate hematologic, hepatic and renal functions ( * Regional lymph nodes, excluding the lateral region).A colorectal surgeon and radiologist diagnosed the clinical stage using computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) intestinal obstruction; (ii) cT4b tumor with invasion into an adjacent organ or the pelvic wall; (iii) lateral lymph node metastases; (iv) history of prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy; (v) presence of any other active malignant disease or severe comorbidity; and (vi) incomplete follow-up data or missing blood samples (Fig. 1).


Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Six courses of mFOLFOX6 or four courses of CapeOX were administered before surgery.Surgery was performed 3-5 weeks after completion of NAC.The response to NAC was determined based on histopathological changes in surgical specimens according to the Japanese Society of Colorectal Cancer grading system.In this study, histological response: grade 1b-3 was defined as a response, while grades 0-1 were considered as nonresponse (Fig. 2).


Sample collection and cell-free DNA extraction

Peripheral blood samples were collected at two time points: before initiating neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and after completion of the full regimen (six cycles of mFOLFOX6 or four cycles of CapeOX).All samples were drawn from peripheral veins in the morning under fasting conditions to minimize biological variability related to circadian rhythms and haemodynamic status.The samples were transferred into BD Vacutainer EDTA tubes (Becton Dickinson, Oxford, UK), and cfDNA was extracted using the plasma QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen).Plasma samples were centrifuged at 1,600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C within 2 h of collection to remove cellular debris, followed by a second centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min to ensure complete removal of contaminating cells.Plasma was stored at − 80 °C until cfDNA extraction.The DNA volume was measured using a ds-DNA-specific Fluorimeter Qubit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (Fig. 3-A).


Primers and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

qPCR was performed using an ABI 7500 PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).We measured the levels of long interspersed element-1 (LINE-1), which is one of PCR products were confirmed by melt curve analysis.The absolute equivalent amount of DNA in each sample was determined using standard curves generated with serial dilutions (5 ng to 0.001 pg) of human genomic DNA.A no-template negative control was included on each plate.All qPCR assays were performed in a blinded manner.Mean values were calculated from triplicate reactions.The cfDNA integrity index was calculated as the ratio of long to short LINE-1 fragment quantities:

Integrity Index = (Quantity of LINE-1 297 bp)/(Quantity of LINE-1 127 bp).(Fig. 3-B).

In this study, we selected the LINE-1 297 bp and 127 bp primer pair for cfDNA integrity analysis, as these targets are highly abundant and well-characterized in cfDNA studies, and had been previously validated with optimized protocols at our institution.The primers used are provided in Additional file 1.


Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 (SPSS Japan, Tokyo, Japan).Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.The chi-square test was used to examine the relationship between the response and non-response groups and clinicopathological parameters.We investigated the histological efficacy of NAC and the patients' backgrounds, pathological factors, and molecular volume changes (pre-and post-NAC; peripheral blood cfDNA volume and cfDNA integrity index).

In addition, a random forest algorithm, a type of machine learning, was employed to assess the relationship between the target and explanatory variables.To construct the model, we used the "randomForest" package in R statistical software to calculate the factor importance of each explanatory variable.Factor importance is an indicator that assesses the contribution of each variable to the predictive performance of the model, specifically '%IncMSE' (percentage increase in mean squared error) and `IncNodePurity' (increase in within-group consistency).

Factor importance results were obtained using the `importance` function and the `varImpPlot` function was used for visualization.This allowed us to quantify the contribution of each explanatory variable to the model's predictive accuracy.


Results


Patients' background

Table 1 presents the patient characteristics.The cohort included 18 patients with rectal and 13 with colon cancers (of the 13, six had right-sided colon cancer and seven had left-sided colon).The median age was 65 years (range Among the 31 patients, 19 were classified as grade 1b-3 (responders), while 12 were classified as grade 0-1a (nonresponders).There were no significant differences in the background characteristics between the response and non-response groups (Table 2).


Importance of features by random forests

`%IncMSE' indicates the extent to which the model's mean squared error (MSE) increases when a variable is removed.A positive value indicates that the variable contributes to the model's predictive accuracy, with a higher value indicating the greater importance of the variable.However, negative values indicate that the variable reduces the predictive performance of the model, suggesting that the variable may not be more important to the model or could introduce noise.In addition, `Inc-NodePurity' indicates how much each explanatory variable increases the degree of consistency within a group.Higher values indicated that the variable was more important in the model.

Variables with negative `%IncMSE' values (sex, age, tumor site, pre-cfDNA, post-cfDNA, histological type) may reduce the predictive performance of the model, making these variables less important.Pre-cfDNA, sex, and age were also negative, suggesting that these variables did not contribute to the model prediction.On the other hand, integrity change has a very high `%IncMSE' of 15.7935147, indicating that it is an important variable that significantly improves the predictive performance of the model.Second, pre-integrity, post-integrity, pre-carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), post-CEA, ypT, and ypN were also positive, indicating that these variables contributed to the predictive performance of the model but were less influential compared to integrity change.Regarding "IncNodePurity' , integrity change showed a high value of 2.21 further confirming its significant contribution to the degree of consistency within the group.In contrast, other variables showed relatively low values, especially those with negative values, which indicated that they contributed little to the degree of consistency within the group.

Thus, integrity change emerges as the most important explanatory variable, significantly improving the models' predictive performance.However, variables such as sex, age, tumor site, pre-DNA, post-DNA, and histological type were deemed less relevant to the model (Fig. 4).


Change in CfDNA integrity index and NAC response

The rate of change in the cfDNA integrity index before and after NAC was low in the non-response group.In contrast, the rate of change of the cfDNA integrity index before and after NAC was large in the response group (P = 0.002, odds ratio = 16.0 [95% confidence interval = 2.81-91.2])(Fig. 5).


Discussion

This study highlights the potential of the cfDNA integrity index as a robust and cost-effective biomarker for predicting responses to NAC in colorectal cancer.The findings demonstrate that the rate of change in the integrity index is a significant predictor, independent of genetic mutations, and adaptable across a range of clinical scenarios.However, deeper exploration of its biological implications, broader clinical relevance, and acknowledgment of study limitations are necessary to further substantiate its applicability.


Biological mechanisms underlying CfDNA integrity index

The cfDNA integrity index encapsulates the interplay between apoptosis and necrosis, two dominant forms of tumor cell death influenced by chemotherapy [19].Apoptosis generates shorter cfDNA fragments, while necrosis releases longer fragments, collectively shaping the measurable patterns of the integrity index.This study's respondents displayed pronounced post-NAC changes in the cfDNA integrity index, likely indicative of a shift toward apoptosis-driven tumor reduction.Nonresponders exhibited minimal changes, suggesting intrinsic tumor resistance, limited drug delivery, or a hostile tumor microenvironment that dampens chemotherapy effectiveness.Chemotherapy-induced cell death encompasses various processes, and as reported in previous studies, the specific cell death mechanisms promoted can vary [25][26][27][28][29][30], potentially manifesting as increases or decreases in the cfDNA integrity index.

Examples from Additional file 2 further support this interpretation.Interestingly, two contrasting responder cases were observed: one exhibited a significant increase in the cfDNA integrity index post-NAC, while the other demonstrated a marked decrease.The increase likely reflects a chemotherapy-induced shift toward apoptosis, with apoptotic fragments disproportionately contributing to the cfDNA pool.In contrast, the observed decrease may indicate effective necrotic cell clearance or heightened immune-mediated degradation of cfDNA fragments.Both scenarios align with successful therapeutic responses, underscoring the nuanced interplay of cfDNA dynamics in reflecting tumor cell death modalities.Conversely, in a non-responder case, the integrity index exhibited minimal change despite the gross leveling of the tumor observed in endoscopic imaging.This Fig. 4 Importance of features by Random Forests.Pre-denotes pre-NAC and post-denotes post-NAC data discrepancy highlights the superiority of molecular assessment over gross morphological evaluation in predicting true histopathological outcomes.The variable responses emphasize the need for integrating cfDNA analysis with complementary biomarkers to better delineate the underlying mechanisms.

Further studies are warranted to elucidate the molecular pathways driving these differences.For instance, integrating proteomic or transcriptomic analyses could reveal insights into how variations in tumor microenvironment or angiogenic activity influence cfDNA release patterns.Such studies would not only deepen our understanding of cfDNA dynamics but also improve its predictive accuracy.


Clinical implications and broader relevance

The cost-effectiveness and non-reliance on genetic mutations make the cfDNA integrity index highly versatile.This biomarker is particularly advantageous in settings where genomic testing is either unavailable or cost-prohibitive.Moreover, its potential utility extends beyond colorectal cancer.For example, similar analyses could be applied to other gastrointestinal malignancies, such as gastric or esophageal cancers, where NAC is becoming a standard therapeutic approach.In contrast to ctDNA, which has shown promise as a biomarker in advanced cancers, its detection sensitivity in stage II/III colorectal cancer remains limited.A recent study reported a low ctDNA positivity rate in these resectable stages, reducing its practical predictive value in the neoadjuvant setting [31].Additionally, ctDNA analysis often requires next-generation sequencing (NGS), which incurs high costs and may be affected by spatial heterogeneity of tumor mutations-where ctDNA fragments harbor variants not present in the primary tumor tissue.These discrepancies may complicate the interpretation of ctDNA-based analyses.

Conversely, cfDNA integrity index analysis does not rely on mutation profiling and is not affected by spatial heterogeneity.It provides a cost-effective and mutationindependent approach that reflects treatment-induced changes in tumor cell death (e.g., apoptosis or necrosis).These advantages make cfDNA integrity a more accessible and versatile biomarker, particularly suitable for early treatment monitoring in settings where NGS-based testing may not be feasible.

From a practical perspective, the integrity index offers a real-time, non-invasive means to evaluate chemotherapy efficacy.Its greatest clinical value may lie in early-phase monitoring shortly after the initiation of NAC (e.g., after the first or second cycle), allowing clinicians to identify Fig. 5 Pre-and post-NAC levels of the cfDNA integrity index.Variability rate between pre-and post-NAC levels of the cfDNA integrity index in groups with NAC response and non-response non-responders in a timely manner.In such cases, clinical decisions such as changing the chemotherapy regimen, incorporating radiotherapy, or performing surgery earlier could help minimize unnecessary exposure to ineffective treatment.

However, due to substantial inter-individual variability in treatment response kinetics, determining the optimal timing for cfDNA-based prediction remains a clinical challenge.We are currently planning a prospective study involving monthly blood sampling during NAC to clarify the timing and predictive value of cfDNA integrity monitoring.

Additionally, the index's adaptability suggests potential use in monitoring disease progression or recurrence in post-operative settings.


Limitations and future directions

While this study effectively demonstrates the utility of the cfDNA integrity index in a small cohort, several limitations must be addressed.First, the single-center design and limited sample size constrain the generalizability and statistical power of the findings.However, given the practical difficulty of initiating large-scale studies without preliminary evidence, this work was intentionally designed as a pilot study to inform future multicenter investigations.

Given the limitations of conventional statistical testing in small datasets-particularly the risk of overinterpretation due to p-value dependence-we adopted a machine learning approach (random forest analysis) to explore variable importance.To reduce the risk of overfitting, we restricted the model to ten variables selected based on prior literature and biological plausibility related to cfDNA dynamics and treatment response.No post hoc variable exclusion was performed, in order to avoid introducing bias.While this approach improves interpretability in exploratory settings, overfitting remains a potential concern, and the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

We also did not assess global model performance using metrics such as area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, or specificity due to the limited sample size.Future studies with larger cohorts should incorporate these indicators to comprehensively validate predictive accuracy.

Future research should also aim to refine the timing and thresholds for measuring cfDNA changes.For instance, assessing cfDNA after the first or second NAC cycle may provide more actionable insights for early decisionmaking.Prospective trials integrating cfDNA integrity monitoring with other biomarkers, such as circulating tumor cells or protein markers, could enhance predictive accuracy.Finally, incorporating machine learning algorithms tailored to larger datasets could further improve the robustness of predictive models, addressing issues of overfitting observed in smaller cohorts.


Conclusion

In summary, this study underscores the promise of the cfDNA integrity index as a predictive biomarker for NAC response in colorectal cancer.While the findings are preliminary, they pave the way for further investigation into its broader clinical applications and biological underpinnings.Validation through larger, multicenter trials and integration with complementary biomarkers could establish the cfDNA integrity index as a cornerstone in precision oncology.
