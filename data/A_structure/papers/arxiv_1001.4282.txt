Higher order Fourier analysis as an algebraic theory III
24 Jan 2010 January 24, 2010

Balázs Szegedy 
Higher order Fourier analysis as an algebraic theory III
24 Jan 2010 January 24, 2010
For every natural number k we introduce the notion of k-th order convolution of functions on abelian groups. We study the group of convolution preserving automorphisms of function algebras in the limit. It turns out that such groups have k-nilpotent factor groups explaining why k-th order Fourier analysis has non-commutative features. To demonstrate our method in the quadratic case we develop a new quadratic representation theory on finite abelian groups. We introduce the notion of a quadrtic nil-morphism of an abelian group into a two step nil-manifold. We prove a structure theorem saying that any bounded function on a finite abelian group is decomposable into a structured part (which is the composition of a nil-morphism with a bounded complexity continuous function) and a random looking part with small U 3 norm. It implies a new inverse theorem for the U 3 norm.(The general case for U n , n ≥ 4 will be discussed in the next part of this sequence.) We point out that our framework creates interesting limit objects for functions on finite (or compact) abelian groups that are measurable functions on nil-manifolds.

introduction

In this paper we continue our program started in [7] and [8] to develop an algebraic framework for higher order Fourier analysis using limits of functions on abelian groups. Higher order characters and decompositions were studied in the first two papers. In this paper we set up the framework which is needed to understand the structure of higher order characters. Our main goal is to explain, probably the most fascinating fact, why non-commutative structures, such as k-nilpotent groups, arise when studying the U k+1 Gowers norm on abelian groups. The key ingredient is the notion of k-th order convolution defined as the expected value of S⊆[k] f S x + i∈S t i as x is taken randomly and {f S } S⊆[k] is a system of 2 k functions on the abelian group A. The k-th order convolution is a k variable function. For k = 1 it is equal to the usual convolution.

If A 1 , A 2 , . . . is a sequence of growing abelian groups then their ultra product A has a natural probability space structure with shift invariant measure. For a shift invariant σ-algebra B on A we study the subgroup G ⊂ Aut(L ∞ (B)) consisting of those automorphisms that preserve k-th order convolution for every k. It turns out that if B is embedded into F k (a canonical σ-algebra on A related to k-th order Fourier analysis) then G is a k-nilpotent group. Furthermore there is a natural way of representing the functions in L ∞ (B) on this group.

In the first chapter of this paper we prove several general results about the structure of G for every k. Note that this part uses a lots of intuition from the fundamental paper [9] by Host and Kra. In the second chapter we deal with k = 1 and k = 2. We demonstrate the efficiency of the infinite method by constructing a "quadratic representation theory" for finite abelian groups. Quite surprisingly it turns out that there is a rigid algebraic notion that we call a "quadratic nil-morphism" mapping an abelian group into a two step nil-manifold.

Ordinary Fourier analysis can be described through homomorphisms of an abelian group into the circle. However, it turns out, that in the higher order case, the natural algebraic objects are functions (called nil-morphisms) that take values in higher dimensional compact topological spaces, so called nil-manifolds. It makes it more interesting that nil-manifolds are not groups but only homogeneous spaces of groups.

Let us start with a simplified example for a quadratic nil-morphism which we call orbit representation. Let N be a two nilpotent Lie group with a discrete co-compact subgroup T and let Z be the center of N . Let M denote the left coset space of T in N . The space M is called a two step "nil-manifold". There is a simple way of representing a finite abelian group in M . Assume that some subgroup H ∈ N acts transitively on a finite point set S ⊂ C in the same way as A acts on some finite set. Using that A is abelian, we get that H/(H ∩ T ) is a homomorphic image of A and so there is an epimorphism φ : A → H/(H ∩ T ) which also defines a map ψ : A → M . We say that ψ is an orbit representation. In other words, an orbit representation is a map ψ : A → M such that for every a ∈ A there is an element n a ∈ N such that ψ(a + b) = n a ψ(b)

for every b ∈ A.

This will not be quite enough for our purposes. We will need twisted versions of orbit representations. (Note that in the higher order case a more complicated definition will be needed.) Definition 1. 1 We say that a map ψ : A → M is a quadratic nil-morphism if for every a ∈ A there is an element n a ∈ N and a homomorphism χ a : A → Z such that
ψ(a + b) = χ a (b)n a ψ(b) for every b ∈ A.

Our program is to build up Higher order Fourier analysis in terms of higher order nil-morphisms.

The main ingredient of this program is to prove a structure theorem for functions on abelian groups which is analogous to Szemerédi's regularity lemma. It says that for any bounded function f :

A → C there is a nil-morphism ψ : A → M into a bounded dimensional k step nil-manifold and a "bounded complexity function" φ : M → C such that
f (x) = h(x) + g(x) + φ(ψ(x))
where h 2 is small and g U k+1 is arbitrary small in terms of the complexity of φ and M . We will deal with the general case in the next paper.

Let us focus on the quadratic case. There are many possible ways of defining a bounded complexity function on M . A very natural choice is to prescribe a continuous embeddings v : M ֒→ R n for every nil-manifold and than say that φ : M → C has complexity at most d if there is a polynomial function p : R n → C of degree at most d such that φ(x) = p(v(x)). Out of technical reasons we also assume that the the embedding of direct products of nil-manifolds is the direct product of the embeddings. It is interesting to mention that polynomial functions and monomials are arbitrary choices for "dense enough" function systems on nilmanifolds but in some sense they seem to be quite natural. As a byproduct of our framework, an interesting limit notion for functions on abelian groups can be defined. The limit notion depends on a natural number k which measures how much information we want to preserve in the limit. For k = 1 the limit notion is only sensitive to dominant fourier coefficients. Quite interestingly for a sequence of measurable functions on compact abelian groups the limit object is again a measurable function on some compact abelian group. It can happen surprisingly, that limit of functions on the circle group is only defined on the torus. For k = 2 the limit object is a measurable function on the inverse limits of two step nil-manifolds. This limit notion comes up in this paper naturally however a much more detailed description will be given in another paper.

Finally we give a rough sketch of our method to shows where nilpotent Lie groups appear.

Step 1. (getting a two nilpotent group) Let f 1 , f 2 , . . . be a sequence of bounded functions on a sequence of growing abelian groups A 1 , A 2 , . . .. Let f be the ultra limit function on A and let f = h + g 1 + g 2 + . . . be the quadratic fourier decomposition defined in [7] and [8]. Let us take a component, say g 1 , and take the smallest shift invariant σ-algebra B on A containing g 1 . Let M be the function algebra L ∞ (B) and let G(M ) be the group of convolution preserving automorphisms of M . It follows from the first part of our paper that G(M ) is a two nilpotent group. Automorphisms of function algebras

Let (X, A, µ) be a measure space and let M be that algebra of complex valued functions in L ∞ (X) (up to 0 measure change) with respect to point wise multiplication. The algebra M is a * algebra with respect to point wise conjugation and has a trace operation defined by Tr (f ) = X f dµ.


Definition 2.3

An automorphism σ of M is a transformation satisfying the following axioms.

1. σ is an invertible linear transformation of the space L ∞ (X),
2. (f g) σ = f σ g σ , 3. (f ) σ = f σ , 4. Tr (f σ ) = Tr (f ).
The reader can easily see that automorphisms preserve the unit element. The numbers Tr (f i f j )

determine the joint moments of the real and imaginary parts of f where x is chosen randomly.

Since the distribution is compactly supported, this sequence determines the value distribution of f .

It follows that automorphisms preserve the value distribution. As a consequence they preserve all possible norms depending only on the value distribution including L 1 , L 2 and L ∞ . The fact that automorphisms are trace preserving imply in particular that scalar products are also preserved. Note that automorphisms basically behave as measure preserving transformations of X, but for us it is more convenient to use this algebraic language.

The next lemma is a useful condition to check if σ is an automorphism. The uniqueness of σ 2 follows from the fact that S is a generating system. The multiplicativity of σ 2 follows from the fact that it is multiplicative on S. Note that σ 2 preserves the Tr operation and commutes with the conjugation automatically. Almost homomorphisms

Before stating the main theorem in this chapter we need some notation. First of all we will use the metric d on the circle group C defined in a way that d(x, y) is the length of the shorter arch on C connecting x, y divided by 2π. We extend this metric to groups N that are central extensions of C. Let X → C be a random variable. We say that X is concentrated if there is a short arch of length at most 2π/3 containing all the values. The significance of concentrated random variables is that they have a well defined expected value which is between the two extremal points of the range of X on the short arch.

This expected value can be computed by considering a universal cover map p : R → C. There is a lift (defined up to a shift in Z) of X to another variable X ′ → R such that the range of X ′ is contained in an interval of length 1/3. The expected value of X ′ composed with p is a unique value. We also have that if X 1 , X 2 are both concentrated on an arch of length 2π/6 the E(X 1 X 2 ) = E(X 1 )E(X 2 ).
