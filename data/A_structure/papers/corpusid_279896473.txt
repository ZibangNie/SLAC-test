This study focuses on the performance evaluation of silicone rubber (SR) nanocomposites fabricated using industry-standard molding and thermal compression techniques.Nanocomposite insulator specimens were synthesized with nano-TiO 2 doping concentrations of 0%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7% by weight.The DC resistance measurements of the nanocomposites exhibited a distinct range from 211.33 GΩ to 161.16 GΩ with increasing nano-filler content.Notably, a 7% nano-TiO 2 loading yielded the highest dielectric constant at both 0 Hz and 2 MHz.Partial discharge (PD) testing indicated that the 5% nano-TiO 2 composite exhibited a 24.1% increase in inception voltage, signifying improved resistance to electrical stress due to the enhanced charge distribution and stabilization effects introduced by nano-filler incorporation.The observed variations in PD inception voltage were attributed to the interplay between doping levels and local charge diffusion dynamics, which modified the electric field distribution.Thermal stability assessments revealed significant modifications in degradation kinetics upon TiO 2 nanoparticle inclusion, as determined via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) demonstrated a 38% reduction in polymer flow rate between − 50 °C and 100 °C, indicative of endothermic transitions.Mechanical property evaluations showed that the 5% TiO 2 -filled composite exhibited the lowest tensile force durability (41.2 N) and tear strength (18.72 kN/m).A comprehensive performance assessment highlights the enhanced operational reliability and longevity of HTV-SR nanocomposites, making them viable candidates for outdoor highvoltage insulator applications.

The incorporation of 1 wt% nano-TiO 2 in polyurethane significantly enhanced dielectric properties, while a hybrid composition of 0.5 wt% nano and 0.5 wt% micro-TiO 2 increased the contact angle from 73° to 95° and improved electrical resistance by approximately 15% 6 .Titania-based epoxy nanocomposites with 3 wt% micro-BN and 1 wt% nano TiO 2 -SiO 2 achieved 0.49 W/mK thermal conductivity (24.4% increase) and 60.61 kV/mm dielectric strength at 10 kHz, 90 °C.Epoxy with 5 wt% BN and 1 wt% TiO 2 -SiO 2 further improved conductivity to 0.61 W/mK, making it ideal for high-frequency, high-temperature applications 7 .Authors of 8 investigated the tracking immunity of nano-TiO 2 in LSR using IEC 60,587 tests.Results showed that filler concentrations above 1 wt% did not improve performance, while 0.6 wt% exhibited the best tracking resistance, confirming an optimal NP threshold below 1 wt% for many base materials.

Another study 9 investigated zepoxy-based nanocomposites with SiO 2 and TiO 2 at varying concentrations to analyze the relationship between leakage current (LC) and partial discharge (PD).Optimal filler loadings for minimum LC and PD were found at 0.6% for SiO 2 (29.94 nA, 2.5 pC), 0.4% for TiO 2 (35.03 nA, 4.06 pC), and 0.8% for TiO 2 @SiO 2 (0.27 nA, 2.13 pC), confirming a strong direct relationship between LC and PD, making LC a valuable parameter for insulation health monitoring.Incorporating 3 phr TiO 2 nanoparticles into NBR increased tracking failure time, reduced leakage current, and enhanced dielectric properties under voltage stress, making it a promising material for electrical power system insulators 10 .The addition of TiO 2 nanoparticles up to 6 wt% in ECR glass-reinforced polyimide (PI) composites significantly enhanced mechanical properties, achieving maximum hardness and elastic modulus of 2.19 GPa and 13.99 GPa, respectively, while also reducing the dielectric constant (1.18), dielectric loss (1.14), and electrical conductivity (3.16 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm) 11 .

Hybrid fillers combining 2 phr graphene with 10 phr modified fumed silica or 20 phr TiO 2 in silicone rubber improved thermal stability and increased AC dielectric breakdown strength by 3% and 5%, respectively, highlighting their potential for outdoor insulating applications 12 .The dispersion of TiO 2 nanoparticles in mixed oils significantly enhances dielectric performance, with an increase in AC breakdown voltage (BDV) by up to 27.13%, highlighting the potential of titania-based nano-fluids for improved high-voltage insulation applications 13 .In the referenced study, the authors focused on relatively high filler loadings (5-15% by weight), concluding that optimal improvements in thermal and electrical properties were observed within the 5-8% range, without significantly compromising mechanical performance 14 .However, this study leaves a knowledge gap regarding the behavior of the material at lower filler concentrations, especially below 5%.

To address this gap and explore the potential for achieving similar or even superior property enhancements with minimal filler content, we selected a novel range of lower TiO 2 concentrations (0%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7%).Insulation properties of SR nanocomposites have been investigated with application of partial discharge (PD) signal for the noise analysis which depicts the insulation condition.This work explores strategies to optimize the balance between thermal and electrical properties in filled composites, emphasizing the role of filler morphology, core-shell structures, and advanced thermally conductive fillers, while providing insights into recent advancements for applications in electrical insulation systems.


Materials purchase and nanocomposites fabrication


Material acquisition

Research-grade HTV silicone rubber was procured from HRS Company in Tokyo, Japan.The procurement cost was estimated to be 148 $/kg, and 5 nm nano titanium dioxide particles were purchased from Macklin Company in Mexico and China at the rate of 214 $/mg.The fabrication of nanocomposites included industrial-grade dicumyl peroxide (DCP) and color pigments for the project 15 .


Nanocomposites fabrication

SR was blended with nano-TiO 2 filler using a 20 HP roll mill mixer for 30 min at 32 rpm.DCP (5%/kg of SR) was added before mixing with nano-TiO 2 , followed by molding at 180 °C for 10 min.The insulator samples were tested using MDR analysis to calculate the optimum cure time (t 90 ) according to ASTM D2084.Fabricated insulator samples have a diameter of 80 mm and a width of 2 mm, with a round-circle shape.Nanocomposite insulator samples are presented with the respective nano-filler concentration along with code and cure time in


Performance analysis of nanocomposites


Measurement of resistance

DC resistance of SR nanocomposites was measured using a Tettex Precision Oil and Solid Dielectric Analyzer 2830/2831 from Haefely Test AG, Switzerland.The measurement setup involved assessing the current flow through the sample and calculating the DC resistance.To minimize the impact of atmospheric conditions on dielectric measurements, the sample holder unit was enclosed within an airtight glass container.Samples were compressed within the holder under a pressure of 2 MPa to ensure airtight conditions.DC resistance measurements were conducted over a temperature range of 28 °C to 60 °C at 50 Hz.


Dielectric properties W.r.t. Temperature

A double vector-meter approach is used to calculate relative permittivity and tan delta.The sample holder device is enclosed in a large glass jar to eliminate air from dielectric measurements.Samples were forcefully crushed between holders to maintain 2 MPa pressure.Dielectric characteristics were investigated at 50 Hz from 28 °C to 60 °C.


Dielectric properties W.r.t. Frequency

LCR Meter 7600 Plus, a device manufactured by IET Labs in the United States, evaluated the dielectric characteristics with request to frequency, including the dissipation loss factor and dielectric constant.


Dielectric breakdown

Breakdown strength of SR composites was evaluated using the Hipotronics D149-DI AC dielectric breakdown tester from Shelton, CT, USA by IEC 60243-1 and IEC 60243-2.The breakdown voltage tests were conducted using a Haefely Hipotronics tester following ASTM D149, with spherical brass electrodes (25 mm diameter) and a 2 mm gap.The AC voltage was ramped at 500 V/s until breakdown occurred.Tests were performed at 23 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity, with samples conditioned for 24 h prior.The average of multiple readings was reported for accuracy.


STRI hydrophobicity classification

Swedish Transmission Research Institute (STRI) classifies hydrophobicity into seven levels, from HC-1 (highest) to HC-7 (lowest).Samples were sprayed with distilled water at a 45º angle for 20-30 s, and images were taken within 10 s using an HD camera.The images were compared to the STRI standard to assess hydrophobicity by analyzing water droplet shapes and wet areas as a percentage of the total surface.


Contact angle measurement

This method measures the angle between a water droplet and the flat surface of the insulator, with a higher angle indicating greater hydrophobicity.The VCA OPTIMA instrument from ASTP was used, employing a 0.5 µL distilled water droplet.


Leakage current measurement

A custom setup with a power supply was used to measure the AC leakage current.A resistor was connected in series with the sample, and a millimeter measured the leakage voltage.These voltage values were then converted to leakage current using Ohm's law 16 .The measurement circuit incorporates a resistor in series with the sample to limit the current flow.In the event of a flashover or breakdown, this resistor serves as a current limiter, ensuring that excessive current does not reach the measurement equipment.


Partial discharge measurement

Partial discharges (PD) were measured according to IEC standard 60,270.A coupling capacitor detected the current waveform proportional to discharge pulses.The measuring impedance unit converted this current into a low-voltage signal for the partial discharge detector 15 .


Samples


Mechanical testing

A Universal Testing Machine (UTM) was utilized to perform mechanical testing by the ASTM D412 requirements.The experiment involved cutting dumbbell-shaped samples from SR sheets that were 2 mm thick.These samples were then tested using a tensile testing machine (Model Z010, Zwick, Germany) at 25 ± 2 °C and a crosshead speed of 500 mm/min, following the guidelines outlined in ASTM D412.The dimensions of our sample were 32 mm in length, 6 mm in width, and 2 mm in thickness.


Thermal testing

Nanocomposites were studied for thermal behavior under the performance of thermo-gravimetric analysis (TGA) analysis, in which the weight of the material as a function of temperature was noted for thermal stability and decomposition temperatures.Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) investigated the heat flow rate of the nanocomposites for phase transitions.


Results and discussions


Dielectric constant

The dielectric constant of SR nanocomposites is illustrated in Fig. 1.The dielectric constant exhibits a significant linear trend as the frequency increases, as indicated by the graphical results obtained concerning temperature.Over the temperature range, SRT1 and SRT7 exhibit the maximum dielectric constants.The dielectric constants of SR0 and SRT3 are the lowest.The dielectric constant of all samples was within the range of 2.5 to 3 for frequencies up to 2 MHz.The structural changes in the SR matrix during the frequency-based analysis are demonstrated by the slight change in the dielectric constant of SRT1 at 1.1 MHz.Nano TiO 2 particles are uniformly distributed within the SRT1 and SRT7.This uniform dispersion enables a more robust interface between the nanoparticles and the matrix, which leads to enhanced polarization effects across a wider frequency range 17 .

Fig. 1.Dielectric constant measurement for all nanocomposites.

At low frequencies, the dielectric constant is significantly higher, especially for samples with higher nano-TiO 2 content (SRT7 > SRT5 > SRT3 > SRT1 > SR0).This is attributed to interfacial Maxwell-Wagner polarization, which dominates at low frequencies due to charge accumulation at the filler-matrix interfaces.As the nano-TiO 2 content increases, more interfaces are created, enhancing polarization effects and thus increasing the dielectric constant 18 .As frequency increases, the dielectric constant decreases and stabilizes beyond ~ 500 kHz.This decline is due to the inability of dipolar and interfacial polarization mechanisms to keep pace with the rapidly oscillating electric field 19 .At high frequencies, only electronic and atomic polarization remain active, which contribute less to the overall dielectric constant.The increasing trend of the dielectric constant with higher nano-TiO 2 loading across all frequencies highlights the role of nano-TiO 2 with high intrinsic permittivity and enhanced interfacial polarization effects.


DC resistance

DC resistance of nano-TiO 2 filled silicone rubber (SR) composites is presented in Table 3.At 30 °C, the nanocomposites demonstrate significantly higher resistance values, which decrease sharply at 60 °C.Despite this decline, the resistance values remain relatively high compared to the literature showing the excellent insulating properties of the manufactured nanocomposites.At 30 °C, the resistance values for SRT1, SRT3, SRT5, and SRT7 are 441.27GΩ, 482.48 GΩ, 382.33 GΩ, and 381.27GΩ, respectively.At 60 °C, SRT3 exhibits a notable drop to 198.66 GΩ, while SRT7 shows the lowest resistance at 161.16 GΩ.The reduction in DC resistance at elevated temperatures, particularly in SRT3 and SRT7, is attributed to the activation of additional charge carriers.This enhancement in electron mobility is facilitated by the nano-TiO 2 particles, which improve charge carrier mobility, dispersion, and interfacial interactions 20 .


Tan delta vs. temperature

Tan Delta measurement of SR loaded with nano-TiO 2 at various ranges has been examined, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Tan delta of SRT is the highest in the range of 0.14 to 0.157, increasing linearly from 30 °C to 60 °C.tan delta of 0.105 was observed in SRT3 at 30 °C, while it was approximately 0.1 at 60 °C.Interfacial contacts between the SR matrix and the nano-TiO 2 particles transform at elevated temperatures, increasing frictional losses within the material.


Permittivity vs. temperature

The permittivity of SR nanocomposites with nano-TiO 2 as a filler has been examined and the results are illustrated in Fig. 2. The permittivity of SRT7 and SRT5 is the maximum.The higher values of nano-TiO 2 loading indicate the improved structural dispersion between the matrix of nano-TiO 2 .The increase in relative permittivity with temperature in HTV SR filled with nano-TiO 2 can be attributed to the interaction between the nanoparticles and the silicone rubber matrix.As the temperature rises, the mobility of the silicone rubber polymer chains increases, leading to enhanced interfacial polarization between the nano-TiO 2 particles and the polymer matrix.This interfacial polarization is a key factor in increasing the dielectric constant.At higher temperatures, the thermal energy activates these polarizable sites, further contributing to the rise in relative permittivity 21 .The effect is more pronounced with higher nano-TiO 2 loading (e.g., 5%, 7%) due to the increased number of nanoparticles and their interactions with the polymer matrix.The permittivity of SRT5 and SRT7 is 6.23 and 6.45 at 30 °C, respectively, while it is 7 and 6.8 at 60 °C.Greater polarization within the material was the consequence of increased nano-TiO 2 influx.Nanoparticles have aligned themselves in response to an electric field.The total polarization and permittivity are subsequently increased due to nanoparticle availability to align at higher loadings 17,22 .


Dielectric breakdown

AC breakdown strength was analyzed using the D149 standard approach on a Haefely dielectric breakdown tester.This standardized testing process enables the calculation of the dielectric breakdown voltage.The breakdown voltage of neat SR0 is 12.73 kV as mentioned in Table 4.A minor increase of 13.20 kV was observed in SRT1.Breakdown strength decreases to 12.21 kV/mm for SRT3, indicating a reduction in the durability of the dielectric.A substantial increase was observed for SRT5, where it reached 13.45 kV/mm.This was followed by a modest increase to 13.65 kV/mm for SRT7.These findings indicate a nonlinear relationship between TiO 2 content and dielectric performance, as evidenced by the fluctuating breakdown strength of TiO 2 concentration.


Contact angle measurement

Contact angle measurement was performed for the nano-TiO 2 filled SR composites, as shown in Fig. 3. Results show that SRT5 had the highest contact angle among all composites with a value of 126.6°, which shows the most hydrophobic nature.While SR0, SRT1 and SRT7 had a similar hydrophobic nature as investigated by contact angle analysis.SRT3 had a contact angle value of 144.4° as the SRT3 improved the hydrophobic nature increases due to its low surface energy, non-polar methyl groups, and surface roughness, which repel water molecules 23 .


STRI classification method

SR0 samples had the highest STRI class of HC3, which shows the most hydrophilic nature among all the nano-TiO 2 filled composites.SRT1, SRT3 and SRT5 had the HC1 class of hydrophobicity, while SRT7 showed HC2 class hydrophobicity as mentioned in Fig. 3.


Leakage current measurement

The ac leakage current of SRT3 was the highest at 4 mA when 2.5 kV was applied, while SR0 had the lowest at 3 mA as depicted in Fig. 4. The leakage current was 5.3 mA in nearly all of the samples at 5 kV.SRT1 exhibited the lowest leakage current of 0.38µA at a DC voltage of 3 kV, while SRT5 and SRT7 had values of 0.07µA.SRT1 had the lowest leakage current value of 0.18µA, while SR0 had a 0.24µA leakage current at the maximum applied voltage of 5kV 24 .


Partial discharge measurement

The partial discharge (PD) analysis provided inception voltage (IV), extinction voltage (EV), and overflow voltage (OV) measurements for each sample is presented in Table 5, offering critical information about the electrical performance of nano-TiO 2 filled SR nanocomposites.SRT3, one of the most intriguing samples under investigation, exhibited an Inception Voltage of 1.99 kV, an Extinction Voltage of 2.12 kV, and an Overflow Voltage of 4.71 kV.SRT3 exhibited the least level of PD noise among all the samples, which suggests that it is more stable and resistant to partial discharges 25 .SRT3 consistently exhibited superior electrical performance in all examined metrics.The decreased inception voltage suggests an increased threshold for partial discharges to commence.Exceptional PD characteristics of SRT3 are essential for applications requiring high electrical dependability and reduced susceptibility to electrical breakdown 26 .The results in Fig. 5 emphasize the potential of SR nanocomposites infused with nano-TiO 2 .Specifically, they point to SRT3 as a viable alternative for electrical insulation applications.In addition to illustrating the efficiency of nano-TiO 2 as a filler, the observed reduction in PD noise also contributes significant context to the comprehension of nanocomposite materials in the context of electrical insulation.SRT5 has the highest overflow voltage with the lowest extinction voltage and the PD starts to appear at 1.42 kV, which shows the presence of PD noise in the cavities of SRT5 matrix.The incorporation of nano-titania fillers improves partial discharge resistance by increasing conductivity and reducing trap depth, leading to higher partial discharge inception voltage and fewer discharges.Nano-titania also affects the electric field distribution, enhancing resistance to electrical tree initiation 27 .These improvements are attributed to changes in trap density and non-linear conductivity as doping levels increase.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis

DSC profile for SR0 exhibits unique characteristics within the temperature range of -50 °C to 100 °C.These consist of an initial endothermic process associated with the glass transition, a peak at 120 °C that indicates the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the SR matrix, and a subsequent exothermic process at 220 °C that may be associated with crystallization.The early endothermic activity that is characteristic of the glass transition area is demonstrated by the decrease in flow rate from − 0.15 to -0.SR nanocomposites 28 .The DSC analysis of SRT7 indicates that thermal changes occur at a slowed pace.During the glass transition area, the flow rate decreases from − 0.15 to -0.25 between − 50 °C and 100 °C, suggesting early endothermic activity.This steady flow rate of approximately − 0.22 from 220 °C to 350 °C is consistent with previous research on the thermal behavior of nanocomposites with higher nano-filler concentrations 29 .


Thermo-gravimetric analysis

The early phases of thermal breakdown are indicated by the decrease in weight% from 100 to 95% at 450 °C.Prior research has demonstrated that nanocomposites experience greater weight loss due to the accelerated disintegration processes induced by nanofillers.The literature suggests that nano additives may contribute to early degradation by serving as nucleation sites for heat disintegration.The stable phase during disintegration is reflected in the consistent weight% of 38% across all composites within this temperature range.The notable reduction in weight loss for SR0 (36-39%) is likely due to the absence of nano-TiO 2 , which affects thermal stability.Adding nanoparticles modifies degradation kinetics, with the results indicating that the decomposition process is regulated and influenced by the nano-TiO 2 content.The weight% of SRT3 increases to SRT7 as the temperature exceeds 600 °C, suggesting a substantial weight loss in nanocomposites with a higher nano-TiO 2 content, as shown in the results in Fig. 7.The synergistic effects of nano additives may result in accelerated thermal degradation of nanocomposites at high temperatures.The observed pattern follows prior research, underscoring the significance of nano-TiO 2 in the modification of the thermal stability of the SR matrix.


Mechanical testing

The mechanical properties of a material depend much on its tensile endurance or the maximum stress it can bear before failure.Figure 8 shows the mechanical stress results applied by UTM machine for nanocomposites.Table 6 elaborates all the mechanical testing results.Up to a strain of 3.8, the stress-strain curve form SR0 exhibits a linear connection; composite breakdown follows from there.By contrast, SRT1 samples show a strain value of 4.3 under linear load applied by UTM.Graphical statistics for SRT3 expose a strain ranging up to 3.8.The stressstrain curve graphically indicates that SRT5 and SRT7 have almost equal strain values of around 3.68.Strong inter-facial contacts between nano-TiO 2 , with its great surface area and aspect ratio, effectively enhance the SR matrix, so increasing load-bearing capacity and tensile strength.According to 20 , effective reinforcing depends on good dispersion of nanofillers inside the SR matrix to minimize agglomeration and ensure homogeneous Fig. 7. Discussion

The dielectric constant of SR nanocomposites exhibited significant frequency-dependent behavior, with SRT1 and SRT7 showing superior performance.The uniform dispersion of nano-TiO 2 particles in these samples enhanced the nanoparticle-matrix interface, enabling effective polarization over a broad frequency range.This enhanced polarization underscores the role of nano-TiO 2 in promoting consistent dielectric properties, which is beneficial for applications requiring stable performance across varied frequencies.The slight changes observed in the dielectric constant at higher frequencies also affirm the structural stability of the composite matrix, further validating the advantages of nano-TiO 2 inclusion.

The resistance revealed a significant temperature dependency, with all composites exhibiting reduced resistance at elevated temperatures.SRT3 showed the steepest decline in resistance, which can be attributed to the activation of additional charge carriers at higher temperatures.This behavior highlights the role of nano-TiO 22 in enhancing electron mobility and improving charge carrier dispersion.The relatively high resistance values, even at elevated temperatures, confirm the effective insulating capabilities of the nanocomposites.These findings suggest that these materials are ideal for applications requiring reliable insulation under varying thermal conditions, particularly where high resistance at elevated temperatures is essential.


Conclusion

This study systematically examines the electrical, mechanical, and thermal properties of high-voltage silicone rubber (SR) nanocomposites, providing critical insights into the effects of varying nano-TiO 2 concentrations compared to the unfilled SR₀ baseline.The DC resistance measurements of the nanocomposites exhibited a distinct range from 211.33 GΩ to 161.16 GΩ with increasing nano-TiO 2 content, demonstrating a significant influence on electrical performance.The 7% nano-TiO 2 -loaded composite (SRT7) exhibited the highest dielectric constant at both 0 Hz and 2 MHz, with enhancements of 60% and 20%, respectively, relative to SR₀.However, partial discharge (PD) testing revealed a reduction in PD resistance for SRT7, suggesting a potential limitation of higher filler concentrations despite their dielectric improvements.Conversely, the 5% TiO 2 -filled nanocomposite (SRT5) demonstrated a 24.1% increase in PD inception voltage, indicating improved charge stabilization within the polymer matrix.However, this formulation exhibited the lowest mechanical durability, with tensile strength and tear strength recorded at 41.2 N and 18.72 kN/m, respectively.This highlights a critical trade-off between enhanced electrical performance and compromised mechanical robustness.These findings emphasize the necessity of a balanced approach in optimizing nanocomposite formulations, where increased filler loading must be carefully evaluated against mechanical integrity, particularly tensile strength and elongation at break.Future research should explore finer concentration gradients and the synergistic effects of multi-nanoparticle systems to mitigate mechanical limitations while maximizing the multifunctional performance of these nanocomposites for high-voltage insulation applications.
