Tobacco treatment billing and tobacco use disorder diagnosis in healthcare settings in the United States: an analysis of South Carolina medicaid claims
5D8C019B8CEEFD906C611F5A4A6F82CB10.1186/s13011-025-00654-wReceived: 2 July 2024 / Accepted: 29 May 2025SmokingSmoking cessationMedicareMedicaid
Background Tobacco use, particularly smoking, remains the leading cause of preventable death in the United States.While Medicaid in all states provides some coverage of tobacco cessation treatments, rates of tobacco use remain much higher among Medicaid beneficiaries (30.0%) compared to those with private insurance (18.0%).The extent to which Medicaid beneficiaries receive cessation counseling services remains unclear.The current study assessed tobacco counseling occurrence among individuals who use tobacco among South Carolina Medicaid fee-for-service beneficiaries from 2019 to 2022.Methods Individuals with a tobacco use disorder diagnosis were identified (N = 49,401) and the differences in the number of patients counseled between demographic groups and the types of providers delivering counseling were assessed.Between-group differences in receiving counseling were assessed using Chi-squared tests.Fewer Black (χ2 = 34.54,23.51, 8.54, 12.02, p < 0.5) and younger (χ2 = 81.43,117.45, 83.25, 78.98, p < 0.0001) beneficiaries received counseling across all four years compared to their White and older counterparts, respectively.Additionally, fewer individuals in rural areas (χ2 = 12.44, 4.05, 5.07, p < 0.05) received counseling compared to those in urban areas in years 2019-2021.There were additional sex and regional differences in some years.ResultsConclusions To improve cessation rates, focusing on increasing availability of cessation counseling services to Medicaid beneficiaries is critical.

Background

Smoking remains the leading cause of preventable death in the United States (U.S.) [1].Current treatment guidelines, such as The Public Health Service Clinical Practice Guidelines for Treating Tobacco Use and Dependence [2], outline evidence-based strategies for treating tobacco dependence.These strategies include brief interventions such as physician brief advice or the 5 A's (Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange), as well as more comprehensive treatment approaches such as individual counseling plus pharmacotherapy (e.g., varenicline, nicotine replacement therapy [NRT]).However, the reach of tobacco treatment often falls short, particularly among under-resourced and socially vulnerable populations (e.g., lower socioeconomic status, uninsured, rural, and individuals from racial or ethnic minority populations) [3], which subsequently perpetuates disparities in tobacco-related health concerns.

Providers delivering tobacco cessation-related care may have a lack of training, limited time, and limited reimbursement for such services, which may prevent treatment from being rendered [4].Additional training is needed to ensure providers are equipped with the skills to effectively deliver tobacco counseling [5].For those who have appropriate training and feel equipped to deliver treatment, tobacco counseling is a billable service for providers with a National Provider Identifier (NPI) number.However, there are numerous hurdles to overcome to ensure this treatment is accessible.Rates of tobacco use are higher among individuals with less access to resources, such as those with lower incomes or lower levels of education [6].Subsequently, these individuals bear a greater burden of tobacco on morbidity and mortality [7][8][9].Accessing tobacco treatment is also difficult among under-resourced individuals due to systemic barriers in the healthcare system that prevent many individuals from accessing health services, including tobacco treatment [4].Indeed, from 2000 to 2015, fewer than one-third of individuals who smoked used an evidencebased cessation strategy to help them quit smoking [3].The systemic barriers precluding access to tobacco treatment include issues such as not having enough providers or having providers inadequately distributed throughout a region [10].There is also a significant racial bias in healthcare settings, and there is a longstanding history of distrust of healthcare settings among Black and African American individuals [11].

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) are a U.S. entity that provides healthcare coverage to individuals with low incomes, children, pregnant women, elderly adults, and people with disabilities [12].Each state currently has a Medicaid program that may vary in coverage policies.However, every state's Medicaid program does cover some tobacco cessation treatments.The current study uses data from Medicaid beneficiaries in South Carolina, which provides coverage for four tobacco treatment counseling sessions per quit attempt for up to two quit attempts each year for Medicaid beneficiaries who use tobacco [13].While South Carolina, along with 21 other states, provides comprehensive coverage of tobacco cessation treatments, barriers to accessing coverage for treatments are still prevalent [14].Studies show that those enrolled in Medicaid have lower recent cessation rates (i.e., quit smoking in the last 6-12 months) than those with private health insurance and have cessation rates below the national average (5.9% versus 7.4%) [3].Indeed, previous studies have shown that tobacco cessation counseling rates among fee-for-service Medicaid beneficiaries vary by state, but are consistently low, ranging from 0.1 to 5.6% [15].Given that current national tobacco use rates (any tobacco product) among individuals enrolled in Medicaid are approximately 30.0%, which is higher than the 18.0% of tobacco use among individuals with private health insurance [6], broader implementation of tobacco cessation treatment could help to significantly lower the $40 billion spent on smokingrelated diseases [1].

Given the low rates of counseling and successful cessation attempts among Medicaid beneficiaries, it is important to learn more about cessation services provided among individuals enrolled in Medicaid to better understand how often tobacco treatment services are provided among this vulnerable population and differences in treatment service provision between diverse groups of people.The present study aimed to determine the prevalence of tobacco treatment counseling among individuals enrolled in Medicaid in South Carolina with a tobacco use disorder diagnosis and identify disparities in receiving counseling services.


Methods


Participants and procedures

Data were obtained from South Carolina Medicaid administrative claims for the 2019 to 2022 period.This work was approved and deemed exempt from continued Institutional Review Board (IRB) oversight by the Medical University of South Carolina's IRB.Medicaid claims data analysis is solely that of the research team and have not been verified by the South Carolina Department of Health and Human Services.

Fee-for-service Medicaid beneficiaries who were classified as currently using a nicotine or tobacco product were identified.We did not include managed care beneficiaries, consistent with previous studies using Medicaid data [15,16].Adult beneficiaries enrolled in fee-for-service were included because there is no capitation in this service delivery model, allowing for a more accurate estimate of service utilization than managed care.In other words, managed care providers have no incentive to add counseling codes since they will not receive additional reimbursement from Medicaid; they receive a flat fee for providing services to a managed care beneficiary.Fee-forservice providers do have incentive to add these codes when tobacco counseling services are rendered because they do receive additional reimbursement.

Beneficiaries were classified as currently using a tobacco or nicotine product if they had one of the following International Classification of Disease (ICD)-10 codes listed as a diagnosis at any point over the four-year period: O99.33 (tobacco use disorder complicating pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium), Z72.0 (tobacco use not otherwise specified), U07.0 (vaping-related disorders), or any of the codes listed under the F17 parent code (parent code for nicotine dependence).Beneficiaries were classified as having received tobacco counseling if they had Current Procedural Terminology codes 99,406 (tobacco or smoking counseling that lasts 3-10 min) or 99,407 (tobacco or smoking counseling that lasts more than 10 min).

Variables of interest included age, sex, race, region of the state in which the beneficiary resided (Lowcountry [including Charleston, which is considered urban], Midlands, Pee Dee, and Upstate, of which the latter three are largely rural), and rurality.Rurality was defined using the Office of Management and Budget's 2010 update.Urban counties were defined as those included in either a Metropolitan or Micropolitan Statistical Area, and rural counties comprised all others.


Statistical analyses

Chi-squared tests of independence were used to determine whether there is any relationship between the characteristics of interest (age, sex, race, rurality, or region) and beneficiaries who received tobacco treatment counseling, and Cramer's V or Phi coefficients were calculated.We conducted analyses within each year rather than combining all years because beneficiaries are eligible for up to eight tobacco counseling services (four per quit attempt, up to two quit attempts) each year.If an individual were to receive counseling across two years, they would be counted as two different individuals if data from all years was combined, as the data were deidentified in a way that made assessing if the same individual was present across multiple years impossible.Thus, we aggregated by year.All analyses were conducted using SAS, Enterprise Edition, release 3.81.


Results

Descriptive statistics of the sample are shown in Table 1.The numbers within each year in Tables 1 and 2 reflect unique beneficiaries.Overall, the percentage of beneficiaries with a tobacco or nicotine product use diagnosis who received tobacco counseling is low across all four years (2019-2022): 7.5%, 8.6%, 9.1%, and 8.5%, respectively.Table 2 displays results of Chi-squared results testing associations between demographic characteristics and receipt of counseling.The Chi-squared results suggested significant differences in the percentage of people who received counseling between different racial backgrounds, age groups, and region of residence across all four years, although the coefficient of association (Cramer's V/Phi coefficients) in all cases is low.There were also significant differences between rural and urban residents from 2019 to 2021, and between males and females from 2021 to 2022.Fewer Black and younger beneficiaries received counseling across all four years compared to their White and older counterparts, respectively.


Discussion

Many fee-for-service Medicaid beneficiaries identified as currently using tobacco or nicotine products may not be receiving cessation counseling, or at least are not being assigned tobacco counseling codes.This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating low cessation counseling usage among fee-for-service Medicaid beneficiaries nationally [15].The present results suggest that increasing tobacco counseling particularly among beneficiaries who are younger, Black, and in rural areas could improve counseling rates and reduce tobacco-related disparities.Differences between the percentage of males and females receiving counseling codes were significant in 2021 and 2022, but differences were minimal (differences of less than 1% in both years).

Results from other studies examining smoking cessation specifically among Medicaid beneficiaries have found varying success with cessation depending on the state and the cessation coverage policies that have been implemented.As stated previously, each state has their own Medicaid program and, while each state has tobacco cessation coverage policies, the specifics of these policies differ.In states that have expanded the eligibility criteria for Medicaid coverage, and thus cover more people, there are lower rates of smoking [17,18].In states that required counseling in order for medications to be covered, there were also lower rates of smoking [18].In South Carolina, counseling is not a requirement for receiving medications.It may be beneficial to implement informational campaigns so that Medicaid beneficiaries are aware that counseling coverage is an option or encourage state Quit Line engagement (e.g., targeted advertising, mass mailings, incentives for calling the state Quit Line, etc [16].Additionally, removing co-pays and expanding coverage to include additional cessation attempts and cover additional cessation options (e.g.nicotine inhaler, nasal spray) may help to promote further smoking cessation [19].

White beneficiaries consistently received a higher percentage of counseling codes compared to Black or African American beneficiaries, and it is important for researchers and clinicians alike to ask why this discrepancy is occurring, especially given that groups such as Black or African American communities have higher distrust of medical institutions and there is racial bias  in healthcare settings [11].These differences in rates of counseling among people from different groups indicates that there is significant room for improvement related to health equity and tobacco cessation.One way to combat potential bias in healthcare settings related to tobacco cessation is implementing standard assessment procedures for tobacco use for all patients at all visits, and providing training to ensure questions are asked in identical ways each time.However, it may also be important to provide those who administer tobacco cessation counseling with training related to bias and multicultural sensitivity to improve patient/provider interactions.Standardized questions may not earn an honest response to tobacco related questions due to factors such as distrust, but further training about multicultural considerations may help equip providers with skills to navigate the unique considerations of different groups and improve the frequency and quality of tobacco assessment and help promote health equity.Fewer tobacco counseling codes were entered for younger adults compared to older adults.This is surprising because older adult smoking rates have been stagnant, while other age groups have decreased [20][21][22].This also raises the question of why so few young adults are being counseled, or if they are declining counseling.It may also be the case that younger adults were given tobacco or nicotine use diagnostic codes because they use electronic cigarettes, as young adults have the highest rates of e-cigarette use [6] and providers may not know of another way to code use of this product or how to treat it.Further research is needed to better understand how to engage different age groups in cessation counseling in the medical, dental, and behavioral health settings, and other areas where individuals may receive care (e.g., pharmacies) for different tobacco and nicotine products, as well as how to educate providers about novel products and accurately documenting nicotine product use.

Individuals from rural areas also had significantly fewer tobacco counseling codes entered compared to their urban counterparts from 2019 to 2021.There are healthcare shortages in rural areas due to not having enough providers, poor distribution of providers across geographic locations, problems with infrastructure, etc. [10], which may contribute to providers not having the time or resources to provide tobacco counseling if they are tasked with specific and more immediate medical issues during a patient visit.Thus, it may be prudent to identify and train other clinic staff or other medical professionals to treat tobacco and nicotine product use and submit reimbursement for these healthcare services.From a tobacco control perspective this would allow clinics with fewer resources to designate a tobacco cessation "champion, " [23] or person who is tasked with making smoking cessation counseling a priority and increase reach of evidence-based interventions among at-risk populations such as those in rural areas.This would require policy changes to allow medical professionals who are not currently reimbursed for tobacco cessation services to be able to enter these service codes.

Another potential solution for increasing tobacco counseling rates is to consider providing Tobacco Treatment Specialist (TTS) training for providers so that they have the knowledge and skills necessary to continue providing effective tobacco counseling.Additionally, adding this training to the curricula for nursing or medical school, or certified medical assistant (CMA) training may help to expose providers and medical staff to tobacco treatment approaches early in their careers and lay the foundation for tobacco treatment as a standard care practice.For those providers who are already established in their clinical practice, one important consideration is where TTS trainings are occurring.These trainings are often hosted by academic medical centers, which are not typically located in rural areas [24].This may act as a barrier for providers in rural areas to obtain this training, which could potentially further geographic disparities in tobacco treatment and health outcomes.However, some training sites offer online options that may allow for providers in areas to receive the training in a way that better suits their time and resource needs, and efforts to increase awareness about these programs may be important to engage more providers [25].

Beneficiaries for the Lowcountry region (including Charleston, SC, which is an urban area) consistently had the lowest counseling codes across the study period.This is surprising given that an National Cancer Institute (NCI)-designated cancer center is located in this region and houses a Tobacco Treatment Program that provides counseling and pharmacological services to promote smoking cessation.More local research is needed to better understand why these state-specific differences may exist.


Limitations

Limitations of this study include that claims data do not show if individuals received tobacco treatment from providers that was not documented by counseling codes.Providers may have referred patients to other services or prescribed medications without inputting a counseling code.We are also unable to ascertain if pharmacotherapy products are being used by the beneficiary, as the claims and diagnostic codes do not include pharmacological data, and we are unable to determine if medications were prescribed, or picked up and used by beneficiaries.Future research may also benefit from assessing the rates of pharmacological therapy use among beneficiaries.Further, our calculations may underestimate the number of Medicaid beneficiaries who engage in tobacco use in South Carolina, as many providers may not add this diagnostic code, may not ask all patients about their tobacco use, or patients may not disclose this information.In addition, managed care beneficiaries were excluded given that there is no additional reimbursement when managed care providers use counseling codes.Thus, they may be less likely to enter codes.Lastly, the coefficients of association (Cramer's V and Phi coefficients) in all cases were low, indicating that significant findings may be a result of the large sample or indicating there is a more complex relationship between the variables tested and receiving tobacco counseling.However, due to the deidentification of the data, we were unable to conduct multivariate analyses.Future research would benefit from multivariate analyses to determine which considerations are associated with tobacco counseling, or lack thereof, among Medicaid beneficiaries.


Conclusions

There is significant room for improvement in providing tobacco counseling among Medicaid beneficiaries.Providing additional tobacco treatment and reimbursement training among medical providers, specifically those serving patients least likely to be counseled (e.g., Black, young, or rural), would expand the reach of tobacco treatment in medical settings.
