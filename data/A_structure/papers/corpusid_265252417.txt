This paper contains a normative interpretation and critique of Palantir's expansion into the health domain by using the conceptual lens of 'sphere transgressions'.The technology company, known for its activities in the sphere of security, expanded into the health domain during the pandemic, providing software to monitor the spread of Covid-19.In 2019 Palantir was severely criticized by human rights organizations for its role in Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) under the Trump administration.Activists and politicians worry about Palantir's move into health.However, critique is often limited to data protection.These concerns fail to grasp the risks and harms of this expansion.In this paper, I explore the risks of Palantir's expansion into the health sphere using Sharon's sphere transgressions framework as a conceptual lens and critical tool to understand and judge this move.We should anticipate the risks of no public returns, dominance, and new dependencies.While this might be true for many Big Tech actors, I add that Palantir's expansion might be particularly pernicious.Palantir's history reveals the perversion of logics under exclusionary politics in the sphere of security.At the very least, this warrants special vigilance when importing their technologies into the sphere of health.I conclude that the sphere transgressions framework reveals risks beyond data protection regarding Palantir's expansion into the health domain.

Introduction

This paper contains a normative interpretation and critique of Palantir's expansion into the health domain by using the conceptual lens of 'sphere transgressions'.Palantir is a technology company that designs software and platforms to 'empower' organizations with highly complex and sensitive data environments (Palantir Homepage, 2022).The enterprise is active across public, private, and nonprofit sectors and involved in many different societal domains, although originally, their software is used in the field of security (e.g., the US army, domestic policing and Homeland Security).During the pandemic, Palantir forged collaborations in the health domain.Activities include providing software to monitor the spread of Covid-19 for the National Institutes of Health (US) and the National Health Service (UK).

In 2019 Palantir was severely criticized by human rights organizations for the role it played in Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) under the Trump administration.Politicians and activists worry about Palantir expanding its activities into the health domain.However, critique is often limited to data protection and market concerns which fail to grasp other risks and harms of expansionism.

In this paper, I use the conceptual lens of 'sphere transgressions' to judge Palantir's expansion into the health domain building on the work of philosopher of technology Tamar Sharon.Roughly, the concept of 'sphere transgression' indicates normative concerns with Big Tech actors that expand their activities from one 'sphere' or social domain, such as security, into another, such as health, creating dominance and dependencies across spheres.Understanding Palantir's expansion into health from this perspective reveals new risks beyond data protection.Central to these risks is the importation of logics and values that structure the sphere of security into the sphere of health in a way that undermines the values and logics of the health sphere.This transgression is enabled by Palantir's position as a powerful technology company who controls dominant goods and its technological platforms that are perceived to be 'neutral'.While these risks are problematic as such, I add a normative argument that Palantir's expansion into the health sphere is particularly pernicious because the company has developed its power, expertise, and technology under exclusionary politics in the sphere of security.Moreover, this has resulted in a perversion of security logics.I anticipate the risk and potential harms of importing technologies from the sphere of security, that aims to efficiently monitor and target suspicious behavior, into the sphere of health, that aims to care for the vulnerable.

While this is a conceptual argument, drawing from philosophy, political and social science, STS, media and surveillance studies, it is informed by public discourse on Palantir's activities in the domains of health and security.I collected press releases on Palantir's website about collaborations in health as well as their mission statements on civil rights, ethics and data protection. 1 Furthermore, I collected reports of civil and human rights organizations as well as newspapers and journalistic websites that covered and evaluated Palantir's activities in both these domains.

I support this argument in four steps.First (section 2-4), I briefly present Palantir's activities in the security domain and its role in the health domain.Then, I introduce the controversy around Palantir's activities in the security domain and I present the main critique of Palantir's expansion into health, which revolves around data protection.I argue that data protection critique is important but not sufficient to address the risks of this move.

Second (section 5-6), I introduce Sharon's sphere transgressions framework as a lens to identify risks of Big Tech expansionism beyond data protection and market critique (Sharon, 2020(Sharon, , 2021;;Sharon & Gellert, 2023).

Third (section 7-8), I present a reconstruction of Palantir's move into health.I propose that it is enabled by the perceived neutrality of Palantir's technologies and by Palantir's mobilization of health and democratic discourse mirroring the language of established and trusted health institutions.I counter this view arguing that these technologies were built according to the values and logics of the sphere of security.These logics should not be perceived as separated from their social-political context.

In the fourth part (section 9-10), I flesh out the risks of Palantir's expansion into the health sphere using Sharon's sphere transgression framework.We should anticipate the risks of non-equitable returns, dominance, and new dependencies in the sphere of health.I add that we should anticipate the harms of traveling technologies transporting logics across spheres -especially if they have been perverted once before within their sphere of origin.I conclude that the concept of sphere transgressions enables us to identify risks of Palantir's expansion into the health sphere beyond data protection concerns.


Palantir: from security to health

Palantir Technologies, founded in 2003, is a technology company that designs software and platforms to 'empower organizations with highly complex and sensitive data environments' (Palantir Homepage, 2022).Palantir is active across public, private, and nonprofit sectors and involved in many different societal domains, although originally, their technologies are used in the field of security.After 9/11 Palantir was heralded as an indispensable tool of US Intelligence in the war on terrorism (Vance & Stone, 2011).Among others, Palantir offers its services to the US Army, Homeland Security, Immigration and Customs Enforcement and local law enforcement.Its main products are the programs 'Gotham' and 'Foundry', which are both data aggregation and analytics programs designed to collect and combine data from different sources into one platform.They are very similar and often referred to as one single 'Palantir platform' (Privacy International, 2020).These technologies are used across industries for various purposes ranging from security to health research.Palantir works in over 150 countries across the globe.In February 2023, the company's enterprise value was 14.47 billion dollar and its net worth 16.87 billion. 2 Despite its significant market position, its world-wide presence, and its many collaborations, Palantir is largely unknown to the broader public.

While Palantir originated and has been predominantly active in the field of security, the company has expanded its activities, making its technologies available to the health domain. 3During the pandemic, these partnerships intensified and took a flight across continents.Palantir forged (new) collaborations with several established health organizations.For instance, in the United States, they offered software, their platform Foundry, to monitor the spread of Covid-19 for the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and Health and Human Services (HHS).Covid-19 also opened doors in Europe, where Palantir obtained a contract with the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom.The services in the health domain, related to Covid, consist of programs intended to surveil the spread of the virus, to manage outbreaks in real-time, to research the virus and to distribute vaccines.For instance, the Palantir program DCIPHER assists public health experts of CDC with 'disease surveillance' and 'outbreak management for real-time events'.DCIPHER is built on Palantir Foundry and 'facilitates the collection and integration of various sources of epidemiological, surveillance, and laboratory data and provides tools for multiple organizations, including CDC's jurisdictional partners, to investigate and take action' (Palantir CDC, 2021).


Controversy in health and security

Palantir's activities in the health domain have been met with resistance.In the UK, digital justice organization Foxglove, is preparing a lawsuit against the UK government.They resist the Federated Data Platform, an NHS platform, likely to be built and run by Palantir, that should contain all the (sensitive) health information of the NHS -a 480-million-pound contract.If Palantir wins the contract, Foundry, its main platform, will be 'permanently installed in the NHS' (Foxglove & Doctors Association UK, 2023).Concerns mainly focus on risks associated with lack of data protection and transparency, which I will elaborate on below.

One major source of concern for activists, politicians and journalists is Palantir's history with human rights violations in the security domain (European Parliament, Parliamentary Question -E-007026/2020).In 2019 Palantir was severely criticized by human rights organizations such as Amnesty International (2020) for the role it played in Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) under the Trump administration.Palantir's contracts with US Homeland Security for ICE date back to 2011.In 2013 this consisted of offering Palantir Gotham software.The contracts were intended to develop 'FALCON-SA' as a technological search and analysis tool for ICE to detect law violations.Under their current contract, Palantir offers its Investigative Case Management System (ICM) and FALCON analytical platforms to 'access a vast "ecosystem" of data to facilitate immigration officials in both discovering targets and then creating and administering cases against them' (Woodman, 2017).One result of how these tools were applied by ICE is the 2019 workplace raid in Mississippi (U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, 2019) where 680 migrant workers were arrested (MacMillan & Dwoskin, 2019).

Palantir's profiling tools consist of invasive surveillance software that mines data from public sources, including state, federal and local databases, but also private sources such as utility bills, phone records, social media accounts, health care information and biometric data (Knight & Gekker, 2020;Mijente, 2018;Woodman, 2017).This enables law enforcement to construct profiles of migrants and their social relations to track and deport them.

The accelerated development of invasive and highly sophisticated surveillance and profiling tools at the disposal of state actors has worried many.Amnesty International marked Palantir as a contributor to the violation of human rights of asylum-seekers and migrants 'who are separated from family members, subject to workplace raids, detained, and face deportation by ICE' often without 'procedural safeguards such as access to legal counsel and adequate interpretation services' (Amnesty International, 2020, p. 6).

After Mississippi, critique also came from within Palantir.Employees discussed whether the ICE contracts made Palantir a tool for former President Trump's political agenda (MacMillan & Dwoskin, 2019).Historically, there exist close ties between Palantir and the Trump administration.Its founder, Peter Thiel, financed the Trump campaign in 2016 and was part of Trump's transition team.Thiel has been described as '[a]n avowed libertarian who has railed against the tech industry's predominantly liberal politics' (MacMillan & Dwoskin, 2019).


Moving into the health domain: data protection critique

In Europe, politicians have criticized Palantir's presence in the health domain.In 2020, several Greek members of the European Parliament questioned the 'non-transparent partnership of the Greek government with Palantir Technologies, a data processing company, to deal with the Covid-19 pandemic' (European Parliament, Parliamentary Question -E-007026/2020):

Palantir Technologies provides data processing and mining tools to companies and governments.Many of its partnerships have provoked strong opposition with regard to personal data breaches, e.g., its relationship with Cambridge Analytica, which gained access to the personal information of millions of Facebook users, its partnership with President Trump to identify illegal immigrants who were consequently deported and the hitherto unpublished contract with the NHS, which resulted in the collection of a huge amount of sensitive personal data.(European Parliament, Parliamentary Question -E-007026/2020) Here, Palantir's involvement in notorious data breaches are described as at tension with their role during the pandemic.The Greek MEPs questioned the transparency of contracts with Palantir that involve the access and processing of (sensitive) personal data of (Greek) citizens and data protection measures.

Data protection is, justifiably so, also at the heart of critical reports by activist organizations such as Privacy International and the Foxglove lawsuit.For instance, Privacy International wrote an extensive, critical report on the non-transparent contracts with Palantir and its access to sensitive, personal (health) data.Palantir collected and connected immense quantities of public and personal data for security purposes in the United States.During the pandemic, Palantir collected unprecedented amounts of sensitive health data.In fact, health institutions such as the HHS and the NHS granted Palantir access to health data during the Covid crisis (Fitzgerald & Crider, 2020;Privacy International, 2020).

Of course, data protection is an important and legitimate critique appealing to the enforcement of legal instruments such as human rights and the GDPR (Downey, 2020;Confessore & Rosenberg, 2018;OHCHR, 2022; European Parliament, Parliamentary Question -E-0070262020; Privacy International, 2020).However, does this focus on data protection adequately capture the harms and risks of Palantir's expansionism?In what follows, I investigate Palantir's expansion from security into health by interpreting and judging it from the perspective of 'sphere transgressions'.As we will see, this lens reveals normative concerns beyond data protection.


Sphere transgressions

While Palantir's move into the health domain is mainly criticized from the perspective of data protection, it seems that this critique does not address the 'wrong' implied in these reports, tied to the (political) history of Palantir's technologies.I want to summarize this wrong, roughly, as Palantir's invasion of the health sphere, aimed at protecting the vulnerable, using expertise, technologies and data that were designed and used for (controversial) security purposes.I use Sharon's sphere transgressions framework to unpack this wrong and to reveal its harms and risks beyond data protection.

Sharon derives the notion of 'sphere transgressions' from Michael Walzer's work Spheres of Justice (1983).Walzer argues that our society consists of multiple spheres.Spheres are domains in our social life, such as the sphere of political power, the market, security and welfare, education and the sphere of the home and family.Spheres are constituted by certain social goods.These goods have a particular meaning within their social domain, tied to 'appropriate' principles of distribution.For instance, the sphere of health, the social domain that tries to distribute a good like medical care, is regulated by distributive logics of care for those in need.Of course, one could legitimately ask Walzer, or Sharon, 'what is 'appropriate'?' (Dworkin, 1983).However, I will not elaborate on the normative grounding of these principles.For now, it suffices to state that the notion of 'sphere trangression' raises normative concerns and that risks of expansionism should be empirically informed (Sharon, 2021).

The goods of different social spheres are distributed according to different principles of justice -standards, procedures, and reasons (Walzer, 1983).However, standards can be violated, goods appropriated, and spheres invaded.After all, there are some goods that are 'dominant': they are attractive to acquire because whoever owns them, gains access to other goods.When an actor or group of actors succeeds in exploiting the dominance of a certain good or cluster of goods, this is called a monopoly.The monopolization of goods allows the 'owners' to convert them into others.For instance, in a technocratic society, technological knowledge converts to prestige and power (Walzer, 1983, p. 11).Now, while they might be contested, monopolies and dominance are not necessarily unjust within spheres.A monopoly is unjust when power acquired through monopolizing goods in one sphere is converted into power and advantages in another sphere.Walzer calls this 'tyranny'.For instance, great financial resources obtained in the sphere of money and commodities should not convert to political influence in the sphere of political power.Likewise, political power should not convert to lucrative deals in the market sphere.This is considered an invasion of another sphere.

Taking inspiration from Walzer, Sharon conceptualizes sphere transgressions as the 'conversions of advantages based on digital expertise into advantages in other spheres of society' (Sharon & Gellert, 2023).The focal point of her critique is Big Tech expansionism.Sharon uses 'sphere transgressions' to question the importation and potential dominance of certain goods, values, practices and logics. 4She pleads special vigilance towards the increasing dominance of industrial logic in the health domain 'in the form of data collection, analysis and infrastructure', making tech corporations indispensable actors in the field which also allows them to convert the goods they have monopolized into advantages in other spheres (Sharon, 2021, p. 324).For instance, due to their technological expertise, sophisticated technological infrastructures, financial resources and access to vast amounts of data companies like Alphabet and Apple have acquired an immensely powerful position in the health sector.In fact, they have become crucial facilitators of the technological infrastructure for public facilities and social services across various social domains such as health care, housing, mobility, safety, security and education.This exceptionally powerful position allows technology giants to increasingly structure our social life.Sharon argues that this might lead to changing values and practices within these spheres, often without public or democratic deliberation procedures (Sharon, 2020).Moreover, while drawing data and resources from society, Big Tech does not necessarily return the value.Big Tech actors have become 'public actors without public values' (Taylor, 2021).In section 9, I elaborate on these risks that Sharon classifies as (1) non-equitable returns (2) dominance and (3) new dependencies by applying the sphere transgressions framework to Palantir's move into health.


Beyond data protection

Given the risks that the lens of sphere transgressions reveals, Sharon argues that we should not be blinded by data protection concerns, because it shifts our attention away from infrastructural power, accumulated by technology companies (Sharon, 2020).Instead, we should point our arrows at the digital infrastructure that tech companies are building and on which we are becoming increasingly dependent.Encryption and data protection are becoming part of the business models of tech companies.For instance, Palantir advertises its platform Foundry as GDPR compliant (Palantir Data Protection, 2023).Its mission statement explicitly says that 'Palantir was founded on the conviction that it is essential to preserve fundamental principles of privacy and civil liberties while using data' (Palantir, Privacy and Civil Liberties, 2023).Yet, even if we assume that Palantir is genuine about and successful in ensuring data protection, there are risks and harms involved in Palantir's expansionism.

Furthermore, Big Tech expansionism has been criticized from angles such as harmful commodification and surveillance capitalism often in conjunction with data protection critique (Zuboff, 2019).While such critique is valid, it is also a limited view to interpret the invasion of corporations such as Palantir into the health domain as the infiltration of marketing logic only.Certainly, we could argue that Palantir capitalizes on a good central to the sphere of health, for instance medical data, and that this marketization undermines values of care.However, this does not capture the risks of growing dependency and dominance following the importation of other dominant values and logics.Sphere transgressions are made across spheres.Beyond the market, one can also imagine the invasion of democratic or industrial values and logics (Sharon, 2021, p. 323).The notion of sphere transgressions may help us to reveal risks beyond data protection.I first reconstruct Palantir's expansion into health.


Expanding into health: a reconstruction

While there might be many factors that facilitate Palantir's seemingly unhindered expansionism across spheres, I want to make two observations that stand out in relation to its move into health when studying Palantir's press releases on contracts with health organizations.I argue that Palantir's expansion is enabled by the perceived neutrality of Palantir's technologies and by Palantir's adaptation of discourse belonging to the sphere of health.Recognizing this is important if we want to detect and map sphere transgressions.

First, the technology that is used in the sphere of health is the same as the technology used in the sphere of security.Palantir uses the same platform for the programs intended to support various activities in the health sphere, namely Palantir Foundry (2023).For instance, for the NIH, Palantir uses Foundry to create the largest centralized collection of Covid-19 patient data in the world intended to support Covid-19 research (Albertson, 2021).For the HHS, Palantir used the Tiberius program, which is also an adaptation of Foundry, to manage vaccine distribution (Palantir press releases, HHS, 2021).For their collaboration with the NHS, Palantir also uses Foundry (Palantir press releases, NHS, 2020).I return to this point in section 8.

Secondly, Palantir deploys health and democratic rights discourse, using language such as 'empowerment', 'equity' and 'vulnerability', mirroring the language of the (trusted) established health organizations they collaborate with.For instance, in the case of the collaboration with NIH, Akash Jain, Palantir's US Government President celebrated the contract by emphasizing helping 'the most vulnerable'.

Palantir was founded to put the most innovative software to work solving the world's most pressing challenges, and working to empower scientific research, especially when it comes to helping the most vulnerable, fits squarely within our mission.(Palantir press releases, NIH, 2020) Again, when announcing their contract with the NHS, vulnerability played a central role in explaining their motivation and mission.

NHS personnel will use Palantir Foundry to understand how COVID-19 is spreading, identify risks to particularly vulnerable populations, proactively increase health and care resources in emerging hot spots, ensure critical equipment is supplied to the facilities with the greatest need, and divert patients and service users to the facilities that are best able to care for them (Palantir press releases, NHS, 2020) Moreover, when they announced the contract with the HHS, Palantir deployed the concept of equity in vaccine programs, drawing from the World Health Organization repertoire on fair distribution of vaccines (WHO, Vaccine Equity, 2021).

Palantir will work with HHS's Office of the Chief Information Officer (OCIO) and provide its software platform to help manage the U.S. COVID-19 vaccine distribution strategy, enable international distribution, and improve the equity and efficacy of vaccine programs (Palantir press releases, HHS, 2020) Through the lens of sphere transgressions, we can see how different logics and values are combined and deployed.Palantir's discourse in the sphere of health emphasizes empowerment, vulnerability, and 'caring' protection.Moreover, it mixes it with an industrial repertoire: language that emphasizes values such as efficiency and effectiveness by means of their technological expertise, data and technological infrastructures.In general, Palantir mobilizes a democratic repertoire to explain its vision on promoting the 'common good', namely through safeguarding civil liberties and privacy rights (Palantir, PCL, 2023).

Palantir adapts its language according to the sphere in which it operates, which gives the impression that Palantir is a 'native' actor in the health sphere.Yet, their language in the sphere of health is at odds with their statements and actions in the sphere of security.Critical discourse analysis is important to understand how actors can move between spheres and nestle themselves comfortably in different spheres without too much resistance.For instance, using the language of the health sphere obscures the importation of the values, logics and practices, dominant in the sphere of security, embodied by Palantir's technology.The technology is presented as serving the values of the health sphere but may in fact threaten them.


Technology: vehicle of values and logics

Let us now return to the first observation.Palantir uses platforms from the sphere of security to organize the sphere of health.This observation relates to another aspect of the reconstruction of Palantir's expansion across spheres, namely, my claim that technology is often perceived as neutral, but functions as a vehicle laden with values and logics that it transports across spheres. 5 The technological infrastructure, data and expertise of Palantir might be perceived as neutral assets of the company Palantir.By 'neutral' I mean that people perceive these technologies as not laden with political ideologies, logics or (moral) values.This view can be coupled with the idea that technology is polyvalent.An analytics platform such as Foundry, used to detect terrorists and criminals, can also be used to detect infected individuals, all for the common good, whether this is security or public health.Statements of the (former) executives of Palantir seem to be in line with this perspective.In response to the critique on Palantir's role under ICE, its CEO, Alex Karp, stated that it is the task of the government to take responsibility for and justify the use of technologies to surveil citizens, not platform corporations (MacMillan & Dwoskin, 2019). 6 This statement suggests that Palantir is 'just the messenger': a neutral producer of neutral technologies, separate from politics.

If one accepts this view, using Palantir's platforms in the sphere of health would not raise a red flag per se.An analytics tool can be used for efficiently and effectively protecting citizens from terrorists or a virus.Yet, from the perspective of sphere transgressions, the origin and history of the technology should matter (Bellanova et al., 2021, p. 140).As Langdon Winner argued, technologies are political and should not be isolated from the context in which they were conceived, designed, developed, and used (Winner, 1980, p. 128).Producers and environments are part of technology and they co-shape each other (Verbeek, 2005).Its use across different social contexts results in structural effects across these contexts, reinforced by the affordances, logics and values of the technology (Bellanova et al., 2021;Milan, 2020).

Palantir's Foundry is a good example.This program has been described as a more sophisticated version of Gotham, Palantir's earlier data analytics platform.Gotham was developed after 9/11 in the context of counter terrorism under Republican president George W. Bush.From its inception then, Palantir has operated and developed its technology according to the practices, logics, and values of the sphere of security.The structure of the technology then is oriented towards identifying suspicious behavior and potential terrorists.This means that Palantir's technologies have consisted of invasive and advanced data aggregation and analytics platforms.Their practices have revolved around surveillance, profiling, identification and targeting of suspects with the aim of effectively and efficiently detecting suspicious activities.This means that the data collected and analyzed was aimed at preventing terrorism.Importantly, its technology has been developed and trained based on this type of data.Gotham and Foundry were built for the purpose of combatting terrorism, but also shaped the sphere of security in turn, enabling large-scale surveillance, profiling and identification.

Palantir developed and acquired its technologies, wealth, data and expertise in the sphere of security.Its technologies are imbued with security values, logics and politics.Moreover, Palantir plays a key role in putting and keeping these complex technological infrastructures in place (Bellanova et al., 2021;Harris, 2017;Knight & Gekker, 2020).The neutrality view obscures these logics, politics and values.Moreover, it obscures that technology may transport them when it travels across spheres.

From the perspective of sphere transgressions, it is important to understand the socialpolitical dimensions of technologies.A technology from the sphere of security, built to identify criminals and terrorists, should be subject to scrutiny if it becomes subsequently deployed to 'help the most vulnerable' during a health crisis.Importing these technologies from the sphere of security to the sphere of health might harm the values, practices and logics that give meaning to the health sphere.I unpack this claim under the risks of sphere transgressions (4).

Finally, Palantir's expansionism can be reconstructed as enabled by its adaptation of health discourse and the perceived neutrality of its technology.Of course, the entire process of 'importing' Palantir's technologies in the sphere of health should be understood as political.If we are concerned with the way in which these technologies impact justice, we should focus not only on the transformative power of technology, but also on the ways in which it is forced and enforced (Bellanova et al., 2021, p. 23).The exceptional circumstances of the COVID-crisis opened doors for Big Tech as societies' problem solvers (Calhoun, 2010;Morozov, 2020).It made for fertile ground for sphere transgressions and corresponding harms that have been argued to stem from perceiving and treating technology as divorced from social-political dimensions (Klein, 2020;Milan, 2020;).While beyond the scope of this paper, I want to note that it is worth studying the history of sphere transgressions as complex and mutually influencing relationships between private and public entities, between industry and policy (de Goede, 2018; Lanzing et al., 2022).In the next section, I elaborate on the risks of Palantir's expansion into health.


Risks: non-equitable returns, dominance & new dependencies

This section maps the risks and potential harms of Palantir's move into the health sphere.It consists of two parts.First, I apply the risks of sphere transgressions as identified by Sharon: non-equitable returns, dominance and new dependencies.In the second part, I add the risk of perverted logics through technologies that travel across spheres.To these ends, let us return to Palantir's sphere of origin: security.The sphere of the security is the sphere in which the majority of Palantir's activities and crucial development of their technologies takes place.In this sphere, industrial logics and values are dominant, although they are frequently combined with market or civic repertoires:

Our earliest work in counter-terrorism required us to ask whether we could meaningfully strengthen national security in the US without weakening constitutional privacy protections.In response, we invested financial and intellectual capital to build technology that is now trusted by the world's most stringent -and skeptical -data protection regimes.(Palantir, Privacy and Civil Liberties, 2023) The emphasis on financial and intellectual capital as well as technology to solve civil rights and value conflicts point at a technocratic approach that fits very well with an industrial 'security' logic aimed at efficiency and effectiveness.Typical assets include surveillance and profiling technologies, data, technology experts and integration platforms.If we translate these assets into 'goods' that are meaningful and dominant in the sphere of security, then these would roughly amount to technological infrastructures, technological expertise, and data.

One can make the argument that Palantir monopolized dominant goods in the sphere of security.The corporation developed its technologies and technological expertise while under contract with Homeland Security, ICE and the US Army (among others).These contracts gave Palantir exclusive access to governmental databases.Moreover, the billion-dollar contracts fortified Palantir's position.For years, these contracts were unknown to the broader public.This made Palantir a powerful, wealthy, expert actor.However, monopolies are not necessarily problematic within a sphere.Following the logic of the sphere of security, goods such as data, are and perhaps should be distributed asymmetrically because of their sensitive nature.Moreover, intelligence and security are highly specialized fields in which technology experts are valued and powerful.

Sharon's concern, however, is that technology monopolists become powerful actors who import certain goods, values, practices and logics across spheres, while their dominance should be questioned.If we apply the risks of sphere transgressions as identified by Sharon to Palantir's move into health, we should investigate to what extent: (1) the value generated by Palantir returns to the public; (2) the company is able to reshape the sphere of health based on its own interests and (3) this move creates new dependencies for the provision of basic goods and public services (Sharon & Gellert, 2023).

These risks are realistic and all intertwined.Palantir's monopoly on dominant 'goods' in the sphere of security converts to open doors and advantages in the sphere of health.Especially during a crisis (Calhoun, 2010).Palantir's expertise, infrastructures and databases were considered powerful, unparalleled tools to combat Covid-19.Consider the case of Palantir's alliance with the NHS (UK) that originated during the pandemic.

Sharon's first risk entails non-equitable returns.Value generated by the company does not flow back to the public.This is a legitimate concern that applies to this case.The NHS provided unprecedented access to health data to Palantir, signed contracts that allowed the company to train their models and profit off NHS health data, and granted intellectual property rights to the enterprise.Aside from data protection concerns, this sphere transgression duped the citizens who originally shared their data for health purposes and who did not share in the benefits (Fitzgerald & Crider, 2020;Privacy International, 2020).Not only Palantir, but also other Big Tech companies as well have been criticized for the lucrative deals they struck during the health crisis (Klein, 2020;Morozov, 2020).

Furthermore, as Palantir expands into the health sphere, Palantir accumulates '(health) data', 'wealth' and 'intellectual property' while at the same time bolstering and developing their technological goods: 'technological expertise', 'technological infrastructures' and 'databases'.The monopoly on these technological goods is fertile ground for sphere transgressions and Sharon's second risk: dominance.Big Tech has been criticized for crowding out or changing the values that are important to the health sphere (Sharon, 2020;Sharon, 2021;Taylor, 2021).Importing Palantir's technologies into the health sphere might affect the values, practices and logics that give meaning to the sphere of health.This might lead to injustices.After all, who should be protected, who is considered vulnerable and who is considered a threat should be recognized as normative and political questions of which the answers should depend on the logics and values of a sphere.For instance, within the sphere of health, it should be a matter of debate whether Palantir and its technologies should draw these lines if they are driven by logics of efficiency and effectiveness, of suspicion and exclusion.Moreover, as we learned, Palantir uses democratic and health discourse such as 'equity ', 'vulnerability' and 'civil liberties'. Yet, it is unclear to what extent these concepts align with the visions of the health institutions they collaborate with.How these values become realized through the technology is at the discretion of Palantir.Furthermore, the language of these press releases reveals that their approach is techno-solutionist and industrial, relying on 'critical equipment', 'innovative software', 'management' and 'strategy' (see section 7).

Expertise and technology are valued for their promise of effectiveness and efficiency to attain health goals (Milan, 2020).Yet, allowing industrial logics and values to transgress into the health sphere increases their dominance and reshapes the sphere.For instance, introducing Foundry in the NHS would change the roles of NHS employees.Computational systems always involve human-technology entanglements (de Goede, 2018).Deploying Palantir's complex platforms entails working alongside Palantir consultants.For instance, collaborating with Palantir has been documented to reshape the practices, meaning and roles of police work (Harris, 2017).Similarly, the roles and practices of NHS employees might change into those of analytics experts rather than public health experts.The potential harm is that sphere of health will increasingly be structured according to the industrial logic and values of the sphere of security, crowding out those of care, changing its meaning.

Finally, the dominance of technological goods creates new dependencies in other spheres, such as health.Tech actors like Palantir are becoming indispensable facilitators of basic services and goods, such as health (crisis) management (Sharon, 2020).These dependencies foster decision-making power without public accountability, transparency, or democratic oversight, as has been the critique on the alliance between the NHS and Palantir (European Parliament, Parliamentary Question -E-007026/2020; Taylor, 2021).This increases power inequalities between private and public entities as well as citizens.Moreover, the company has been accused to be deliberately designed as a monopoly provider, using technologies that are incompatible with other platforms and not interoperable in practice (Foxglove & Doctors Association UK, 2023).Users become locked-in and dependent on Palantir for updates, training and expertise (Harris, 2017).

Based on these risks, one could judge Palantir's expansion into health to be high-risk and potentially harmful -Walzer might argue 'unjust' and 'tyrannical', especially when one considers that Palantir is also increasingly present in other spheres such as space, energy, food, and humanitarian aid.In the last section, I add a type of risk that should be of importance to the sphere transgressions framework as proposed by Sharon.


Perverted logics

A sphere transgressions framework questions the importation and dominance of logics and values across spheres.For instance, following the risks of Sharon, security logics may already be considered problematic for reshaping and undermining practices, values and logics of care once they invade the sphere of health.Earlier, I argued that technologies are 'vehicles', imbued with these values and logics, that allow them to travel. 7Building on this argument, I want to add that already risky expansions might become particularly pernicious when the logics that are imported already proved to be vulnerable to perversion and led to injustice within their sphere of origin.At a minimum, such history should warrant special vigilance but one can also imagine a full 'travel ban' for a Big Tech actor.Palantir's history with human rights violations in the sphere of security would make it a candidate for this additional risk assessment.

Then, given this history, what risks should we anticipate with Palantir's move into health, a sphere that revolves around vulnerability and equity?Compared to other Big Tech actors, Palantir is special because its sphere of origin.Its technologies are deeply entangled with politics.National security is high on the political agenda and immediately affected by who is elected.While Palantir's practices might still be in line with the logics of the sphere of security, these logics are particularly prone to perversion when politics demand unjust practices from the field of security.For instance, Palantir's technologies enabled the large-scale arrests and deportation of migrants.Palantir's Foundry was developed for and during its activities for ICE.This means that the system was fed with and trained on migrant data from mainly the Latinx and Chicanx community.This led to biased data and disproportionate surveillance of these communities.Designed for the purpose of mass deportation of migrants, this technology fuels 'discriminatory policing practices targeting people of color' and 'undermining Sanctuary City policies' (Mijente, 2018).Industrial logics of efficiency and effectiveness were mobilized for exclusionary politics, and the technology became imbued with them.

We should anticipate the particularly pernicious effects of technology developed under unjust politics when it 'travels' to other spheres.By pernicious effects, I mean that injustices become structural, reinforced, and reproduced across spheres.For instance, in the US, the Congressional Hispanic Caucus worried about the collaboration between Palantir and the HHS.Politicians were concerned about whether the health data that Palantir could access could also then be used for law and immigration enforcement purposes in the sphere of security. 8This fear is a known reason for migrants (and other marginalized groups) not to vaccinate.Engaging with Palantir in the health sphere could exacerbate already existing problems of exclusion and distrust undermining the meaning of and right to health care (Foxglove & Doctors Association UK, 2023).

Industrial values, such as effectiveness and efficiency, and industrial logics such as discerning efficiently and targeting effectively, are anything but neutral.Stefania Milan argues that while industrial practices such as calculation are also measures of care, these standards often exclude marginalized groups.For instance, during the pandemic, '(…) undocumented migrants who do not have access to health care might simply not feature in the national COVID-19 counting, which excludes them from any mitigation strategy' (Milan, 2020, p. 3).Techno-solutions are fraught with racialized relations.If we fail to recognize the social-political dimensions of technologies, we fail to recognize their potential as structural sources of violence, oppression, and injustice across spheres (Bellanova et al., 2021;Benjamin, 2020;Noble, 2018).

We should anticipate that using Palantir's technologies in the health sphere will impact the meaning of equity, vulnerability, and care.Given Palantir's history, I judge this sphere transgression to be particularly high-risk, especially for marginalized groups.How we structure the sphere of health and make meaning within it, determining who is vulnerable or not, who should receive care or not, should not be at the discretion of a platform whose logics already led to injustice against marginalized groups in its sphere of origin.


Conclusion

This paper explored the risks of Palantir's expansion from the sphere of security into the health sphere using Sharon's sphere transgressions framework as a conceptual lens and critical tool to understand and judge this move.I argued that data protection critique is insufficient to address the risks and harms of Palantir's expansionism.Palantir's expansion into health is more adequately understood through the lens of sphere transgressions, pointing at different normative concerns regarding Big Tech expansionism.I explained the concept of sphere transgression, building on Sharon and Walzer, as the 'conversions of advantages based on digital expertise into advantages in other spheres of society' (Sharon & Gellert, 2023).I then reconstructed Palantir's move from security into the sphere of health, arguing that this expansion was enabled by the adaptation of democratic and health discourse, as well as the perceived neutrality of technology.Next, I applied Sharon's risks of sphere transgressions to the case of Palantir: non-equitable returns, dominance and new dependencies.Central to these risks is the warning against the importation and dominance of certain, particularly industrial, goods, values, practices and logics across spheres, and in this case, the health sphere.In the final part, I added a risk pertaining to the spread of perverted logics that could make a sphere transgression particularly pernicious.A history of logic perversions within spheres should make us vigilant towards technologies that travel across spheres and their potential for reproducing injustices.I conclude that the sphere transgressions framework reveals risks beyond data protection and market concerns regarding Palantir's expansion into the health domain.
