Food web theory has illustrated that mobile top predators, such as lake trout (Salvelinus namaycush), can be potent stabilizers of food webs due to their ability to shift foraging behaviors in response to changing conditions.Consistent with this, research has demonstrated that mean lake trout food web attributes (i.e., trophic position and nearshore coupling) structurally change across environmental gradients; however, intraspecific variation in these attributes across gradients has not been fully explored.Here, we used stable isotope-based food web metrics to investigate how both mean and intraspecific variation in trophic structure changes in Canadian boreal shield lakes across gradients in ecosystem size, temperature, and competition.Consistent with earlier findings, we find nearshore coupling decreases and trophic position increases with warmer summer climate.In contrast to previous findings, increasing lake area predicted increased nearshore coupling and was not associated with lake trout trophic position.Our results show that warmer temperatures and smaller ecosystem sizes reduce the expression of intraspecific variation in food web structures.Specifically, larger lakes increased variation

INTRODUCTION

Food webs respond dynamically to changing conditions such as ecosystem size, climate, and resource diversity, often in ways that may mediate the stability of aquatic ecosystems (Kondoh, 2003;McCann & Rooney, 2009;McMeans et al., 2016;Tunney et al., 2012).Mobile top predators can play a critical role in governing these stabilizing dynamic food web structures (i.e., who eats whom and how much).For example, rapid behavioral responses by mobile top predators to spatial resource variation can act to stabilize whole food web dynamics by releasing predation pressures on low-density prey populations and strengthening predation on high population densities, providing a stable resource supply to mobile top predators and maintaining the persistence of resource populations (Kondoh, 2003;McCann et al., 2005;McCann & Rooney, 2009;McMeans et al., 2016).Such behaviors structure food web interactions and contribute to the adaptive capacity (i.e., the ability to adjust or expand coping range to maintain function in the face of changing conditions), which is an important component of the resilience of these ecosystems (McMeans et al., 2016).

Mobile predators across a wide range of aquatic ecosystems including streams, lakes, floodplains, and marine food webs have been shown to generate these stabilizing dynamic structural changes in response to changing conditions (Champagne et al., 2022;McMeans et al., 2019;Pool et al., 2017;Vander Zanden, Shuter, et al., 1999;Vander Zanden & Vadeboncoeur, 2002;Wolf et al., 2020).Stable isotope approaches have often been a key tool in the characterization of such structural changes by assaying attributes that measure the gross architecture of the whole food web (e.g., energy flow through different carbon pathways, trophic position).Such information can be valuable for assessing adaptive capacity for different kinds of environmental change and, therefore, the potential resilience of ecosystems.Nonetheless, these studies have tended to focus on the mean food web response of top predators, and to our knowledge, not many studies have examined how intraspecific variation in food web characteristics of these organisms responds to changing conditions (e.g., climate, ecosystem size).

Given that we are now consistently documenting mean food web structural shifts across gradients of changing conditions, it remains to ask if intraspecific variation in food web structure also changes consistently across similar environmental gradients.Implicit in the mean structural change literature are the ideas that intraspecific variation remains constant or is irrelevant; yet neither are ideas that are well justified empirically (Bolnick et al., 2003).Indeed, ecologists have empirically demonstrated that trophic niche width (a measure of individual variation in diet) within populations responds to varying intra-and interspecific competition, which is mediated by resource diversity and foraging behavior, and across environmental gradients such as lake size and altitude (Bolnick et al., 2003;Bolnick & Ballare, 2020;Eloranta et al., 2022;Svanbäck et al., 2015;Svanbäck & Bolnick, 2007).Additionally, theoretical work has argued cogently that intraspecific variation in traits such as foraging behavior can strongly influence resilience in ecosystems by allowing a portfolio of responses that buffer against changing conditions (Bolnick et al., 2011;Mimura et al., 2017).These empirical and theoretical results hint that intraspecific variation in food web structure may respond consistently across gradients of changing conditions, and there may be a significant link between intraspecific variation and ecosystem resilience, making it an important component to empirically and theoretically understand.

Lake trout (Salvelinus namaycush), a mobile apex predator in many Canadian boreal shield lakes, is an ideal model species to explore the intraspecific variation in trophic responses to changing conditions.Its role in structuring aquatic food webs across environmental gradients is well documented (Dolson et al., 2009;Guzzo et al., 2017;Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 1996).Lake trout prefer cold-water habitat, and empirical results have suggested that their nearshore (i.e., littoral) resource use in summer and trophic position are mediated by thermal accessibility (Dolson et al., 2009;Guzzo et al., 2017;Tunney et al., 2014;Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 1996).During the summer, increased separation of deep offshore and nearshore zones (e.g., due to lake size) or elevated water temperatures in the nearshore limit access by this cold-water stenotherm to nearshore productivity and therefore reduce the energy that can be acquired from this habitat by lake trout (Dolson et al., 2009;Guzzo et al., 2017;Tunney et al., 2012Tunney et al., , 2014)).Tunney et al. (2012) found that low nearshore energy use was associated with reduced lake trout density, and in turn weakened top-down control of offshore forage fish and decreased omnivory.These well-studied mean trophic structure responses present us with an opportunity to explore how intraspecific variation in lake trout food web dynamics changes across these same key gradients, which has implications for how future conditions under climate warming may alter population and food web structure.

Despite not specifically investigating intraspecific variation, these previous studies hint at how thermal accessibility might influence intraspecific variation in lake trout foraging.Results from previous studies suggest that we may reduce intraspecific variation with increasing temperature because warmer conditions will further restrict lake trout to the offshore environment, thus reducing opportunities for foraging in a variety of habitats (see Temperature Hypothesis, Table 1).Past work has found that lake size can have a similar effect as temperature (Eloranta et al., 2015;Tunney et al., 2012;Vander Zanden & Vadeboncoeur, 2002), with lake trout utilizing nearshore resources much more in small lakes than in large lakes (although this lake size effect disappears over large temperature ranges; Tunney et al., 2014).Thus, based on the intraspecific variation literature (Bolnick et al., 2011;Des Roches et al., 2018;Violle et al., 2012), we may also expect competition (either intra-or interspecific) and resource diversity (that often increases with lake size) to mediate the variation in individual food web structural responses (see Competition Hypothesis and Resource Diversity-Size Hypothesis, Table 1).Again, T A B L E 1 Summary of the hypotheses and corresponding mechanisms and predictions being investigated in this study.

mean lake trout food web positions have been shown to vary with competition (e.g., presence of invasive smallmouth bass [Micropterus dolomieu] [Vander Zanden, Shuter, et al., 1999]) and changes in resource diversity (e.g., availability of cisco, Coregonus artedi; Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 1996); however, changes in the degree of intraspecific variation in food web characteristics have not been identified.

In what follows, we used stable isotope and fish monitoring data from 52 boreal lakes across Ontario, Canada, to explore how both mean and intraspecific variation in lake trout food web structure changes across a gradient of physical and biological attributes (i.e., lake size, temperature, and competition, both intra-and interspecific).For each lake, we calculated six food web metrics to characterize the mean and intraspecific variation of lake trout (mean trophic position [TP], mean proportion nearshore carbon [PNC], TP range, PNC range, standard ellipse area, and standard deviation of nearest neighbor distance [SDNND]).We then used statistical tests to evaluate the relative strength of each of our hypotheses (Table 1) in driving the observed changes in food web structure across the environmental gradients.


METHODS


Data collection


Lake selection and food web sampling

We combined food web data, physical limnology data, and catch data from a total of 52 lakes across Ontario, Canada (Appendix S1: Figure S1).Of these 52 lakes, catch data were only available for 28.Food web data were collected primarily from lakes sampled by the Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources (OMNR) Broad-scale Monitoring (BsM) program over multiple sampling periods (46 lakes; 2007-2020), and these were supplemented by data from an additional six lakes from Algonquin Provincial Park (2007), Ontario, to increase the number of lakes used in our analysis.Collectively, these lakes span over a 1600 km distance (Appendix S1: Figure S1), range in surface area from 108 to 27,691 hectares, and vary in mean summer (June, July, August) air temperatures from 14 C to 21 C. Thus, these lakes establish large gradients that allow us to explore both mean and intraspecific variation in food web structural responses to changing conditions.The OMNR's BsM sampling program uses standardized protocols to generate comparable information on fish species and communities, as well as physical and chemical lake attributes across hundreds of lakes in Ontario (Sandstrom et al., 2013).Full details on the BsM protocols are described in Sandstrom et al. (2013).Briefly, depth-stratified netting surveys were conducted for each lake between May and September (i.e., when surface water temperature is above 18 C) of their respective sampling years using standardized gill netting practices.For each lake, standard North American benthic multipaneled gillnets (large mesh gangs 24.8 m long by 1.8 m high, with eight panels ranging from 38to 127-mm mesh size and small mesh gangs with eight panels ranging from 13-to 38-mm mesh sizes) were set overnight (16-20 h).The total number of gangs of gillnets used in each lake was dependent on lake size and depth, to ensure that effort was equally distributed over the entire lake and the whole fish community was sufficiently sampled and evenly represented.To achieve this, the lake was divided into quadrants and depth strata (1-3 m, 3-6 m, 6-12 m, 12-20 m, 20-35 m, 35-50 m, 50-75 m, and >75 m), and the number of gangs per area and depth strata were determined using table 4 in Sandstrom et al., 2013.During sampling, all captured fish were identified to species and measured for length (fork length, FL, ± 1 mm) and weight (round weight, RWT, ± 1 g).Relative abundances of each fish species were estimated as catch per unit effort (CPUE, catch per gang (24.8 m) of gill net).For a subset of captured fish, an epaxial muscle sample was taken from the dorsal surface of the fish for stable isotope analysis and stored in a freezer at −18 C prior to analysis.In addition to fish, unionid mussels and nearshore grazing snails were collected to use as offshore and nearshore baselines, respectively.Offshore refers to deep, pelagic habitat, and nearshore refers to shallow, littoral habitat.We note that benthic δ 13 C values can vary with depth (e.g., Bootsma et al., 1996), and because our benthic baseline taxa (snails) were not collected across depth gradients, our estimates of individual variation may underestimate the full range of nearshore resource use, particularly if lake trout specialize along a depth gradient within the benthic zone.Baseline collection methods were based on Sandstrom et al., 2013 Appendix XIII where the mean sample size was on average 6.2 ± 3.8 for mussels and 4.7 ± 3.4 for snails for each lake.Snails and mussels were removed from shells prior to stable isotope analysis.

To supplement the data from BsM lakes, we used food web data from six lakes in Algonquin Provincial Park, Ontario, which were collected by the Harkness Laboratory of Fisheries Research (OMNR) in 2007 (Ridgway et al., 2023).This sampling effort collected comparable stable isotope and physical lake characteristics; however, due to differences in the netting protocol, we were not able to generate comparable CPUE data.

Therefore, these lakes were only used in our supplemental analysis with the extended dataset of 52 lakes.

To ensure that lake trout trophic structure was not driven by the presence or absence of whole trophic groups, we only included lakes that have both nearshore and offshore prey fish in the analysis.Additionally, we only included lakes where both snail and mussel baseline data were available.This generated a total dataset of 52 lakes, where 28 had comparable CPUE data and were used for the main analysis to test for the relative influence of our three hypotheses.Full information on lakes used in this study can be found in Appendix S1: Table S1.


Drivers of lake trout trophic structure

We used physical (lake area, summer air temperature) and biological data (predator densities) collected from each of the study lakes to test the potential mechanisms driving differences in mean and intraspecific variation in lake trout trophic structure.Lake area was used as the measure of ecosystem size, and mean summer air temperature (a proxy for water temperature) was used to estimate thermal accessibility.Density (measured as CPUE) of lake trout was used to measure intraspecific competition, and density of other predators was used to measure interspecific competition.

Data on lake surface area (in hectares) were obtained for each lake from OMNR databases.To generate mean summer air temperature, daily mean air temperatures for the year of stable isotope sampling were extracted at the longitude and latitude of each study lake from the historical climate data of the Ontario Climate Data Portal (https://lamps.math.yorku.ca/OntarioClimate/HistoricalObservations.htm#/).We then calculated the grand mean of mean daily air temperatures for the months of June, July, and August.This temperature metric was chosen as it incorporates the mean temperature over several months, akin to the integration that is associated with stable isotope data.Catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) data were calculated from fish caught in the large mesh gillnets in the BsM standardized netting surveys.CPUE for each species in each lake was calculated using the following equation:
CPUE ¼ total caught sum of gangs ,
where total caught is the total number of individuals caught of a particular species in that lake and sum of gangs is the total number of net gangs used in that lake during the survey.Lake trout CPUE was used to estimate the density of lake trout in each lake (proxy for intraspecific competition), and the sum of walleye (Sander vitreus), smallmouth bass (Micropterus dolomieu), largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides), muskellunge (Esox masquinongu), burbot (Lota lota) and northern pike (Esox lucius) CPUE was used to estimate other predator densities (proxy for interspecific competition).Beyond our three hypotheses, previous work has shown that foraging behavior and trophic structure in lake trout and other fish species may be driven by other physical lake attributes like lake shape, the proportion of littoral area, and water quality (secchi depth and total dissolved phosphorus) (Dolson et al., 2009;Jenkins et al., 1992;Kaunzinger & Morin, 1998;Schindler et al., 1996;Svanbäck & Bolnick, 2007;Yodzis, 1984).To test for the potential influence of these additional lake attributes, we conducted a supplementary analysis to examine the relationship between lake trout trophic structure and these other lake attributes.Detailed information on how additional lake attribute data were collected can be found in Appendix S1.


Stable isotopes


Trophic position and nearshore carbon use

Following previous studies, we used stable carbon (δ 13 C) and nitrogen (δ 15 N) isotope ratios of lake trout and baseline invertebrate tissues to calculate the trophic position and the proportion of nearshore carbon assimilated in the diet (nearshore coupling) of our focal species (Dolson et al., 2009;Post, 2002;Tunney et al., 2012Tunney et al., , 2014;;Vander Zanden, Shuter, et al., 1999).Specifically, we used δ 13 C to trace the basal carbon sources (i.e., pelagic vs. benthic primary production) because there is different δ 13 C fractionation between the nearshore and offshore habitats but little to no fractionation with transfers among trophic levels (France, 1995;Post, 2002;Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 1999).In contrast, δ 15 N demonstrates predictable fractionation from prey to consumer and is used to estimate trophic position as we can quantify the number of trophic transfers that occur in the route from basal resource to our focal predator (Vander Zanden et al., 1997).

Our methodology of tissue collection, processing, and analysis can be found in Tunney et al. (2014).In summary, fish muscle tissue and baseline invertebrate tissues were either dried in an oven at 65-70 C for approximately 48-72 h or freeze-dried for 7 days; the samples were then ground into fine powder using a ball mill or mortar and pestle.Powdered tissues were simultaneously analyzed for δ 13 C and δ 15 N contents and isotopic compositions by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) (University of New Hampshire Stable Isotope Laboratory, University of New Brunswick Stable Isotopes in Nature Laboratory).We used fish muscle tissue due to its longer isotopic turnover time compared to other tissues such as liver, providing us with food web metrics that were integrated over a longer time frame (Heady & Moore, 2013).The majority (96%) of sampled lake trout were over 300 mm in FL to ensure that they were largely piscivorous and to reduce the ontogenetic effects that may play a role in diet differences.We used unionid mussels and nearshore grazing snails as isotopic baselines for the offshore (pelagic) and nearshore (littoral) food chains, respectively, for each lake.Baseline adjustment allowed us to account for natural variability in isotopic signatures across lakes, thus providing comparable trophic metrics.Due to our isotope samples having relatively low C:N ratios (70% < 3.5 and 91% < 4), we did not lipid correct the δ 13 C values (Post et al., 2007).

To estimate the proportion of nearshore carbon in the diet (hereafter PNC) and the trophic position (hereafter TP) of our focal species, lake trout, we used two source mixing models (Post, 2002).To calculate the PNC assimilated by each individual lake trout, we used the following equation from Post (2002): PNC = (δ 13 C laketrout − δ 13 C mussel )/ (δ 13 C snail − δ 13 C mussel ).This PNC measure is constrained by 0 and 1 and assumes that δ 13 C fractionates negligibly between trophic levels (Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 2001).We then calculated the TP of individual lake trout using the following equation from Post (2002) [26]: TP = 2 + (δ 15 N laketrout − (δ 15 N mussel × (δ 15 N mussel × (1 − PNC) + (δ 15 N snail × PNC))))/3.4,where 2 is the assumed TP of the baseline invertebrates, and 3.4 is the assumed increase in δ 15 N due to fractionation for each trophic level (Vander Zanden & Rasmussen, 2001).


Isotopic niche and community metric calculations

To explore intraspecific variation in PNC and TP of lake trout in these food webs, we calculated isotopic niche and community metrics using the R package SIBER (Figure 1) (Jackson et al., 2011), with PNC and TP representing δ 13 C (x-axis) and δ 15 N dimensions (y-axis), respectively.We estimated isotopic niche size using the frequentist version of the standard ellipse area corrected for small sample sizes (SEAc) (Jackson et al., 2011).Additionally, we used three metrics of community dispersion proposed by Layman, Arrington, et al. (2007), Layman, Quattrochi, et al. (2007) but applied them to the dispersion of individuals within each lake trout population: TP range (hereafter dTP), PNC range (hereafter dNC), and SDNND.When considering lake trout trophic ecology, dNC represents the diversity in basal resource use within the population, dTP is a proxy of the degree of trophic diversity in the population, SEAc represents the isotopic niche size of the population, and finally, SDNND provides information on the evenness and clustering of the population in isotopic space, which can be used to the interpret how similar resource use is among individuals.Additional information on the calculation of these metrics and their interpretation can be found in Figure 1.


Statistics

All data processing, statistical analyses, and figures were completed using the R Statistical Computing Environment (v.4.3.1,R Core Team, 2023).Assumptions of normality and heteroskedasticity were tested graphically using Q-Q plots, boxplots, and histograms of standardized residuals (Zuur et al., 2010).To avoid multicollinearity, predictor variables (e.g., lake surface area, mean summer air temperature, lake trout CPUE, other predator CPUE) that had correlation coefficients greater than 0.5 were not included in the same models (Appendix S1: Figure S2).

We used generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) to test the relative strength of our three leading hypotheses (resource diversity-size, thermal accessibility, and competition) on lake trout trophic structure (both mean and intraspecific variation metrics).Using the mgcv package in R (Wood, 2017), we analyzed two GAMM models for each response variable (SEAc, dTP, dNC, SDNND, mean TP, mean PNC).The first model included lake area, mean summer air temperature, and intraspecific competition (lake trout CPUE), and the second model included lake area, mean summer air temperature, and interspecific competition (other predator CPUE).In each model, we included sampling year as a random effect to account for multiple years of data collection, and the model was fit using restricted maximum likelihood (REML).Additionally, to utilize the full set of 52 lakes, we fit GAMMs for each response variable using only lake area and mean summer air temperature.When interpreting our analyses, we were interested in relationships that were both p < 0.10 and p < 0.05.We considered p < 0.05 a significant relationship and p < 0.10 marginally significant.See Appendix S1 for the model for each response variable.

Lastly, to explore potential effects of other lake attributes beyond our leading hypotheses (i.e., lake shape, the proportion of littoral area, secchi depth, and phosphorus), we conducted univariate linear regression analysis between each lake attribute and each trophic structure metric (see Appendix S1).


RESULTS


Mean response

Mean TP and mean PNC showed varying responses to gradients in lake area and mean summer air temperature and did not respond to changes in competition.Mean lake trout TP did not change across gradients in lake area (Figure 2a; Appendix S1: Figure S6a) and intra-or interspecific competition (Figure 2c; Appendix S1: Figure S3a).Mean lake trout TP increased with increasing mean summer air temperature (Figure 2b), where the trend was significant (or marginally significant) in the model with interspecific competition and in the extended dataset (i.e., 52 lakes), (Appendix S1: Figures S3b and S6b).

Mean lake trout PNC increased with lake area (all p < 0.05; Figure 2d; Appendix S1: Figure S3d; Appendix S1: Figure S6c) and decreased with mean summer air temperature (all p < 0.05; Figure 2e; Appendix S1: Figures S3 and S6d).Mean PNC showed no response to intra-or interspecific competition (Figure 2f; Appendix S1: Figure S3b).


Intraspecific variation response

We found evidence that lake trout populations exhibit intraspecific variation in food web attributes across gradients of ecosystem size and temperature, while exhibiting minimal changes across gradients in competition.Intraspecific variation in lake trout TP (dTP) increased (significantly or marginally significantly) with lake area when accounting for competition (n = 28 lakes; Figure 3a; Appendix S1: Figure S4a).However, across all 52 lakes without accounting for competition, there is no significant change in dTP (Appendix S1: Figure S7a).Additionally, in all models, dTP showed no response to mean summer air temperature, or intra/interspecific competition (Figure 3b,c; Appendix S1: Figures S4b,c and  S7b).Intraspecific variation in lake trout PNC (dNC) significantly increased with increasing lake area (Figure 3d; Appendix S1: Figure S4d) and decreased with increasing mean summer air temperature (Figure 3e; Appendix S1: Figure S4e; relationship significant in model with intraspecific competition).These trends were consistent and marginally significant across all 52 lakes (Appendix S1: Figure S7c,d).Lake trout dNC showed no response to intra-or interspecific competition (Figure 3f; Appendix S1: Figure S4f).These results suggest that there is less intraspecific variation in PNC use by lake trout in smaller lakes and under warmer conditions and potentially more intraspecific variation in lake trout TP in larger lakes.

Isotopic niche size (SEAc) significantly increased with increasing lake area in all models (Figure 4a; Appendix S1: Figures S5a and S8a; note marginal significance for extended lake dataset).SEAc demonstrated marginally significant declines with increasing mean summer air temperature (Figure 4b, Appendix S1: Figure S5b); however, in the extended lake dataset without accounting for competition, this trend disappeared (Appendix S1: Figure S8b).SEAc showed no change in response to intra-or interspecific competition (Figure 4c; Appendix S1: Figure S5c).

The dispersion in lake trout niche positions (SDNND) increased with increasing lake area, where this effect was significant when accounting for interspecific competition and in the extended lake dataset (Appendix S1: Figures S5d and S8c).When accounting for intraspecific competition, the trend was consistent but not significant (Figure 4d).SDNND showed no response to mean summer air temperature in all models (Figure 4e; Appendix S1: Figures S5e and S8d).Lastly, SDNND significantly increased with increasing intraspecific competition (Figure 4f); however, it did not change in response to interspecific competition (Appendix S1: Figure S5f).

Collectively, our results suggest that lake trout in larger lakes tend to demonstrate increased intraspecific variation in trophic structure, and increasing temperatures tend to reduce intraspecific variation.Interestingly, competition tended to have little to no effect on lake trout intraspecific variation metrics.


Exploring additional drivers of lake trout trophic structure

Lastly, we explored other potential drivers of lake trout trophic structure beyond our three leading hypotheses and largely found no significant responses between these additional lake attributes (lake shape, the proportion of littoral area, secchi depth, and phosphorus) and the six trophic metrics (mean TP, mean PNC, dTP, dNC, SEAc, and SDNND; Appendix S1: Tables S5-S8).However, we did see evidence of two significant relationships, where dTP significantly increased with increasing shoreline complexity (SDI; Appendix S1: Table S6) and mean TP significantly increased with increasing secchi depth (Appendix S1: Table S7).See Appendix S1 for discussion and interpretation of these results.S2).Fish illustrations by Caden McCann, thermometer illustrations by Cassandra Kotsopoulos, lake illustrations by Braden Fallon.


DISCUSSION

Previous work finds that lake trout food web structure changes across key environmental gradients such as ecosystem size, temperature, and competition (Dolson et al., 2009;Tunney et al., 2012Tunney et al., , 2014)).Here, we find that, consistent with these earlier findings, mean lake trout PNC decreased and mean TP increased with increasing summer air temperature.Additionally, we found mean PNC increased and mean TP did not change with increasing lake surface area.Analysis of intraspecific variation in lake trout trophic structure across these environmental gradients suggests that lake trout in larger lakes tend to demonstrate increased intraspecific variation in trophic structure, and increasing temperatures tend to reduce intraspecific variation (Figure 5).Specifically, larger lakes increased variation in PNC and TP, resulting in larger niche areas (SEAc), and warmer lakes reduced variation in PNC and tended to generate smaller niche areas.

Interestingly, both intra-and interspecific competition showed very little effect on lake trout mean and variation in trophic structure.Our results suggest that, contrary to existing implicit assumptions about boreal lake food web structure, intraspecific variation does change across environmental gradients like ecosystem size and temperature and may play an underappreciated role in structuring food webs and maintaining their resilience in response to global change.

Foraging and residing in the offshore habitat reduces exposure to warmer temperatures in the nearshore environment; during summer stratification, it is energetically inefficient for lake trout to acquire nearshore prey when offshore fish are available (Blanchfield et al., 2023;Bloomfield et al., 2022;Dolson et al., 2009;Guzzo et al., 2017;Tunney et al., 2014).Studies of fish movement using acoustic telemetry are consistent with those based on isotopic metrics, where lake trout use nearshore habitats in cooler seasons but avoid them in summer months when water temperatures are above their preferences.Additionally, in the absence of offshore prey fish, lake trout attempt to acquire nearshore prey most efficiently by using steep areas of the lake to avoid thermal stress (Rodrigues et al., 2025).These results provide further evidence of a reduction in nearshore accessibility in lake trout due to physiological constraints under warmer temperatures (Blanchfield et al., 2023;Bloomfield et al., 2022;Guzzo et al., 2017;Plumb & Blanchfield, 2009).Here, we focus on the summer months; however, other seasons should be considered in future work as other studies have  S2).Fish illustrations by Caden McCann, thermometer illustrations by Cassandra Kotsopoulos, lake illustrations by Braden Fallon.

In larger lakes, lake trout tended to increase foraging on nearshore resources and expressed greater intraspecific variation in nearshore foraging and TP, resulting in larger isotopic niche areas (SEAc) and greater dispersion (SDNND).These results suggest that in larger lakes, individual lake trout exhibit more individual diversity in foraging patterns across nearshore and offshore habitats and across different trophic guilds, exploiting resources at different levels of the food web (Figure 5).Interestingly, the increase in mean nearshore foraging is contrary to previous research where the mean proportion of nearshore carbon in lake trout diets tends to decrease as lake size increases (Eloranta et al., 2015;Vander Zanden & Vadeboncoeur, 2002).This suggests that the mean pattern of reduced nearshore foraging in larger lakes appears to break down as we expand latitudinal gradients (Tunney et al., 2014).Past studies have mainly examined this relationship over a limited geographical range, potentially reducing temperature effects (Eloranta et al., 2015;Vander Zanden & Vadeboncoeur, 2002); however, the latitudinal gradient used in our study is approximately four times greater than those of previous studies.Under such F I G U R E 5 Schematic of the intraspecific variation (a-e) and mean responses (c-e) in lake trout food web attributes across gradients of ecosystem size, temperature and intraspecific competition (lake trout catch per unit effort [CPUE]).(a) As temperature increases, the littoral zone is thermally inaccessible in the summer and lake trout increase their reliance on pelagic resources, reducing intraspecific variation in nearshore habitat use.In smaller lakes there is less intraspecific variation in nearshore habitat use since lake trout are in a smaller ecosystem with less resources available to them, which decreases their opportunity to demonstrate variation.(b) Under colder temperatures, there is more intraspecific variation in nearshore habitat use because the littoral zone is thermally accessible, allowing lake trout to forage on resources in the nearshore habitat.As lake size increases, lake trout have access to a wider range of resources, allowing them to demonstrate more intraspecific variation in nearshore carbon use.widespread latitudinal change, accompanied by a large range in lake temperatures, our mean results are consistent with Tunney et al. Additionally, earlier research has reported significant increases in mean TP of apex predators with increasing lake size, whereas we found no change in TP (Eloranta et al., 2015;Tunney et al., 2012Tunney et al., , 2014)).Notably, all the lakes used in our study contain the pelagic forage fish cisco, such that this food source was available to lake trout in all lakes.Our results may have differed if we included smaller lakes without cisco or other pelagic forage fishes.The absence of pelagic forage fishes, such as cisco, may result in lower TP with more among-lake variation as lake trout would tend to have a higher reliance on lower trophic-level resources in the offshore like Mysis or Chaoborus (Pazzia et al., 2002;Vander Zanden, Shuter, et al., 1999) and their piscivory may be restricted to forays into the nearshore zone in summer (Rodrigues et al., 2025).

The increase in intraspecific variability in nearshore coupling and trophic position with increasing lake size suggests that our results are consistent with the Resource Diversity-Size Hypothesis (Table 1).Given that resource diversity increases with ecosystem size (Cohen & Newman, 1991;MacArthur & Wilson, 1967;Post et al., 2000;Schoener, 1989), intraspecific variation in foraging likely also increases with lake size as individuals have a greater opportunity to diversify when presented with a wider range of prey items.Consistent with this, we found a positive correlation between species richness and lake area for our study lakes (Appendix S1: Figure S2).Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated structural changes in food web metrics with the addition of keystone pelagic resources (Devlin et al., 2017;Ridgway et al., 2020;Vander Zanden, Shuter, et al., 1999).For example, Ridgway et al. (2020) found that the isotopic niche size of cisco was twice as large in lakes containing the predatory crustacean Mysis diluviana.Additionally, cisco in lakes with M. diluviana exhibited an expansion in habitat use and phenotypic diversification (Ridgway et al., 2020).Taken altogether, this increase in resource diversity via ecosystem size may be fueling the increased intraspecific variation that we observed in larger lakes.Thus, it is important to consider the implications of these findings for boreal lake ecosystems in the context of global change and increasing ecosystem fragmentation, as past research has found a collapse in predator niche width in fragmented aquatic systems driven by a reduction in prey diversity (Layman, Arrington, et al., 2007;Layman, Quattrochi, et al., 2007).

Lastly, we found little evidence that competition influenced mean or intraspecific variation in lake trout trophic structure.The only relationship we found was a reduced dispersion of individual lake trout in isotopic niche space with increasing levels of intraspecific competition represented by a reduction in SDNND with increasing lake trout CPUE.This suggests that as intraspecific competition increases, lake trout demonstrate more evenness and tighter clustering in isotopic space, which is indicative of similar resource use.This response contrasts what was suggested by the Competition Hypothesis (Table 1).We initially expected increased levels of intraspecific competition to promote diversification in lake trout foraging strategies, as they may exploit resources that are not used by their competitors, which is similar to the mechanism illustrated in earlier research used to relieve the pressures of intraspecific competition (see Competition Hypothesis, Table 1) (Svanbäck & Bolnick, 2007).However, our results suggest that the opposite phenomenon may be occurring, and individuals demonstrate more uniform foraging strategies with increased levels of competition.Work examining the effects of intra-and interspecific competition in European whitefish demonstrated a hump-shaped relationship with increasing abundance of sympatric competitors (Eloranta et al., 2022), suggesting that potentially our gradient in CPUE spans from intermediate to high competitor density.However, given the lack of effect of competition on other trophic metrics, it is possible the resource base is abundant enough to support larger lake trout populations, and they do not have to diversify their prey sources when faced with increased levels of competition.

Mobile predators play a vital role in ecosystems, as they can forage across a range of macrohabitats, creating food web linkages where energy channels and nutrients are coupled (McCann et al., 2005).In addition, habitat coupling can have stabilizing effects on food webs as predators can alter their foraging behavior and exploit a new flow of resources in response to the depletion of prey from a single habitat, ultimately reducing consumerresource oscillations (Kondoh, 2003;McCann et al., 2005;McMeans et al., 2016;Rooney et al., 2006).Our results, along with past work, have shown that increased temperatures can result in a decoupling effect and less variation in habitat use by lake trout (Tunney et al., 2014).Furthermore, theoretical ecology has argued that this decoupling effect can reduce lake trout biomass and trophic control, reducing the offshore predator-prey interaction.This may have a stabilizing effect if the predator population is inflated due to strong trophic control, or it could have a destabilizing effect if the energy to the predator is limited (McCann, 2011;McCann et al., 2005;Rooney et al., 2006).In our case, we see a compression in space as the nearshore zone becomes inaccessible, and the reliance on pelagic resources increases; if this happened within a lake, it may increase the interaction strength between lake trout and prey fish in the offshore habitat temporarily until there is a numerical response in lake trout (McCann et al., 2005).Therefore, the ability to express intraspecific variation in habitat use is important as it allows populations to demonstrate diverse foraging and ultimately promotes stability.This is particularly important to consider for lake trout and other cold water food webs under global change as temperatures continue to increase, further reducing nearshore habitat accessibility.

Our results provide evidence that large, cool lakes ought to provide increased adaptive capacity that can promote stability in lake trout food webs as these lakes facilitate greater trophic diversification across habitats and prey types, allowing lake trout to maintain variable foraging behaviors.Our observations are consistent with earlier research and suggest that increased temperatures, in addition to decoupling habitats, can reduce the variability in these systems, which can potentially lead to further destabilization (Eloranta et al., 2015;McCann et al., 2005;Tunney et al., 2012).

It has been recognized that resilient ecosystems are composed of species that can demonstrate differential responses to perturbations (McCann, 2000;Naeem, 1998;Naeem & Li, 1997;Yachi & Loreau, 1999).Based on our findings, increased temperatures and smaller ecosystem sizes appear to reduce the ability to express intraspecific variation by a cold-water predator.This suggests that a warming climate and a reduction of ecosystem size, for example, by habitat fragmentation, can have implications for adaptive capacity and ultimately ecosystem resilience.Therefore, it is paramount that we work toward conserving the intraspecific variation in lake trout food webs, more so as ecosystems are increasingly disrupted by global change.
