Introduction

Environmental pollution has emerged as one of the most critical challenges to global public health.While the physical consequences, such as respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, are well documented, the impact on mental health has only recently begun to receive attention.Although less visible, this aspect is equally significant and demands thorough exploration [1].Recent studies suggest that many infectious and contagious diseases are caused, directly or indirectly, by organisms that Everyday environments, including the air we breathe, water we drink, and soil we touch, are often contaminated with harmful chemicals and particles.These pollutants not only affect the body but also the mind.Continuous exposure to fine particles and toxic chemicals has been linked to various mental and emotional disorders [6].Studies suggest that air pollution increases oxidative stress in the brain, promoting inflammation and neurochemical changes that may trigger or worsen mental health conditions such as depression and anxiety [7].Globally, nations are striving to reverse this trend by promoting environmental care.One of the primary causes of the problem is unregulated population growth without proper urban planning, followed by industrialization.Notably, around 20% of industrialized nations report mental health issues directly linked to environmental pollution [8].

The psychological impact of pollution extends beyond its biological effects.It also includes social and emotional consequences [9].Communities most affected by pollution often have limited economic resources and reduced access to healthcare.This inequality increases stress and anxiety, creating a cycle of vulnerability and hopelessness [10].It is, therefore, crucial to examine the psychological consequences of polluted environments.Environmental stimuli can significantly affect mood and behavior.When these stimuli exceed an individual's adaptive capacity, they may act as powerful stressors capable of causing psychological harm [11].Living in a polluted environment carries an emotional burden that can lead to mental health deterioration.Perceived risk, helplessness, and reduced quality of life can intensify psychological distress.This often manifests in symptoms like headaches, fatigue, depression, and anxiety [12].Additionally, uncertainty about the long-term effects of pollution can increase stress [13].

Pollution can affect brain development on a neurobiological level, particularly in children and adolescents [14].These are critical periods for cognitive and emotional growth; exposure to pollutants during these stages can have long-term consequences.Studies show air pollution is linked to children's attention deficit, hyperactivity, and cognitive decline [15].Historically, the environment has played a crucial role in psychiatry and psychology, helping to shape our understanding of mental illness [16].

Environmental pollution remains a leading cause of disease in Peru, especially among children under five.Exposure to pathogens through unclean environments can result in severe health conditions or even death [17].In Lima, one of the cities that generates the most garbage in Peru, the lack of adequate waste management programs causes dissatisfaction among 15% of the population.It increases the risk of diseases such as acute mental disorders (8.6%), intestinal parasitism (2.2%), diarrhea (1.1%), dengue fever (0.3%), and malaria (0.1%) [18].Similarly, a study in Lima revealed that only 1.8% of residents consider the public cleaning service good, while 98.2% rate it as bad or very bad, attributing this perception to the lack of adequate waste collection programs [19].

In Puno, critical environmental problems include inadequate solid waste management, which pollutes water, soil, and air [20], and only 20% of the rural population of Juliaca has access to water and sewage services connected to a public network, which causes related diseases [21].This situation in Juliaca is attributed to poor public cleaning management and rapid population growth without adequate urban planning [22].Projects such as cleaning rainwater channels and designing efficient urban drainage are necessary to improve order in the city, promoting orderly development that protects its inhabitants' physical and mental health [23].

Satisfaction with cleanliness is defined as the degree to which people feel happy and satisfied with the level of cleanliness in their environment, whether in their homes, workplaces, public spaces, or communities in general; this satisfaction can influence people's physical and emotional well-being, as well as their perception of quality of life [24].Likewise, individuals or groups perceive and positively value hygiene, order, and the absence of pollutants in a specific environment based on public health criteria, comfort, functionality, and psychological wellbeing [20].

The Elementary Theory and Principles of Cleanliness were used as the foundation for this instrument.According to this theory, exposure to odors and decomposing matter that emit toxic gases can harm physical and mental health.Cleanliness is therefore crucial not only for disease prevention but also for mental well-being.It promotes a sense of comfort and positivity [25].Environmental pollution is strongly related to mental health, with urban areas reporting higher rates of psychiatric disorders than rural ones [9].

Based on this theory, two key dimensions were identified.The first is Fresh Waste, which includes wet, sticky, and smelly waste such as food remains and animal droppings.This waste causes discomfort and stress and can impair sleep, focus, and mood [25].The second is Powdered Waste, which is dry waste that becomes airborne dust.It can affect food, utensils, and air quality, resulting in physical and mental health issues [26].This exposure often leads to increased medication use, fatigue, and emotional problems [9].

Whinery's theory of cleaning up fresh and powdery waste and the Environmental Pollution Perception dimension underline the importance of maintaining a clean environment for physical health and mental and psychological well-being [26].Effectively cleaning this waste can reduce stress and anxiety, improve air quality and the visual environment, and provide a sense of control and accomplishment [27].These benefits are essential to promote a healthy and balanced environment at home and in other spaces frequented by people [28].The causes of dissatisfaction with non-cleanliness are environmental pollution due to the accumulation of waste, garbage, and other waste found in streets, markets, and rivers, and this can lead to health problems and less safety for people and animals in addition to being unpleasant to the eye and smell [29].

The evaluation of cleanliness in its different aspects is of great importance worldwide, as it influences the perception and well-being of people in various environments.Specific instruments have been developed to measure this variable: The Cleanliness Perceptions Scale (CPS) [29], designed to assess how customers perceive cleanliness in specific elements of the service environment and how these impact on a physical and psychological level.The scale consists of 24 items and three dimensions, which explain 72.9% of the total variance, with a high Cronbach's alpha coefficient in its dimensions: clean places (0.94), cool places (0.92), and clear places (0.84).In addition, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were performed, which showed good adjustment results.Furthermore, the New York City Service Satisfaction Survey (SSNYCS) [30], with 20 items and five dimensions, has a reliability of 0.729 and KMO = 0.90, but only an exploratory factor analysis was performed, which limits the robustness of its results; The Cleaner Production (CP) scale, aimed at measuring satisfaction with street cleanliness in the United States [31], consists of 25 items and five dimensions, with exceptional reliability of 0.988 and KMO = 0.981.However, this instrument was limited to eliminating three dimensions during development, which affected the original theoretical structure and underscored the challenges in adjusting theoretical models in practice.

Although instruments such as the Cleanliness Perceptions Scale (CPS) and the Cleaner Production (CP) scale have demonstrated strong psychometric properties in their respective contexts, these tools were developed for different sociocultural settings and with specific evaluative goals, such as customer satisfaction in service environments or municipal waste collection performance.These instruments were not designed to assess the perceived relationship between cleanliness and mental health or to reflect the contextual realities of cleanliness in Peruvian urban and rural areas.Furthermore, no cross-cultural adaptations or validations of these instruments have been reported in the Peruvian context.Therefore, a new instrument was necessary to capture culturally relevant dimensions of cleanliness satisfaction that align with adults' physical, psychological, and environmental conditions in the San Román, Peru province.Therefore, the general objective of this study is to build and validate an instrument that brings together the appropriate psychometric properties to measure satisfaction with cleanliness in adults from the province of San Román, Peru-concerning the specific objectives, which are to obtain evidence of the content validity index of the scale, to obtain evidence of the scale's construct validity, and finally, to determine the reliability by internal consistency, and invariance by sex to measure satisfaction with cleanliness in adults from the province of San Román, Peru.


Materials and methods

The present study used a quantitative approach, a nonexperimental, cross-sectional, instrumental design [32].


Instrument

The development of the ESL-AK was carried out through a systematic process.First, the theoretical dimensions of satisfaction with cleanliness were defined from the existing literature [25].Based on these dimensions, an initial bank of items was generated theoretically and empirically: the authors created the items, ensuring that they covered the relevant aspects of each dimension.Then, a panel of psychometric experts evaluated the relevance and clarity of the items, refining the wording of those that were redundant or unclear.The preliminary version of the ESL-AK was obtained from this procedure, consisting of 2 theoretically defined dimensions with 23 items.From this, the corresponding psychometric analyses were carried out.After the EFA, 2 items were eliminated, and the remaining 21 were grouped into 4 dimensions.The names of the 2 new dimensions were decided by reviewing the items that compose them and comparing them with the theoretical background.Finally, the AFC showed the final structure of the instrument with 21 items in 4 dimensions.The final version is found in the supplementary material to this article.


Participants

The sample for the study was obtained through a nonprobabilistic sampling for convenience [33].This group was selected according to the inclusion criteria, which included being a San Román resident over 18 and under 60 years of age and having signed the informed consent.The total sample (n = 866) was divided into two independent subsamples to validate the structure of the scale.The first group of 400 participants was used for Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), while the second group, with the remaining 466 participants, was used for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).This division of the sample was performed randomly, ensuring that both subsamples were comparable in key demographic characteristics.


Sociodemographic characterization (exploratory analysis)

Table 1 shows a total of 400 inhabitants of the province of San Román, of which 258 (64.5%) are female; in the same way, in the study, 241 (60.25%) report that they are between 18 and 30 years old, likewise, 222 (55.5%) report that they are from the rural area.About the degree of education, 161 (40.25%) mention that they only have a degree of technical instruction.Concerning the district where 140 (35%) live, they say they live in San Miguel and Juliaca.


Sociodemographic characterization (confirmatory sample)

Table 2 surveyed a total of 466 inhabitants of the province of San Román, of which 318 (68.2%) reported being female; in addition, 363 (77.9%) were between 18 and 30 years old, and 297 (63.7%) said they were from rural areas, and 199 (42.7%) said they had a "technical" degree of education.Finally, 63 (35%) reported living in the San Miguel district.


Data collection technique

For data collection techniques, permission was first requested to collect data from the inhabitants of the City of Juliaca, according to available time.This questionnaire was applied in person to assess the participant's perception of the evaluation and avoid biases in the collection process.To do this, the Cleanliness Satisfaction Scale (ESL-AK) instrument built by the authors of this study was used, which consisted of 23 items and 2 dimensions: Fresh Waste, Powder Residues, Cleaning Quality, and Visual Impact; this scale can be evaluated individually or in groups, and although it does not have an exact duration it can be completed in 10 to 15 min, the response category is a 5-point Likert stake.


Data analysis

The statistical techniques used in the study included descriptive statistical analyses, such as relative and absolute frequencies for the variables.Likewise, central tendency and dispersion measures were estimated, as well as asymmetry and kurtosis for the intervening variable.Subsequently, based on 8 expert judges, Aiken's V was assessed, considering those greater than (V > 0.70) valid in the indicators of representativeness, coherence, and clarity [34].Next, the Mardia coefficient was tested for multivariate normality, the Exploratory Factor Analysis was carried out using the Minimal Residues extraction method, which is more robust for samples that do not present multivariate normality, and the Oblimin rotation method for the definition of factors, parallel analysis and scree plot were used, as for the factor loads of each item, they were considered adequate when they are greater than 0.30.In addition, the suitability of the sample was verified using the Kaiser-Meyer Olkin test (KMO > 0.50), as well as Bartlett's sphericity test (p <.01) [35].Then, the Mardia coefficient was tested for multivariate normality; once the number of factors was formed, the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) of the model was performed using a WLSMV (Weighted Least Square Mean and Variance Adjusted) estimator, considering that the items have 5 categories of Likert-type response and the sample size was sufficient for an adequate power of the estimator, it is also an unbiased estimator for items with ordinal responses and non-normal distribution.From this, the standard measures of goodness of fit were reported: The comparative fit index (CFI), which is adequate when it is > 0.90; the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), which is acceptable when it is > 0.90.The standardized mean square residual (SRMR) and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) are considered adequate with values < 0.08.Reliability was found using Cronbach's Alpha Coefficient and McDonald's Omega.Measurement invariance by sex and population was analyzed, too.All analyses were performed in the statistical software Jamovi [37] and R, version 4.4.3[38].


Ethical aspects

The ethics committee of the UPEU Graduate School approved the study with the number 2024-CEEPG-00109.After the data were collected and consent was requested and reported, it should be noted that the participants voluntarily approved their participation in the study, considering whether to participate in the evaluation.Furthermore, all personal information is kept anonymous following articles 36 and 65 of the Code of Ethics of the College of Psychologists of Peru.Additionally, the purposes and results of our research are communicated using clear and understandable language to avoid any situation that could jeopardize the emotional stability of the person being evaluated.In case of any issues, the contact information for the authors responsible for this research will be provided following the Declaration of Helsinki [39].

Invariance testing by sex

Table 9 shows measurement invariance of the four-factor model examined through multigroup confirmatory factor analysis by sex.The configural model showed an acceptable fit (CFI = 0.933, RMSEA = 0.085), indicating that the factor structure is equivalent across the sexes.Model fit remained stable when constraining factor loadings   (metric invariance) (ΔCFI = 0.000; p =.682), supporting the equivalence of item-factor relationships.The scalar model showed a slight decrease in fit (ΔCFI = − 0.002), with a statistically significant chi-square difference (p =.038); however, the ΔCFI remained within acceptable limits (≤ 0.01), suggesting practical support for scalar invariance.Finally, the strict invariance model exhibited a significant drop in fit (ΔCFI = − 0.003; p <.001), indicating that residual variances differ across groups.Overall, the results support configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance, allowing for meaningful latent mean comparisons between males and females.


Invariance testing by population

Table 10 shows measurement invariance of the fourfactor model assessed across population groups (urban vs. rural) using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis.The configural model demonstrated an acceptable fit (CFI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.079), indicating that the factorial structure is comparable across groups.When factor loadings were constrained to be equal (metric     invariance), model fit remained stable (ΔCFI = − 0.001; p =.251), supporting the equivalence of item-factor associations between populations.The scalar model, which added constraints on item intercepts, showed no significant reduction in fit (ΔCFI = 0.000; p =.417), providing evidence for scalar invariance.Similarly, the strict model, which also constrained residual variances, did not yield a significant deterioration in fit (ΔCFI = 0.000; p =.347).

Overall, the findings support full configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance, indicating that the four-factor model operates equivalently across urban and rural populations.This invariance permits valid comparisons of latent means and observed scores across groups.


Discussion

The scientific literature and community of psychology and public health specialists highlight the importance of assessing the perception of satisfaction with cleanliness in various environments, especially in urban contexts.A fundamental reason lies in the need to identify the factors that contribute to the population's well-being and quality of life and their impact on physical and emotional health [40].In the case of the surveyed population, aspects such as waste management, maintenance of public spaces, and citizen participation can significantly influence their perception of cleanliness and their general satisfaction with the environment in which they live.In this sense, the objective of this research was to build and validate an instrument that brings together the appropriate psychometric properties to measure satisfaction with cleanliness in adults from the province of San Román, Peru, to identify areas for improvement, and implement strategies that promote a cleaner and healthier environment.The first specific objective was to obtain evidence of the validity of content through the review of eight expert judges.The judges evaluated the clarity, congruence, relevance, and adequacy to the context of the items, obtaining an overall Aiken V index of V = 0.95; 95% CI [0.782-0.99],indicating robust validity.By dimensions, factor 1, "Solid Waste Management, " reached V = 0.94; 95% CI [0.771-0.99];moreover, factor 2, "Residues in the dust, " also showed acceptable indices V = 0.95; 95% CI [0.791-0.99].These results are comparable with previous studies, which investigated the effects of citizen insecurity and resulted in the validity of content V = 0.87, indicating its validity [41].These results are because the items were carefully designed based on theoretical constructs and previous evidence, ensuring their alignment with the instrument's objectives.In addition, the evaluation process by expert judges allowed an exhaustive review of the linguistic and conceptual aspects, ensuring that each item adequately measured the proposed dimensions.The Aiken V index is a statistical method to measure content validity, where values greater than 0.8 are considered adequate; this index evaluates the agreement between judges on aspects such as clarity, relevance, and coherence of the items considered that an instrument has acceptable content validity if the indices of agreement among the experts reach values greater than 0.8 [42].

Meanwhile, the other specific objective is to obtain evidence of the validity of the construct, and for this purpose, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were carried out.The exploratory analysis yielded a KMO = 0.931 and Bartlett's test = 0.001, the total variance explained is 58.3%, distributed in four main factors; for the confirmatory factor analysis, the initial structure of 19 items showed moderately acceptable adjusted indices (X² = 131; TLI = 0.953; CFI = 0.960; RMSEA = 0.037; SRMR = 0.036).These results are consistent with studies such as [43], where the KMO = 0.80 and the Bartlett test = p < 0.001.Likewise, the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was carried out, in which better values could be presented by adjusting to the initial model (CFI >.90, SRMR <.08, NFI >.95, RMSEA <.08), as well as the study by [44] where a result of KMO =.981 was obtained, and as for the confirmatory analysis, it had a value of (SRMR =.043).These results can be explained by the fact that the evaluated instruments have a solid theoretical structure that supports grouping the items into specific factors.In addition, the initial design considered key psychometric criteria, such as the items' clarity, relevance to the dimensions assessed, and internal homogeneity, making it easier for factor analyses to identify consistent patterns in the data.The above is supported by Kline, who indicates that values greater than 0.8 are considered 'meritorious, ' and greater than 0.9 are 'excellent' [45].Similarly, Vos et al. state that Bartlett's test must be significant (p < 0.05) for the correlation matrix to be adequate for factor analysis [44].Finally, Kline argues that the adjustment indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) should be evaluated jointly [30].

Regarding the last specific objective, which is internal consistency, Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω coefficients were used, obtaining global values of α = 0.935 and ω = 0.936, which reflect excellent reliability.By dimensions, the consistency was adequate: fresh waste α = 0.889 and ω = 0.886, solid waste α = 0.902 and ω = 0.903, perception of cleanliness α = 0.862 and ω = 0.864, hygiene and health α = 0.867 and ω = 0.867.These results are similar to the studies of Van Rizing [46], who had reliability with Cronbach's alpha coefficient α = 0.729, as well as the study by [34] in which they conducted a study and had a reliability coefficient: α = 0.95 and ω = 0.95.Based on the above, we can see that the instruments evaluated have a solid theoretical structure that supports the grouping of the items into specific factors; in addition, the initial design considered key psychometric criteria, such as the clarity of the items, their relevance to the dimensions evaluated and internal homogeneity, which facilitated the factor analyses to identify consistent patterns in the data.Some authors who support these results are (49), who introduced the α coefficient as a measure of internal consistency to evaluate the homogeneity of items in an instrument and point out that values greater than 0.7 are acceptable.In comparison, values greater than 0.9 indicate high internal consistency.Furthermore, the measurement invariance analysis by sex confirmed that the Cleanliness Satisfaction Scale (ESL-AK) maintains a stable factorial structure across male and female participants.The results supported configural and metric invariance (ΔCFI = 0.000), as well as partial scalar invariance (ΔCFI = − 0.002), indicating that the factor structure, as well as the strength of the relationships between items and latent constructs, are equivalent between groups.Although strict invariance was not fully achieved (ΔCFI = − 0.003), configural and metric invariance allows meaningful comparisons of latent means across sexes.These findings reinforce the psychometric robustness of the ESL-AK and its applicability in diverse demographic groups, aligning with recommended practices in scale validation literature that emphasize ΔCFI values below 0.01 as indicative of acceptable invariance [30,44].

The measurement invariance analysis across urban and rural populations confirmed that the Cleanliness Satisfaction Scale (ESL-AK) exhibits a stable and equivalent factorial structure in both groups.The configural model showed acceptable fit (CFI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.079), and subsequent models demonstrated invariance at the metric (ΔCFI = − 0.001; p =.251), scalar (ΔCFI = 0.000; p =.417), and strict (ΔCFI = 0.000; p =.347) levels.These results indicate that the factor structure, the strength of item-factor relationships, the item intercepts, and the residual variances are equivalent across populations.Therefore, the ESL-AK allows meaningful comparisons of latent means and observed scores between urban and rural respondents.These results reinforce the psychometric soundness of the ESL-AK and its applicability across diverse population contexts.The ability to generalize the factor structure and score interpretation across urban and rural respondents aligns with best practices in cross-group validation.It supports the scale's use in population-level comparisons.

The study's implications have important issues for public health since it allows for an accurate assessment of how adults from the province of San Román, Peru, perceive the conditions of cleanliness in their daily environments.Likewise, the scale contributes to public health awareness and education since the results can be used to develop awareness campaigns on the importance of cleanliness and hygiene in preventing diseases.In addition, it allows monitoring of the impact of public policies and sanitation strategies in different communities, providing evidence to optimize resources and prioritize actions.

The study had the following limitations: that the study lacks concurrent criteria (convergent/divergent), expanding the sample diversity to achieve greater representativeness and robustness in the results, as well as validating the study in similar, close and even distant international contexts, and being translated into other languages for its validation in different countries with different languages, in addition, in the study the temporal reliability (Test -Retest) was not performed, in the same way, the instrument does not have a scale to qualify the population because the population margin is greater than 6000 people and due to time it could not be carried out.


Conclusion

The construction of this scale aimed at measuring satisfaction with cleanliness (ESL-AK) becomes essential in the current context, where the perception of hygiene and maintenance of space directly impact people's quality of life and emotional well-being.Likewise, the psychometric properties of this scale guarantee its validity and reliability as a measurement tool.It is composed of a total of 4 dimensions and 19 items, having validity of (V = 0.95), and reliability of dimensions of fresh waste (α = 0.889 and ω = 0.886), the solid waste dimension (α = 0.902 and ω = 0.903, the cleaning quality dimension (α = 0.862 and ω = 0.864), finally, the visual impact dimension (α = 0.867 and ω = 0.867), Despite the limitations, this research is considered to offer a significant contribution to
