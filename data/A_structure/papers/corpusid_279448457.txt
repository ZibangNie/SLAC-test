Correspondence on "Early dynamics of clinical and laboratory parameters predict primary refractory disease in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma receiving atezolizumab"by Graser et al

We read with great interest the recent article by Graser et al 1 titled 'Early dynamics of clinical and laboratory parameters predict primary refractory disease in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma receiving atezolizumab' published in the Journal for ImmunoTherapy of Cancer.The authors proposed a novel model incorporating earlyon-treatment parameters to predict primary refractory disease in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) receiving atezolizumab.This study represents a valuable effort in the field of precision oncology, aiming to identify patients who are less likely to benefit from immunotherapy early in the treatment course.However, we would like to raise several concerns regarding the methodology and interpretation of the results, which we believe warrant further investigation.

The study includes PD-L1 expression (IC2, OR=0.805) as a predictor in the model.However, it overlooks the dynamic changes in PD-L1 expression during treatment.Emerging evidence indicates that PD-L1 expression can evolve under therapeutic pressure.For instance, studies have shown that PD-L1 expression can fluctuate over time in response to treatment, 2 and baseline measurements alone may not accurately predict treatment outcomes.This static approach may lead to an underestimation of the risk of treatment failure in patients who develop adaptive immune escape mechanisms.As a result, the model may have an increased false-negative rate for patients who initially express low levels of PD-L1 but later develop resistance through immune escape.This limitation could be addressed by incorporating longitudinal measurements of PD-L1 expression to capture its dynamic changes over time.

The LASSO model used in the study includes 35 variables for 483 patients, resulting in a variable-to-sample ratio exceeding 1:14.This high dimensionality poses a significant risk of overfitting, especially given the relatively small sample size.Previous research has highlighted that the stability of variable selection in LASSO models diminishes in high-dimensional settings without external validation. 3The authors relied solely on 10-fold cross-validation within the same trial cohort, which can introduce optimism bias and overestimate the model's performance.For example, a study by Varma and Simon 4 demonstrated that cross-validation alone may not be sufficient to prevent overfitting in high-dimensional data.Moreover, some variables included in the model, such as urine-specific gravity (OR=1.504),lack a clear mechanistic link to immunotherapy resistance, raising concerns about spurious associations.To mitigate these risks, external validation in independent cohorts and the use of more robust regularisation techniques should be considered.

The model compares the efficacy of atezolizumab to chemotherapy but fails to account for significant differences in post-progression treatments.For instance, in similar trials, 37.8% of patients in the placebo arm crossed over to receive PARP inhibitors. 5This discrepancy violates the 'overlap assumption' in causal inference, which requires that all patients have a non-zero probability of receiving either treatment.The violation of

this assumption can lead to biased estimates of treatment effects and invalidate personalized treatment recommendations.Future studies should consider more sophisticated causal inference methods, such as propensity score matching or inverse probability weighting, to address this issue.

The study data were obtained from the IMvigor211 trial data provided through the Vivli platform.Vivli is a data-sharing platform designed to promote the sharing and reuse of clinical trial data.However, the key issue lies in whether the informed consent forms of the original trial explicitly covered the use of data for subsequent secondary analyses.If patients, when participating in the original trial, only consented to their data being used for the specific research purposes of that trial and did not explicitly authorize the use of their data for subsequent other studies (such as the development of predictive models), then using these data for secondary analyses may constitute an infringement on patients' autonomy.Patients have the right to determine the scope of use of their personal data, and unauthorized secondary use may violate their informed consent rights.

To enhance the robustness and clinical applicability of the model, we suggest the following improvements: 1. Incorporate Longitudinal PD-L1 Measurements: Future studies should include repeated measurements of PD-L1 expression to capture its dynamic changes during treatment.This approach has been shown to improve the accuracy of predictive models in other cancer types.2. External Validation: The model should be validated in independent cohorts to assess its generalizability and robustness.External validation can help identify potential overfitting and ensure that the model performs well in diverse patient populations.3. Robust Regularization Techniques: Consider using more advanced regularization methods, such as elastic net regularization, which combines L1 and L2 penalties to improve variable selection stability.

4. Causal Inference Methods: Apply causal inference techniques to account for differences in post-progression treatments and ensure that treatment comparisons are valid.5. Explicitly confirm the content of the informed consent forms from the original trial.

In conclusion, while the study by Graser et al presents an innovative approach to predicting primary refractory disease in mUC patients, addressing the aforementioned limitations is crucial for translating these findings into clinically actionable tools.We believe that incorporating dynamic biomarkers, external validation, and robust statistical methods will significantly enhance the model's performance and clinical relevance.
