Introduction

The thyroid gland is one of the largest endocrine glands, secreting thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). [1]9] Mild hypothyroidism can have many negative consequences, even though clinically the patients are euthyroid.Many research studies were conducted to evaluate the serum creatinine levels in hypothyroidism however, relatively little research was conducted to evaluate serum urea and serum uric acid (UA) levels in subclinical hypothyroidism.Local literature on this issue is also very limited.As a result, this study was carried out to investigate urea, UA, and creatinine levels in patients with hypothyroidism.Exclusion criteria: Subjects with known cases of chronic kidney diseases (CKDs), muscular dystrophies, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, smoking, alcoholism, and patients taking allopurinol and probenecid were excluded from the study.


Materials and Methods


Source of data


Method of sample collection

A convenient sampling method was used to collect the samples.A detailed history was obtained from the patients.

Five millilitres of venous blood was collected in a plain tube from the patients who were diagnosed with primary hypothyroidism Serum was separated from the whole blood sample and it was used for the estimation of the following parameters Urea by kinetic method (GLDH method)

UA by uricase method

Creatinine by Jaffe's method Tests were done with the help of Erba's fully automated analyser Values were noted and processed for statistical significance


Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the mean and co-efficient of variation.The variables were presented in terms of mean and standard deviation.Pearson's correlation coefficient test was done to see the correlation of serum urea, UA, and creatinine levels with the TSH levels.'P' values <0.05 were considered significant.


Results

The patients in the age group between 20 and 60 years were included in the study, which involved a sample size of 96.

Gender distribution is shown in Table 1, and Figure 1 suggests that the number of Primary hypothyroidism cases were more in females (83%) than males (17%).

Increased levels of urea, UA and creatinine were seen in the age group of 20-30 years when compared with the other age groups [Figure 2 and Table 2].

From the Pearson correlation coefficient for TSH, it was observed that TSH was positively correlated with urea (0.0161) [Figure 3 and Table 3] while TSH was negatively correlated with UA (0.0011) [Figure 4] and creatinine (0.0029) [Figure 5 and Table 3].


Discussion

The study was done to determine the level of serum urea, UA, and creatinine in patients of primary hypothyroidism and to find the correlation between TSH and serum urea, UA and creatinine.Patients in the age group 20-60 years were included in the present study.Out of the 96 study subjects, 83% were females and 17% were males, which showed that hypothyroidism was found on the higher side in females compared to males.Tejomani M et al. [10] found that women were more likely than men to have hypothyroidism which was like the present study.Another study done by Qahtan A. Rashead et al. [11] also showed that the distribution of the study population had a higher percentage of females than the percentage of males.

There was no statistical significance in the mean values of urea, UA and creatinine As shown in Table 4, which is contradictory to a study done by Gagandeep Kaur Sidhu et al. [12] According to Kaur et al., mean serum urea, creatinine and UA levels were found to be significantly higher in hypothyroid patients compared to controls which shows that hypothyroidism is associated with deteriorating renal function.Hypothyroid-induced renal dysfunction may lead to adverse clinical consequences, especially among patients on medications cleared by the kidneys.The thyroid function should, therefore, be regularly monitored for evaluation in patients presenting with deranged renal function and vice versa.

Adverse clinical outcomes can result from hypothyroid-induced renal impairment, particularly in individuals who are taking drugs that are renally cleared.Thus, thyroid function needs to be regularly evaluated for the assessment of individuals who exhibit impaired renal function and vice versa.

Studies by Kreisman and Hennessey et al., Khan AH and Majumder, and Vaneet Kaur et al. [16][17][18] also concluded mean creatinine level was significantly higher in hypothyroid cases.In hypothyroid patients, the serum creatinine content rises because of a decreased GFR due to hemodynamic abnormalities because of severe hypothyroidism. [19]rum creatinine levels fluctuate quickly and seem to be reversible.Understanding this relationship and the possibility that it explains creatinine increase in a hypothyroid patient may be therapeutically significant.Additionally, it should notify the physician to consider evaluating thyroid function in a patient whose thyroid status is unclear but who has a modest rise in serum creatinine and UA.

Hypothyroid myopathy may also cause a rise in serum creatinine levels. [17]When individuals present with acute renal failure and high muscle enzymes, hypothyroidism must be considered.Reduced cardiac output, increased systemic and renal vasoconstriction, decreased renal blood and plasma flow, and decreased GFR could all be contributing factors to the renal impairment.

The final by-product of dietary and endogenous purine metabolism, which is impacted by thyroid hormones, is UA.It was stated that elevated UA levels were linked to decreased renal plasma flow and GFR in individuals with hypothyroidism. [20]study done by Giordano et al. [21] revealed a 33.3% prevalence of hyperuricemia in hypothyroid individuals.Erickson et al. [22] and Dariyerli et al. [23] discovered hyperuricemia in patients with hypothyroidism in similar trials.Gout and hyperuricemia are very common with hypothyroidism.Hyperuricemia in hypothyroidism is due to reduced glomerular filtration and decreased renal plasma flow. [18]tients suffering from primary thyroid dysfunction have elevated serum UA levels as compared to patients with primary hyperthyroidism.In addition to raising the chance of developing additional concomitant disorders, these individuals' high serum UA levels may also have an impact on the course and outcome of their illness.

The study conducted by Mona Youssry Helmy, [24] in 2020, concluded that in subjects with primary thyroid dysfunction, creatinine and urea levels were higher in hypothyroid patients than in other studied groups with a significant difference.A significant negative correlation between UA and TSH was established in their study.This research indicates a thyroid function test might be needed for patients with hyperuricemia, Ischaemic Heart Disease, and CKD.

While a significant reduction in renal function in humans is not common, it is important to note that most problems that have been reported improve when thyroid hormone is administered.It is crucial for a clinician to understand the reversible relationship between hypothyroidism and high blood creatinine and UA.With this information, individuals who present with either elevated creatinine or gout and an unknown thyroid status could avoid needless examinations, expensive treatments, and anxiety.When evaluating individuals who present with impaired renal function, thyroid function should be regularly assessed.


Conclusion

The study concludes that serum urea showed a positive correlation with TSH, whereas UA and creatinine showed a negative correlation with TSH and there was no statistical significance in the mean values of urea, UA and creatinine in hypothyroid patients.


Further research

We believe that further studies with larger samples should be carried out to determine the role of urea, UA and creatinine in patients with primary hypothyroidism.
