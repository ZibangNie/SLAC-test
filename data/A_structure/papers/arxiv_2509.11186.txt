Simulations of Tidal Disruption of Supernova in Galaxy Nuclear Region: A Novel Model for Ambiguous Nuclear Transients
September 16, 2025706A5E3A42543C123E4D85BB9BC8CD03arXiv:2509.11186v1[astro-ph.HE]Accretion (14)Active galactic nuclei (16)Supernovae(1668)Tidal disruption (1696)Hydrodynamics (1963)
An increasing number of ambiguous nuclear transients, including some extreme nuclear transients, have been reported, which cannot be simply explained by the tidal disruption event (TDE) due to their high energy and/or overmassive black holes.Stars that form in or are captured by AGN disks will grow and migrate inward, potentially exploding as supernovae once the inner cold accretion disk disappears during the phase of AGN decay.We propose that tidal disruption of a supernova (TDS) by a supermassive black hole (SMBH) can produce nuclear transients that are even more luminous than typical TDEs and are not limited by the SMBH mass.In this scenario, the SMBH can capture the supernova ejecta, which subsequently self-intersects and circularizes into an accretion disk.Based on hydrodynamical simulations, we find that the accretion rate of the TDS disk exhibits a slow decline that can last for months to decades.The peak accretion rate of a typical core-collapse SN scenario can exceed the Eddington limit for SMBHs with M BH ≲ 10 7.5 M ⊙ , while it remains sub-Eddington for more massive SMBHs.This model provides a mechanism for triggering an energetic TDE-like flare with luminosity ≳ 10 45 erg s −1 in galaxies with M BH ≳ 10 8 M ⊙ or triggering turn-on changing-look AGNs.

INTRODUCTION

Accretion onto supermassive black holes (SMBHs) powers many of the most luminous events in the universe.Continuously accreting SMBHs are observed as active galactic nuclei (AGNs) that present in ∼1-5% of galaxies in the local universe (e.g., Ho 2008;Haggard et al. 2010).In the era of multi-wavelength transient surveys, an increasing number of flares originating from galactic nuclei have been detected (e.g., York et al. 2000;Chambers et al. 2016;Kochanek et al. 2017;Bellm et al. 2019;Yuan et al. 2022).The tidal disruption of stars by SMBHs, known as tidal disruption events (TDEs), has been extensively studied in recent years, with more than two hundred cases reported (e.g., Rees 1988;Komossa 2015;Hammerstein et al. 2023;Yao et al. 2023;Langis et al. 2025).Apart from TDEs, some ambiguous nuclear transients (ANTs) or extreme nuclear transients (ENTs) have been discovered (e.g., Neustadt et al. 2020;Hinkle et al. 2022;Wiseman et al. 2025;Hinkle et al. 2025), which appear to be related to SMBHs but display observational properties distinct from both TDEs and AGNs.For example, a subset of TDE-like flares occurs in galaxies that host SMBHs with M BH ≳ 10 8 M ⊙ and shows peak luminosities ≳ 10 45 erg s −1 (e.g., Leloudas et al. 2016;Hinkle et al. 2025).In these cases, the luminosities are significantly higher than typical TDEs, and the SMBHs are too massive to tidally disrupt a Sunlike star.Moreover, many extremely variable quasars (EVQs) and changing-look AGNs (CLAGNs) have been identified in optical and X-ray surveys, exhibiting variability distinct from the stochastic fluctuations typical of AGNs (e.g., MacLeod et al. 2016;Rumbaugh et al. 2018;Ricci & Trakhtenbrot 2023).The physical mechanisms that drive ANTs, ENTs, CLAGNs, and EVQs remain very unclear.

Galactic nuclei commonly host compact, massive nuclear star clusters (NSCs) that are resolved in many nearby galaxies (e.g., Schödel et al. 2007;Genzel et al. 2010).The NSCs are also closely correlated with extreme gravitational phenomena (e.g., extreme massratio inspirals, EMRIs) and tidal disruption events (see Neumayer et al. 2020, for a review).In AGNs, gravitational instability in the outer regions of the accretion disk can promote in-situ star formation, and some stars above or below the disk can also be captured.The rapid accretion and evolution of disk stars can lead to rapid metal enrichment as suggested by the high metallicities observed in the broad-line region (e.g., Paczynski 1978;Goodman & Tan 2004;Wang et al. 2021;Fan & Wu 2023).Such a dense disk environment further promotes stellar accretion and inward migration.These massive stars can reach the inner region within a few hundred gravitational radii (e.g., Bartko et al. 2010;Bellovary et al. 2016;Pan & Yang 2021;Cantiello et al. 2021).After the AGNs fade, the inner cold Shakura-Sunyaev disk (SSD) will disappear or transit to an advectiondominated accretion flow (ADAF) at tens to hundreds of gravitational radii when the accretion rate falls below a critical value (e.g., ∼ 1% Eddington accretion rate) (e.g., Ho 2008;Yuan & Narayan 2014).In this case, the former disk stars will stop accretion and evolve quickly, and their lifetimes are short due to high mass.In this scenario, these stars may explode as supernovae in the fading phase of AGNs.The environment near an SMBH may cause SN ejecta to evolve differently from an isolated explosion (e.g., Burrows & Vartanyan 2021;Li et al. 2023;Fabj et al. 2025).

If a star explodes sufficiently close to the SMBH, the SMBH can capture a large fraction of the SN ejecta and tidally stretch it into an elongated stream.We refer to it as the tidal disruption of a supernova (TDS).The resulting streams circularize and form an accretion disk that can power very luminous nuclear flares, potentially explaining some energetic ANTs or ENTs (e.g., Leloudas et al. 2016;Holoien et al. 2022;Hinkle et al. 2025).To explore this process, we perform three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of TDSs.Section 2 describes the numerical methods, Section 3 presents the results, and Section 4 summarizes our conclusions and discusses the observational features and possible counterparts.


NUMERICAL METHODS

SN explosions are generally isotropic, but the tidal field of a nearby SMBH can strongly break this symmetry and alter the ejecta evolution.To investigate these effects, we perform three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of SN ejecta launched from the midplane of a disk around an SMBH.As a fiducial case (Model-0 ), we consider a faded AGN hosting a central SMBH with M BH = 10 8 M ⊙ and an Eddington ratio f Edd = Ṁ / ṀEdd = 10 −3 .In this scenario, the inner region of the disk is expected to stay in ADAF state.We therefore adopt the ADAF gas distribution as the SN environment, using the self-similar ADAF solution with a dimensionless shear viscosity parameter α = 0.2 (Narayan & Yi 1994;Nelson et al. 2013).For the SN ejecta, we adopt an isotropic exponential density profile with a linear velocity structure, commonly used in analytical and numerical models (e.g., Nomoto et al. 1984;Matzner & McKee 1999;Grishin et al. 2021).In our simulations, we set the ejecta mass M ej = 10 M ⊙ and energy E ej = 2×10 51 erg, exploding at R ej = 100 R g , representing typical values of a core-collapse SN and a location near the disk transition radius (e.g., Ho 2008;Yuan & Narayan 2014;Burrows & Vartanyan 2021).To explore the impact of supernova ejecta parameters, we further simulate two different cases, Model-1 and Model-2.The key fiducial parameters are summarized in Table 1, and detailed equations and complete initial profiles are provided in Appendix A.

Hydrodynamics Process

Figure 2a-2c illustrate the self-intersection and circularization stages of the TDS.Initially, the spherical SN ejecta expand beyond the tidal limits, where the tidal forces from the SMBH stretch the ejecta into a diffuse, elongated stream.The low-eccentricity TDS stream undergoes strong self-intersection, which efficiently dissipates angular momentum and drives inward migra-tion.To track the circularization process, Figure 2 shows the photon trapping radius, R trap , which is defined as the radius where the photon diffusion timescale τ diff ≃ τ o R/c equals the electron dynamical timescale τ dyn ≃ R/v where τ o is the optical depth and v is the gas velocity.This condition can be expressed as ∞ Rtrap ρ(R) κ s dR ≃ c/v trap , where κ s is electron scattering opacity and v trap is velocity at R trap (Rees 1978).The temporal evolution of the R trap in mid-plane is shown in the bottom panel of Figure 3.After ∼ 80 days, the irregular TDS stream forms precessing ringlike structures within ∼ 150R g (see Figure 2b).By t ∼ 256 days, the stream settles into a stable circular structure (see Figure 2c), indicating the completion of circularization.

Figures 2c-2d show the TDS disk structure and its subsequent expansion, which is also reflected in the growing trapping region.After t ∼ 300 days, the TDS stream forms a stable disk with an extended trapping region of ∼ 200R g .Most of the material concentrates near the mid-plane, accompanied by outflows at large polar angles.At a later stage, the disk expands beyond the simulation domain, so our measured trapping region may underestimate its true size.Nevertheless, the overall trend of continuous outward expansion is evident.

The accretion-rate evolution of the TDS depends critically on the initial ejecta velocity v ej and launch site R ej .For comparison, we present two additional cases in Figure 4 with higher SN energy E ej = 10 52 erg launched at R ej = 100 R g (Model-1 ) and R ej = 300 R g (Model-2 ).In Model-1, the accretion rate exhibits a slow power-law decline, Ṁ ∝ (t − τ fall ) −0.55 , after reaching a peak of Ṁ ∼ 0.7 M ⊙ yr −1 at t ∼ 132 days.The bottom panels show that circularization completes at τ cir ∼ 149 days.In this case, τ rise < τ cir , indicating that angular momentum redistribution in the accretion flow is relatively inefficient.In Model-2, the accretion rate reaches its maximum of Ṁ ∼ 0.8 M ⊙ yr −1 at τ rise ∼ 29 days.Most of the outer ejecta material is accreted within ∼ 400 days before full circularization, where τ rise ≪ τ cir and the accretion rate declines more steeply as Ṁ ∝ (t − τ fall ) −1.1 .


SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION

We carry out three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of the tidal distortion of a supernova that explodes close to an SMBH.In our simulations, a fraction of the ejecta is captured by the gravitational potential, undergoes strong self-intersection, and eventually forms an accretion disk.The fiducial simulation yields a steady accretion rate Ṁ ∼ 0.4 M ⊙ yr −1 , which corresponds to a luminosity of L TDS = η Ṁ c 2 ∼ 2 × 10 45 erg s −1 with radiative efficiency η ∼ 0.1.Two generic accretion behaviors are identified: (1) a quasi-steady plateau phase when circularization is efficient and a new disk is established, and (2) a power-law decline when fallback and dissipation proceed more gradually.These results demonstrate that TDS can produce TDE-like accretion episodes and peak energetics comparable to those of observed luminous nuclear transients.We note that our present calculations omit radiation transport, magnetic fields, and full general-relativistic effects; therefore, quantitative predictions for light curves and spectra require radiation-GR(M)HD follow-up studies.

In Section 3, we show that the accretion properties of the TDS are primarily governed by the circularization timescale τ cir and the rise timescale τ rise , which determine whether the accretion rate evolves into a plateaulike phase resembling a stable disk or follows a powerlaw decline similar to the initial fallback rate.In our fiducial simulation (Model-0 ), the timescale to reach the peak accretion rate is τ rise ∼ 5.8 R ej /v ej , a scaling that also holds for Model-1 where SN velocities are much smaller than the escape velocity.In Model-2, the rise timescale approaches the free-fall timescale, indicating that the outer SN ejecta undergoes nearly radial infall with minimal self-intersection, due to an ejection velocity approaching the escape velocity.The circularization timescale can be expressed as τ cir ∼ f cir τ fall like that in TDEs (Bonnerot et al. 2017;Chen & Shen 2021), where the factor f cir measures the circularization efficiency.Based on our simulations, we find f cir ∼ 9.4, 7.0 and 6.2 for Model-0,-1,-2 respectively, where the stronger self-intersection enhances angular momentum dissipation with increasing values of v ej /v K .Higher explosion velocities at a larger launch site result in a steeper power-law evolution of the accretion rate (τ rise ≪ τ cir ), due to incomplete redistribution of energy and angular momentum during self-intersection.We also note that the initial ejecta size can slightly affect the accretionrate evolution, which requires higher-resolution simulations for further investigation.

Although our simulations are conducted for a black hole mass of M BH = 10 8 M ⊙ , the characteristic timescales scale as GM BH /c 3 .Consequently, TDS disks around smaller black holes (M BH ≲ 10 7.5 M ⊙ ) can sustain super-Eddington accretion rates over correspondingly shorter durations.Moreover, the accretion rate also scales with the ejecta mass, Ṁ ∝ M ej .Very massive supernovae, such as pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) with M ej ∼ 100 M ⊙ (Fryer et al. 2001;Gal-Yam et al. 2009), can therefore produce long-lived, super-Eddington disks.In this super-Eddington regime, a photosphere may form around the TDS disk, powered by radiation-pressure-driven winds or self-intersectioninduced outflows, analogous to TDEs (e.g., Dai et al. 2018;Curd 2021;Qiao et al. 2025).Consequently, TDSs are expected to exhibit multiwavelength signatures similar to TDEs, but they are predicted to be more luminous, exhibit smoother light curves, and persist for longer durations because of the larger progenitor mass and more efficient angular momentum redistribution.Unlike TDEs, the TDSs can naturally occur in galaxies hosting SMBHs of any mass and can produce extreme flares even for M BH ≳ 10 8 M ⊙ .Spectroscopically, the heavy-element-enriched SN ejecta should imprint strong metallic features in TDS, which may lead to some strong metal lines (e.g., O, Si, Fe) that can be tested in future spectroscopic observations.Several extreme TDE-like flares around overmassive SMBHs have already been reported.ASASSN-15lh is a luminous event with L bol,peak ∼ 2.2×10 45 erg s −1 occurring in a galaxy center (Dong et al. 2016;Godoy-Rivera et al. 2017), initially classified as a Type I SLSN (Dong et al. 2016) and later re-interpreted as a TDE based on its optical and infrared light curve evolution (Leloudas et al. 2016;Krühler et al. 2018).However, the inferred central SMBH mass (M BH ∼ 5×10 8 M ⊙ ) is too large for a standard TDE (Leloudas et al. 2016).Spectroscopic observations of the host galaxy indicate a weak AGN signature (Krühler et al. 2018).These properties make ASASSN-15lh a plausible TDS candidate, as this scenario can naturally sustain a high luminosity for years around such a massive SMBH.Similarly, a newly reported class of ENTs has been interpreted as the tidal disruption of high-mass (∼ 3 − 10 M ⊙ ) stars by overmassive SMBHs (M BH ≳ 10 8 M ⊙ ) (Hinkle et al. 2025), exhibiting smooth, luminous (2 − 7 × 10 45 erg s −1 ) and long-lived (> 150 days) flares.The TDS scenario also provides a natural explanation for these events.

The evolution of a TDS begins after the supernova explosion, so the resulting flare is normally delayed relative to the SN.In our fiducial simulations, the TDS disk reaches a peak luminosity of L TDS ∼ 2 × 10 45 erg s −1 , comparable to that of superluminous supernovae (SLSNe; e.g., Ginzburg & Balberg 2012;Sukhbold & Woosley 2016).If an SLSN were to be tidally disrupted, its light curve might initially display an SLSN phase for several months, followed by the emergence of a TDS component.A notable example is ASASSN-17jz, which was reported as a Type IIn SN embedded in an AGN disk (Holoien et al. 2022).This event is thought to have triggered a disk state transition, reaching a peak bolometric luminosity of L bol,peak ∼ 8.3 × 10 44 erg s −1 .TDSs could also explain the slowly declining light curves observed over several years after the early SLSN peak.Furthermore, although the physical mechanism driving CLAGNs remains unclear, TDSs can supply the material needed to trigger the turn-on phase of these AGNs.
