4Patterns of biological diversity across the tree of life are the result of millions of years of 5 evolutionary history and are shaped by natural selection. A long-standing proposal is 6 that most morphological diversity at a macroevolutionary scale (among species) arises 7 along a microevolutionary "line of least resistance". However, we frequently observe 8 major shifts in phenotypes among lineages [1, 2, 3], suggesting that species or clades 9 may also evolve away from the line of least resistance [4]. Over 32 longer timescales, the evolutionary line of least resistance is analogous to the major axis 33 of phenotypic variation [8,9]. Numerous studies show that phenotypic divergence is bi-34 ased along the evolutionary line of least resistance, and therefore reflects the major axis 35 of phenotypic variation, over 1-2 million year timescales [8, 10,11]. Recent evidence sug-36 gests that this stability can even extend over macroevolutionary timescales spanning tens 37 of millions of years [10]. However, while the evolutionary line of genetic least resistance 38 explicitly reflects genetic constraints, the macroevolutionary major axis of phenotypic 39 variation is an emergent property of genomic and developmental constraints interacting 40 with selection on a moving adaptive landscape [3,12]. Specifically, 50 Endler et al. refer to evolution of new phenotypes along the same major axis of phe-51 notypic variation as elaboration. 681Modelling nested trait covariances 69 Addressing the relative contributions of elaboration and innovation to the origins of 70 biodiversity in deep-time requires 1) large, multivariate datasets to allow exploration 71 of trait covariances at different scales, 2) reliable and efficient computational methods 72 to estimate the major axes of phenotypic variation, and 3) a set of mathematical tools 73 that can estimate degrees of elaboration and innovation at any scale. To meet the first 74 challenge we use an eight-dimensional beak shape morphospace based on a geometric, 75 morphometric dataset of 8748 species of birds (described in [17]). To meet the other 76 two challenges we introduce a novel analytical pipeline for measuring elaboration and 77 innovation at the species-level and clade-level (Fig.SS1 and SS2). To estimate the major 78 axis of phenotypic variation we fit Bayesian phylogenetic generalised linear mixed model 79 (pGLMM) models with beak shape as an eight-dimensional response variable ([18]).

We 82 can estimate levels of elaboration and innovation based on that major axis of phenotypic 83 variation (see Methods). We regard the major axis of phenotypic variation as a deep-84 time analogue of the microevolutionary G matrix [19] and posit that it represents the 85 line of least evolutionary resistance, capturing the effects of historical contingency on 86 multivariate evolution. The major axis of phenotypic variation can be defined at any 87 phylogenetic scale (i.e. for all species in the phylogeny, or for all species within any 88 clade). To assess the roles of elaboration and innovation across scales we fit our pGLMMs 89 as nested models in which we define variance-covariance matrices, and therefore major 90 axes of phenotypic variation, for i) the entire phylogeny of all species included in the 91 data (hereafter the global phylogenetic level), ii) all species within each of nine clades 92 mapping approximately to super-orders (the super-order level), and iii) all species within 93 each of 27 clades mapping to taxonomic orders (the order level). These nested partitions 94 of multivariate trait space provide the basis for all subsequent quantitative estimates of 95 elaboration and innovation at multiple scales.

We for-263 malise this paradox by testing whether elaboration is more common at higher taxonomic 264 levels than at the species level. We compared the area under the curve of the scaled den-265 sity of elaboration and innovation at both the species level and the clade level. We then 266 measured the difference between both areas and found that there was a significant dif-267 ference in innovation among species and clades but no clear difference in elaboration 268(Fig. 4). This difference confirms our observation that while innovation is common and 269 perhaps even dominant at the clade level, elaboration dominates at the species level. So, 270 although species evolve preferentially along a shared major axis of phenotypic variance, 271 this major axis changes frequently among clades. However, we suggest that rather than272 a paradox the reorientation of trait space can instead arise as a result of small innova-273 tions that arise in a common direction among closely-related species. This idea is similar 274 in concept to multiple adaptive peak models where species evolve in a heterogeneous 275 adaptive landscape and share similar responses to selection pressures within adaptive 276 zones [20]. This implies that gradual directional evolution [2], rather than exceptional 277 megaevolutionary jumps[1, 14], may be sufficient to explain diversity in avian beak .

Conclusions 293 Our results show that although at the species-level most bird beaks are elaborating along 294 a megaevolutionary major axis of phenotypic variation, at the macroevolutionary level 295 innovation away from the global phylogenetic major axis of phenotypic variation is much 296 more common. This nested structure of elaboration at a lower (species) taxonomic level 297 and innovation at a higher (clade) taxonomic levels could thus explain the diversity of 298 bird beaks we observe today. However, individual species-level variation in the past 299 could also have led to a shift in a clade's line of least evolutionary resistance through 300 different evolutionary routes on a dynamic adaptive landscape. Taken together, our re-301 sults suggest that the signature of evolutionary reorientations in deep-time (innovation), 302 coupled with elaboration at the species-level, is a robust explanation of the massive di-303 versity of bird beaks we observe today and is consistent with recent suggestions that 304 apparently abrupt phenotypic shifts observed in univariate analyses can be explained 305 by gradual Darwinian processes [2, 3, 16]. Indeed, the global phylogenetic major axis 306 of phenotypic variation is an emergent property of reorientation of trait space among 307 clades that requires no special evolutionary process: megaevolutionary patterns appear 308 to be an inevitable outcome of evolution on a shifting adaptive landscape. shape data. Because of the very large size of our model and 348 dataset, however, running the full model on multiple trees was computationally unfeasi-349 ble (it would require 2 CPU years and 4TB of RAM for each of the models, i.e. the global 350 phylogeny and the Passeriformes models). Instead, we developed and implemented a 351 "mini chains" MCMCglmm method using the R package mcmcmglmmm [26]. 352 mcmcmcglmmm. This method runs relatively short Monte-Carlo Markov chains 353 (MCMC) across a large number of different phylogenetic trees and concatenates the 354 resulting short posteriors into one larger posterior distribution that contains the phylo-355 genetic uncertainty information. We performed the method using the following protocol 356 (see Fig. SS2). First we ran three independent MCMCglmm chains (hereafter the param-357 eterisation chains) for 50k generations with the trait data and the model described above 358 on the consensus tree from the tree distribution, along with flat priors with a belief pa-359 rameter of 2%, i.e. with a very low weight on the priors; Next we extracted the following 360 parameters from the parameterisation chains: a) the conservative burnin average time 361 across the three chains, defined as the highest number of iterations required to first reach 362 the median posterior likelihood value multiplied by 1.1; and b) the mini-chains priors, 363 defined as the median posterior model values from the parameterisation chains with 364 a belief parameter of 5%. We then ran 400 mini chains in parallel with the estimated 365 burnin time and priors to run 10 samples past the burnin. This resulted in 10 exploitable 366 posterior samples for each tree. We used 400 trees because that was the number of mod-367 els required to reach an effective sample size (ESS) of at least 200 for all the estimated 368 parameters (see Fig. SS4 and SS5). Using this approach, we reduced the computational 369 time to around 45 CPU hours and 9GB of RAM per mini-chain -a 400 fold improve-370 ment. The total analysis took around three CPU years using the shARC cluster from the 371 University of Sheffield.
