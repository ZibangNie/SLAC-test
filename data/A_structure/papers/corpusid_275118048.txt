A drug-drug co-amorphous system for highly improved solubility of breviscapine: an experimental and computational study

Drug-drug co-amorphous systems are a promising approach to improve the aqueous solubility of poorly water-soluble drugs.This study explores the combination of breviscapine (BRE) and matrine (MAT) form an amorphous salt, aiming to synergistically enhance the solubility and dissolution of BRE.In silico analysis of electrostatic potential and local ionization energy were conducted on BRE-MAT complex to predict the intermolecular interactions, and solvent-free energies were calculated using thermodynamic integration and density functional theory.The co-amorphous mixture, prepared by solvent evaporation, was characterized using various analytical techniques, including polarized microscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, and powder X-ray diffraction, confirming its amorphous nature.Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and molecular dynamic simulations revealed strong hydrogen bonding, with a proton transfer from the carboxyl group of BRE to the tertiary amine nitrogen of MAT.The resulting co-amorphous salt demonstrated substantial solubility improvement (> 8000-fold in water) and enhanced in vitro dissolution of BRE.The study also confirmed that the coamorphous salt maintained physical stability at 40 °C and 75% relative humidity over 6 months.These findings provide a viable strategy for developing drug-drug co-amorphous formulations to enhance solubility and stability, with significant potential for pharmaceutical applications.

Given these considerations, we hypothesized that a BRE-MAT co-amorphous system could synergistically enhance BRE's solubility and dissolution.This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of the BRE-MAT coamorphous system, including an analysis of intermolecular interactions through molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and solvation free energy calculations.The co-amorphous mixture was prepared and characterized, and its solubility, dissolution, and physical stability were extensively investigated to assess its potential as a stable, high-solubility formulation.


Materials and methods


Materials

Breviscapine and matrine, with a purity of > 97%, were supplied by Jingzhu Biotech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China).High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade methanol and other analytical-grade chemicals were form Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.All chemicals were used without further purification, and ultrapure water was used for all solutions (Arium ® Comfort II, Sartorius, Germany).


Sample preparation

The co-amorphous mixture of BRE and MAT was prepared at molar ratio of 1:1 using the solvent evaporation method 19 .Briefly, 100 mg of BRE and MAT were accurately weighed and fully dissolved in anhydrous methanol.After sonication at room temperature for 5 min, the solvent was removed by a rotary vacuum evaporator (RE212B, Yamato, Japan) at 45 °C.The resulting mixture was further dried under vacuum at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete solvent removal.

For comparison, pure BRE and MAT were recrystallized following the same solvent evaporation procedure.Additionally, physical mixtures of BRE and MAT were prepared by manually blending the powders in a mortar and pestle for 2 min to achieve a uniform distribution.All samples were sieved through an 80-mesh screen and stored in a desiccator with anhydrous silica gel to prevent moisture absorption until further analysis.


Morphology and microstructure observation

The morphology of pure or recrystallized BRE and MAT, as well as their physical or co-amorphous mixtures, was observed using optical microscope (OM, BM1000, Jiangnan Photoelectric Group, Nanjing, China) and polarized light microscopy (PLM, MXP-260, Precise Instrument, Beijing, China).

Further microstructural examination was conducted using a scanning electronic microscope (SEM, Phenom Pro, Phenom World, Netherlands).Prior to imaging, samples were metallized and analyzed at the acceleration voltage of 10 kV in the backscattered electron mode to enhance image contrast.


Solid state characterization


Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Thermal analysis of the samples was performed using a DSC apparatus (Setsys Evo 16, Setaram, France).Approximately 5 mg sample was sealed in aluminum pans (L8*Φ5mm), and heated from 30 to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 20 mL/min.Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) PXRD patterns were obtained using a diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu-Kα radiation.Scanning range was set from 5° to 40° in 2θ, with a rate of 8°/min and a step size of 0.02°.During the measurements, a constant dry nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min was maintained to prevent sample degradation.


Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra were recorded on a spectrophotometer (Great20, Ruijie Technology, Tianjin, China).Samples were mixed with KBr and compressed into tablets, then scanned over a range of 4000 to 400 cm −1 at a resolution of 2 cm −1 .


Reaction mechanism simulation

The 3D structure of BRE and MAT were retrieved from ChemSpider database and optimized at the B97-3c 20 level using ORCA software.To identify the most reactive sites, quantitative surface analyses were performed using the Multiwfn 21 program to calculate electrostatic potential (ESP) and average local ionization energy (ALIE).

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were conducted with ORCA 5.0.3 22 and visualized using VMD 23 , employing the SMD solvent model with methanol as the primary solvent.A time step of 1 fs was used, and temperature control was achieved through a CSVR (Canonical Sampling through Velocity Rescaling) thermostat 24 , with a time constant of 20 fs.The B97-3c method was selected for calculations due to its balance of accuracy and computational efficiency.After simulation, wavefunction files were analyzed using Multiwfn.


Equilibrium solubility determination of BRE

The equilibrium solubility of BRE in various solvents was determined using the shake-flask method.Excess sample was added to a vial containing 5 mL of solvent, and the mixture was continuously shaken at 37 °C for 72 h in an oscillating gas bath to ensure saturation.After equilibration, the suspensions were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 5 min.The supernatant was then diluted to an appropriate concentration and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter for HPLC analysis.

HPLC analysis was conducted on a S6000 HPLC system (Chrome, China) equipped with an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).The detection wavelength was set at 335 nm, and the column temperature was maintained at 25 °C.The mobile consisted of methanol and 0.05% formic acid solution (65:35, v/v), with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.


Free energy calculation

According to Eq. ( 1) 25 , dissolution can be divided into two distinct processes: solid sublimation and molecular solvation.The sublimation free energy of a given solute remains consistent across different solvents; therefore, the solubility in various solvents is primarily influenced by the solvation process.Consequently, the solubility of SCU and its complex in different solvents can be evaluated in terms of solvation free energy, where higher solubility corresponds to a more negative solvation free energy.
∆G dissolution = ∆G sublimation + ∆G solvation(1)
The solvation free energy was calculated using both quantum mechanical and thermodynamic integration methods, allowing for comparison with the experimental solubility results.


Thermodynamic integration method

The thermodynamic Integration (TI) method was employed to calculate the free energy differences between SCU and its anionic form in explicit solvent environments 26 .Both SCU and solvent molecules were optimized at a B3LYP-D3/def-TZVP level [27][28][29] , with single-point energy calculations conducted at the B3LYP/def2-TZVP level.RESP2 charges 30 were fitted using Multiwfn program, and the necessary parameter files were generated using the sobtop program 31 under the general Amber force field (GAFF).

In the simulation setup, a total of twelve systems were prepared in GROMACS (version 2018.6) 32, each involving the target molecules and solvent molecules in a 1.2 nm simulation box.The molecule number for solvents were 1600 for water, 900 for methanol, 500 for ethanol, 400 for acetone, 700 for acetonitrile, and 300 for ethyl acetate.For anionic systems, a sodium ion was added to neutralize the charge.Energy minimization was carried out using the conjugate gradient and steepest descent methods.This was followed by a 500-ps equilibration at 298.15 K and 1 bar, employing the Parrinello-Rahman barostat and Nose-Hoover thermostat.Electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a cutoff of 1 nm, a time step of 1 fs, and periodic boundary conditions applied.A 5-ns production phase simulation was then conducted, with all free energy analyses performed using alchemical analysis tools 33 .


Density functional theory (DFT) method

In the DFT approach, solvation free energy (∆Gsolvation) was calculated as the difference between the Gibbs free energy in solution (∆Esolv) and the gas phase (∆Egas).Given the structural diversity of SCU in different solvent environments, each structure was optimized at the B3LYP-D3/def-TZVP level using the SMD solvation model.Single-point energy calculations for both vacuum and solvent models were conducted at the M052X/6-31G* level 34 .Finally, the solvation free energy was derived using the following Eq.( 2):
∆G solvation = ∆E solv − ∆Egas + ∆G thermal (2)

In vitro dissolution test

Drug dissolution profiles of BRE from different formulations were assessed using the paddle method, following the Chinese Pharmacopoeia guidelines.Dissolution tests were conducted in a dissolution apparatus (RCZ-8 M, Tiandatianfa Technology, Tianjin, China) with 900 mL of purified water as the dissolution medium, maintained at 37 °C ± 0.5 °C.The paddle speed was set at 50 rpm to ensure consistent mixing.BRE-containing samples (equivalent to 15 mg of BRE) were carefully introduced into the dissolution medium, and the system was continuously stirred.At predetermined time points, 3 mL of the medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium to maintain a constant volume.The collected samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and BRE content was analyzed using the previously described HPLC method.


Powder hygroscopicity

Powder samples were placed in a constant-temperature chamber maintained at 25 ± 1 °C and 80 ± 1% relative humidity (RH) for 24 h.The initial and final weights of the samples were recorded to evaluate any mass changes, indicating moisture absorbing.


Storage stability test

Co-amorphous samples were stored in airtight, light-protected vials at 40 °C and 75% RH for 6 months.During the storage period, the samples were monitored for changes in appearance, potential recrystallization (assessed via PXRD), and dissolution behavior (using the previously described dissolution protocol).


Results and discussion


Morphology and microstructure observation

The morphology and microstructure of pure BRE and MAT, along with their recrystallized, physical and coamorphous mixtures, were examined by various microscopical techniques to assess their physical state and particle characteristics.

As shown in Fig. 2, bulk BRE appeared as a yellowish powder, while MAT was an off-white crystalline powder.Upon recrystallization, BRE exhibited a more needle-like clustered structure, whereas MAT formed irregular granules with reduced size.The physical mixture showed as light yellow granules after grinding, while the co-amorphous mixture of BRE and MAT displayed as dark yellow blocks, indicating distinct morphological characteristics from the crystalline forms.

Under PLM, MAT samples (both raw and recrystallized) showed significant birefringence, confirming their crystalline nature.Weak birefringence was observed in the BRE powders and physical mixtures, while the coamorphous mixture exhibited no birefringence, suggesting a loss of crystallinity due to co-amorphization.

SEM analysis further confirmed these observations.The raw BRE showed irregular blocks with a fascicular texture, transforming into long rod-shaped particles upon recrystallization.Raw MAT appeared as large rodlike crystals, and the recrystallized MAT as smaller, irregular particles.The co-amorphous mixture, however, showed dense, smooth-surfaced particles with a more compact morphology, consistent with a co-amorphous state.These distinct morphological differences indicate successful co-amorphization and the formation of a stable, amorphous structure in the BRE-MAT mixture.


Solid state characterization

As shown in Fig. 3a, BRE exhibited endothermic peaks at 139.3 °C and 197.4 °C corresponding to its melting and degradation points, respectively, along with an exothermic peak at 212.8 °C, which is in agreement with previous reports 12 .The endothermic peaks were slightly shifted to lower temperatures upon recrystallization, indicating minor structural changes.In contrast, MAT displayed an endothermic peak at 85.9 °C, attributed to its melting, which is shifted to 75.9 °C after recrystallization 35 .However, no distinct peaks were observed for their physical or co-amorphous mixtures, indicating the occurrence of specific interactions during the co-processing stages.

PXRD patterns in Fig. 3b provided further confirmation of the amorphous state.Raw BRE displayed characteristic sharp peaks at 2θ angles of 8.0°, 9.4°, 10.1°, 14.3°, 16.0°, 19.1°, 25.9°, 26.8°, and 28.9°, indicative of its crystalline nature.After recrystallization, some new peaks at 11.3°, 12.4° and 18.4°appeared, while other peaks at 14.3° and 16.0° disappeared, showing slight structural modifications.Similarly, MAT showed prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ of 7.0°, 11.5°, 14.0°, 16.7°, 23.0°, confirming its crystalline form, which persisted in the physical mixture, albeit with reduced intensity.The co-amorphous mixture, however, exhibited a complete absence of sharp peaks, instead showing a broad halo, which is characteristic of amorphous materials, confirming successful co-amorphization of BRE and MAT.

FTIR (Fig. 3c) were recorded to investigate the potential interactions between BRE and MAT in the coamorphous form.Pure BRE showed characteristic absorption bands at 3392 cm −1 (-OH stretching), 1611 cm −1 , 1573 and 1488 cm −1 (C = O stretching), while MAT exhibited peaks at 1606 cm −1 (amide C = O), 2932 cm −1 , 1472 cm −1 (C-H bonds), and 1341 cm −1 (C-N stretching).These peaks were retained with slight intensity reductions in the recrystallized drugs and their physical mixture.However, in the co-amorphous sample, the disappearance of the peaks at 1573 cm −1 and 1488 cm −1 , along with a broadening in the 1500 -1350 cm −1 region, suggested the formation of new intermolecular interactions, specifically hydrogen bonding between the carboxyl group of BRE and the tertiary amine of MAT.


Reaction mechanism

ESP analysis, a common tool in evaluating chemical reactivity, highlights electron-rich regions that favor electrophilic interactions 36,37 .As shown in Fig. 4a, the ESP map indicated that the carboxyl group of BRE is attracted to electron-rich sites on the MAT molecules, particularly around the tertiary amine nitrogen.ALIE mapping, which reflects local ionization energies, identified low-energy sites on MAT's nitrogen atom (Fig. 4b), suggesting its high reactivity 38 and potential to form hydrogen bonds with BRE's carboxyl group.These complementary ESP and ALIE results pinpointed specific sites likely involved in intermolecular bonding, thus supporting the stability of the co-amorphous complex.

MD simulations were performed to observe the dynamic interaction between BRE and MAT. Figure 5 illustrated the complexation process, showing a hydrogen atom transfer from the carboxyl group of BRE to the tertiary amine nitrogen on MAT.Initially, the nitrogen-hydrogen distance decreased from 3.5 Å to 1.0 Å within the first 400 femtoseconds, indicating the formation of a stable N-H bond (Fig. 5a).Concurrently, the oxygenhydrogen distance increased from 1.0 Å to over 4.0 Å (Fig. 5b), confirming the cleavage of the O-H bond in BRE's carboxyl group.To further validate the hydrogen transfer, Mayer bond order analysis was conducted 39 , which quantifies bonding interactions 40 .The bond order between nitrogen and hydrogen in MAT increased from 0 to approximately 0.8, while the bond order between oxygen and hydrogen in BRE decreased from 0.7 to near zero (Fig. 5c), confirming the formation of a new N-H bond and the disruption of the original O-H bond.Charge analysis using atomic dipole corrected Hirshfeld (ADCH) methods (Fig. 5d) showed a shift in electron density: the nitrogen atom's charge shifted toward neutrality, while the hydrogen atom's charge decreased to approximately 0.25, supporting the formation of ionic species within the co-amorphous system.The total charge of BRE was calculated as 0.0069 and − 0.9542 before and after the molecular reaction, respectively.

These analyses collectively suggest that the observed hydrogen transfer stabilizes the co-amorphous structure by forming a salt-like complex with ionic character, which is likely a key factor in enhancing the solubility and dissolution properties of BRE in the co-amorphous BRE-MAT system.


Saturated solubility of BRE

As shown in Fig. 6, the amorphous form of BRE demonstrated significantly higher solubility across all tested organic solvents compared to its crystalline counterpart, except in acetonitrile.The solubility of BRE followed a descending order: methanol > ethanol > acetone > ethyl acetate, which aligns with the polarity and solvation potential of these solvents.

In aqueous media, the co-amorphous BRE-MAT system exhibited an extraordinary increase in solubility.Due to the formation of a highly viscous fluid upon adding large amounts of powder, precise quantification in water was challenging.However, estimates indicate that the solubility of co-amorphous BRE in water exceeds 0.52 g/mL, representing an approximately 8000-fold increase compared to crystalline BRE (0.0642 ± 0.0038 mg/ mL).Similarly, MAT's solubility in water increased approximately 4.2-fold compared to the crystalline MAT (66.72 ± 2.23 mg/mL), reaching over 0.28 g/mL in the co-amorphous system.

The presence of MAT, a water-soluble co-former, in the co-amorphous mixture contributed to the dissolution enhancement of BRE.The significant solubility improvement can be largely attributed to the formation of a salt- like complex with ionic properties, which enhances BRE's electrostatic interactions with water molecules and promotes dissolution.

The solubility of the BRE-MAT co-amorphous system in water, far surpassing typical crystalline forms, indicates the effectiveness of the co-amorphous salt strategy in overcoming solubility limitations.This finding aligns with recent studies on co-amorphous systems, where ionic interactions and amorphous states lead to remarkable solubility improvements.For example, the authors reported a 103-fold improvement on water solubility of tranilast for its co-amorphous system with MAT 35 and observed a hydrogel-like state for the solution of the co-amorphous system at high content.

However, in certain cases, some co-amorphous drugs have demonstrated lower solubility compared to their crystalline forms.For instance, the aqueous solubility of both the drug and co-formers in the co-amorphous systems of sinomenine with either platensimycin or sulfasalazine was found to be lower than that of their respective crystalline forms 41 .Similarly, in a co-amorphous system of resveratrol and MAT 42 , the solubility of MAT in pH 6.8 PBS decreased from 55 mg/mL in its crystalline form to 6.3 mg/mL in the amorphous state.These contrasting solubility profiles suggest that variations in intermolecular interactions among the components are critical in influencing solubility outcomes.


Thermodynamic description

Figure 7 presents the solvation free energies for SCU and its anion across different solvents, derived from both TI and DFT calculations.In general, the TI and DFT results exhibited similar trends, although discrepancies were observed for SCU in ethyl acetate and acetonitrile, likely due to limitations in the computational models.The overall trend indicates that SCU and its anionic form achieve higher solvation free energies in polar solvents, aligning with the experimentally observed solubility.

Table 1 summarizes the contributions of van der Waals and Coulombic (electrostatic) interactions to the solvation free energy of SCU in various solvents.In water, the solvation free energy was primarily influenced by Coulombic interactions, as SCU's hydroxyl groups engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules.In contrast, nonpolar interactions dominated in less polar solvents like acetone and ethyl acetate, correlating with the lower solubility of SCU in these solvents.

Upon transitioning to its anionic form, SCU exhibited enhanced electrostatic interactions with polar solvents, particularly water, leading to higher solubility in these media.The anionic form benefits from stronger electrostatic interactions with polar solvents, reducing the barrier to dissolution.This interaction is further facilitated in the co-amorphous system, where the amorphous state of BRE-MAT minimizes lattice energy, thus requiring less energy for dissolution.

The thermodynamic analyses underscore that the co-amorphous BRE-MAT system's high solubility in water is primarily due to the balance between enhanced electrostatic interactions and the reduction in lattice energy associated with the amorphous form.These findings align with the solubility data and support the hypothesis that the BRE-MAT co-amorphous system leverages both ionic interactions and the amorphous state to achieve substantial solubility enhancement.


In vitro dissolution test

The dissolution profiles of BRE from various formulations were evaluated to determine the effect of coamorphous formulation on drug release.

As shown in Fig. 8, BRE dissolution was extremely low in pure water and pH 5.0 PBS, with maximum release percentages of only 2% and 8%, respectively.However, drug release was significantly accelerated in PBS at pH 6.8 and 7.4, rapidly reaching an equilibrium of over 60%.This variation in dissolution behavior across different media may be attributed to BRE's much lower solubility in acidic environments compared to neutral or basic conditions 43,44 .

The co-amorphous powder formulations rapidly released over 65% of the loaded BRE within the first minute across all tested media.In contrast, the physical mixture showed a notable increase in initial BRE release, from 5% at pH 5.0 to 60% at pH 7.4, with final release percentages at 30 min reaching 19%, 69%, and 72% in media at pH 5.0, 6.8, and 7.4, respectively.These results suggest that the presence of MAT in the formulations enhances BRE dissolution through intermolecular interactions in aqueous solutions, while the co-amorphous state of BRE in the formulations further improves overall drug dissolution.

The substantial improvement in dissolution from the co-amorphous system highlights the effectiveness of using MAT as a co-former to facilitate the rapid release of BRE.This enhanced dissolution behavior aligns with the thermodynamic and solubility findings, confirming that the co-amorphous BRE-MAT formulation not only improves solubility but also promotes faster and more complete drug release in aqueous media, addressing the limitations of crystalline BRE.


Hygroscopicity and physical stability

The hygroscopicity and physical stability of the BRE-MAT co-amorphous system were evaluated to determine its suitability for long-term storage and potential pharmaceutical application.As shown in Fig. 9a, the pure crystalline BRE exhibited moderate hygroscopicity with a weight gain between 2% and15% after 24 h under 80% RH at 25 °C.In comparison, the physical mixtures and crystalline MAT demonstrated higher hygroscopicity, with weight gains exceeding > 15%, indicating their high moisture sensitivity 45 .

The co-amorphous BRE-MAT formulation showed a noticeable but controlled increase in hygroscopicity, absorbing moisture yet retaining its amorphous state without significant recrystallization.Upon exposure to high humidity for 10 days, the co-amorphous powder transitioned into a viscous, gel-like state (Fig. 9b), indicative of moisture absorption but without phase separation or crystallinity changes.This behavior suggests the need for appropriate packaging to prevent excessive moisture uptake during storage.

For the stability assessment, samples of the co-amorphous BRE-MAT were stored at 40 °C and 75% RH for six months.Throughout the storage period, the PXRD patterns (Fig. 9c) showed no appearance of sharp diffraction peaks, confirming the retention of the amorphous state.Additionally, the dissolution profile (Fig. 9d) of the stored co-amorphous system remained consistent with the freshly prepared samples, indicating that its enhanced dissolution behavior was preserved.These findings confirm the physical stability of the BRE-MAT co-amorphous system under accelerated conditions, supporting its potential application as a stable and effective formulation 46 .


Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a BRE-MAT co-amorphous salt that significantly enhances the solubility and dissolution rate of BRE.Through a combination of experimental and computational approaches, we demonstrated that the amorphous state and the formation of a salt-like complex between BRE and MAT led to enhanced intermolecular interactions and a substantial increase in aqueous solubility.Molecular dynamics simulations and thermodynamic analyses revealed that proton transfer from BRE's carboxyl group to MAT's amine facilitates strong hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions, stabilizing the co-amorphous system.

The co-amorphous BRE-MAT system showed over an 8000-fold increase in solubility compared to crystalline BRE, with a rapid and sustained dissolution profile, making it a promising formulation for improving the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs.The stability tests confirmed that the co-amorphous state and enhanced dissolution properties were retained under accelerated conditions, supporting its feasibility for long-term storage with appropriate moisture protection.

This study demonstrates the potential of drug-drug co-amorphous systems as an effective strategy to overcome solubility challenges.The findings provide a foundation for future in vivo investigations on the pharmacokinetic behavior of the BRE-MAT system, as well as its therapeutic efficacy in disease models.This co-amorphous approach may also be applicable to other drug pairs with complementary physicochemical properties, offering a versatile solution to solubility enhancement in pharmaceutical development.
