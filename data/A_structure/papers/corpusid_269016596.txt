This work describes the synthesis and characterization of manganese (II) imidazolate framework (MIF) using an environmentally friendly approach.The MIF, which is novel compound was synthesized for the first time using water as the solvent and imidazole as the ligand under temperate reaction conditions.The synthesized MIF was characterized by multitude of analytical techniques, considering (powder) X-ray diffraction (PXRD), Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Energy dispersive Xray spectrometry (EDX) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR).The characterization results disclosed that the synthesized MIF has a well-defined crystalline structure with metal-ligand bonding.Additionally, the MIF exhibited excellent gas adsorption 19) properties for various gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane.The findings of this study demonstrate the potential of using green synthesis methods for the development of highly functional materials with various practical applications, including catalysis, gas storage, and separation.

Introduction

Zeolite Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) and Manganese (II) Imidazolate Frameworks (MIFs) are both fascinating classes of Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs), which are highly porous materials with promising applications in various fields [1][2][3].In this comprehensive exploration, we will delve deeper into the characteristics, synthesis methods, and potential applications of ZIFs and MIFs.Additionally, we will emphasize the importance of green synthesis approaches and discuss the intricate coordination chemistry between metal ions and imidazole ligands in these MOFs.


ZIFs and MIFs

Zeolite Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) are a subset of Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) known for their remarkable porous structures.These MOFs are constructed by linking central metal ions, including Zinc, Iron, Copper, and Cobalt, with Imidazole ligands.ZIFs have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional characteristics, such as high porosity, large surface area, and customizable pore size.

Manganese (II) Imidazolate Frameworks (MIFs) represent a novel class of MOFs.They were first synthesized by coordinating central metal ions, specifically Manganese (Mn), with Imidazole ligands.MIFs exhibit exceptional thermal and chemical resistance, making them promising candidates for applications in catalysis and gas adsorption.


Green Synthesis in MOFs

Green synthesis has emerged as a vital approach in materials science, aiming to develop sustainable and eco-friendly methods for creating compounds with desirable properties.MOFs have attracted particular interest in this context due to their unique characteristics.Green synthesis methods not only reduce the environmental impact but also offer advantages in terms of product yield and purity.


Green Synthesis of MIFs

In this study, we present an innovative green synthesis approach for MIFs, utilizing water as the solvent and Imidazole as the ligand under mild reaction conditions.This eco-friendly method significantly reduces the environmental footprint associated with MOF synthesis [4].We will explore the detailed procedures and outcomes of this green synthesis.


Characterization of Green-Synthesized MIFs

The synthesized MIFs are characterized using a range of analytical techniques to determine their structural, thermal, and gas adsorption properties.These characterizations are essential for assessing the effectiveness of the green synthesis approach in developing functional MIFs with outstanding properties.


Comparing Green Synthesis with Organic Solvent Synthesis

In the broader context of MOF synthesis, we will compare the green synthesis approach using water as a solvent with traditional organic solvent-based methods.We will explore the differences in terms of product yield, purity, reaction conditions, and environmental impact.The advantages of using water as a green solvent will be highlighted.


Coordination Chemistry in MIFs

Understanding the coordination chemistry between manganese ions and imidazole ligands is crucial in comprehending the structure, properties, and potential applications of MIFs [5].We will delve into the intricate metal-ligand bonds that form the three-dimensional framework structure in MIFs [6].The role of Imidazole as a coordinating ligand with its lone pair of electrons will be elucidated.


Properties and Applications of MIFs

MIFs possess unique properties stemming from the coordination of manganese and imidazole, including exceptional porosity, a large surface area, and adjustable pore size.These properties are vital for various applications, such as gas storage, separation, catalysis, and sensing [7][8][9] .We will explore the potential of MIFs in these fields and discuss how their properties can be optimized by varying reaction conditions.


Differences Between ZIFs and MIFs

ZIFs and MIFs both are the type of MOF family with little difference.ZIFs and MIFs, despite some similarities, exhibit key differences primarily related to the metal ion used in their composition.ZIFs typically involve zinc, cobalt, copper, or iron ions, while MIFs uniquely feature manganese ions.These differences result in distinct structural and chemical properties in the two MOF types.We will delve into these disparities in detail.


Synthesis Methods for ZIFs and MIFs

Another significant difference between ZIFs and MIFs lies in their synthesis methods.ZIFs are typically synthesized using solvothermal or hydrothermal techniques, whereas MIFs can be synthesized through various approaches, including solvent-based and solvent-free methods.The choice of synthesis method plays a pivotal role in determining the properties of the resulting MOFs [10].


Tailoring MOFs for Specific Applications

Understanding the distinctions between ZIFs and MIFs is crucial for designing and synthesizing MOFs with customized properties tailored to specific applications.We will discuss how knowledge of these differences can aid in the development of new MOFs that address specific industry needs [11].In this extensive exploration of Zeolite Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) and Manganese (II) Imidazolate Frameworks (MIFs), we have covered their characteristics, green synthesis methods, coordination chemistry, properties, and applications.Furthermore, we have compared these two MOF types and emphasized the significance of understanding their distinctions for tailored materials design.


Methodology


Reagent and Solvent

The metal source Manganese (II) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4.H2O 98.72%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.Sigma Aldrich provided the Chemical compound of organic origin, imidazole (Im, 99.5%), for this study.


Synthesis

The synthesis method employed in this study proceeded as follows:


Preparation of Metal Salt Solution

A solution was prepared by dissolving 1.85M (0.839 g) of Manganese(II) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4.H2O) in 3 ml of distilled water.


Ligand Solution Preparation

In parallel, 0.444M (3.012 g) of Imidazole was accurately weighed and dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water.


Combining Solutions

The MnSO4.H2O solution was gradually introduced into the Imidazole solution while maintaining stirring at room temperature.The transition of the ligand solution to a light brown color marked a critical phase in the synthesis process.


Stirring and Color Transformation

The solution was stirred for a period of 5 hours at 1000 rpm, facilitated by a magnetic bar.

After 2 hours of stirring, the solution underwent a noticeable transformation, turning into a dark brown hue.


Rest Period

Subsequently, the solution was allowed to rest at room temperature for a duration of 24 hours.


Product Recovery

The product was separated from the solution through centrifugation, employing a speed of 2000 rpm for 10 minutes.To eliminate any residual contaminants, the product underwent three successive washes with methanol.


Drying

The product was subjected to a drying process in a vacuum oven for two days at a controlled temperature of 100°C, product obtained as depicted in Fig 1.


Characterization by FT-IR, XRD, TEM, EDX Study

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The functional groups present in sample were identified using an PerkinElmer Spectrum IR Version 106.1.


Powder X-Ray Diffraction (PXRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to characterize the sample.The XRD investigation involved scanning in the 2θ range of 3° to 45°, with a step size of 0.02° and a duration of 0.2 s per step, enabling the examination of all the samples.


Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

The Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) is similar in function to an optical microscope, but with much higher resolution capabilities, reaching up to approximately 0.1 nanometers for lattice images.The Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) operates within a voltage range of 80 to 200 kilovolts, providing a maximum magnification of 15 million times.Its resolution capability is 0.194 nanometers for point images and 0.14 nanometers for lattice images.The detector used in TEM is a Charge Couple Device (CCD) of Model JEM2100 Plus and Make JEOL.


Energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDX)

Energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry use to define characteristics and nature of the elements present in the sample.indicating the hydrating nature of MIF.Furthermore, the presence of absorption bands at 1062.65 cm-1 is conclude to the combined bending vibrations of O-H bonds and Mn atoms, while the strong peaks at 1434.95 cm -1 correspond to the stretching of the entire ring formation.Several spectral bands in the range of 1350 to 900 cm -1 are conclude with in-plane bending of the ring, whereas the peaks at 862.19 and 745.21 cm -1 are related to the bending of aromatic sp 2 hybridization C-H bonds [12].Lastly, the strong band at 418 cm -1 indicates the stretching of Mn-N bonds, as the Manganese atoms in the MIF structure connect to amine group of the Imidazolate ligand linker during its formation [13].
