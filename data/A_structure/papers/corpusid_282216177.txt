Percutaneous Thromboaspiration of Left Main Coronary Artery Vegetation in a Patient With Aortic Valve Infective Endocarditis and Acute Coronary Syndrome

Acute myocardial infarction from a vegetation is a rare complication of infective endocarditis.Thrombolysis, percutaneous coronary intervention with balloon angioplasty, stenting, and thromboaspiration, and surgery have all been described in this challenging clinical scenario.A woman with staphylococcal bioprosthetic aortic valve infective endocarditis developed acute myocardial infarction due to a vegetation invading the left main coronary artery, which was successfully managed with percutaneous thromboaspiration.

Introduction

I nfective endocarditis (IE) causing acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is rare and challenging to manage.We present a case of IE causing AMI managed acutely with percutaneous thromboaspiration, and review the available treatments in this scenario.


Case report

A 49-year-old female presented with subacute infective endocarditis (IE) in the context of left second toe osteomyelitis and right shoulder periprosthetic joint infection.Past medical history was significant for previous aortic valve IE managed with bioprosthetic valve replacement and root debridement, obesity, alcoholic peripheral neuropathy, and osteoporosis with multiple complex lower limb fractures.The patient required a frame to ambulate and carers to support activities of daily living.Blood cultures grew methicillin sensitive staphylococcus aureus, and intravenous cefazolin, rifampicin, and fusidic acid were commenced.Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) demonstrated a pedunculated 21 × 9 mm vegetation anchored to the left coronary cusp of the aortic valve (Fig. 1A).Computed tomography revealed splenic and renal infarcts.

The patient suffered a sudden deterioration on the ward with acute chest pain.ECG demonstrated anterior ST-segment depression (Fig. 1B).Coronary angiography revealed a bulky vegetation invading the left main coronary artery (LMCA) causing >50 % stenosis, extending to the ostial left circumflex artery (LCx) (Fig. 2A).Thromboaspiration was recommended with urgent redo-surgery if that were unsuccessful.

Initial aspiration attempts with the 6F guide catheter were unsuccessful.Aspiration was reattempted with a 6F thromboaspiration catheter (Thrombuster II, Kaneka Medical Products) with reduction in vegetation volume.Post-aspiration, a portion of vegetation had migrated down the ostial left anterior descending artery (LAD), which was wired and reaspirated.Following all thromboaspiration there was an excellent angiographic result, with TIMI-3 flow in both LAD/LCx, resolution of chest pain, and improvement in ST-segments (Fig. 2B).The aspirate was macroscopically consistent with bacteria, with no thrombus (Fig. 3A).There were no recognised postprocedural complications.

TOE post-angiography demonstrated normal LV size and systolic function, and a residual 9 × 6 mm vegetation (Fig. 3B).Redo valve replacement was not recommended due to the patient's comorbidities.The patient was managed medically with intravenous antibiotics and local infectious diseases follow-up.

Unfortunately, the patient deteriorated 7-weeks post angiography with recrudescent IE complicated by aortic root abscess, aortic thrombus, and embolic strokes.The patient died from refractory multiorgan failure and septic shock.


Discussion

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a rare complication of IE and may be caused by ostial occlusion, coronary embolism (CE), compression by an abscess, or development of an aneurysm or fistula.CE is uncommon, occurring in up to 2 % of IE cases [1].LMCA embolization is rare and frequently accompanied by cardiogenic shock and sudden death [2].The likely mechanism of AMI in this case is not a typical CE.Rather, the distinctive underlying anatomopathological event was likely engagement of the left coronary os by the large, mobile vegetation, and fragmentation into the left main trunk.

The treatment of choice for septic CE causing AMI is uncertain.Surgical thrombectomy or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI), and medical management with anticoagulation have all been reported with success in the literature.This is, to our knowledge, the first case describing the successful use of percutaneous thromboaspiration alone to treat a LMCA endocarditic vegetation causing AMI.


Medical management

Thrombolytics are contraindicated in patients with IE and AMI due to the high risk of intracranial haemorrhage (57 %) and death (43 %) as reported in one review [3].Moreover, since the embolized material may be primarily composed of vegetation rather than thrombus, thrombolysis is unlikely to be effective.

The role of therapeutic anticoagulation remains uncertain.Van de Boogert et al. described a case of aortic bioprosthetic endocarditis with AMI from a LMCA embolus where the patient was successfully managed conservatively with 6 weeks of therapeutic heparin and penicillin [4].In centres without cardiothoracics or PCI, or in patients deemed too high-risk for intervention, therapeutic anticoagulation may be reasonable to prevent secondary thrombus formation.


Percutaneous coronary interventions

Coronary angiography with PCI represents the preferred initial approach for embolic AMI complicating IE, offering both diagnostic confirmation and therapeutic potential while guiding subsequent surgical planning.Although angiography is generally safe, the risk of catheter-induced vegetation embolizationalbeit rareis heightened with large, mobile aortic vegetations [5].Supporting this, Laperche et al. reported no strokes among 49 patients with aortic vegetations undergoing pre-surgical angiography, though two cases (2.2 %) of mitral valve IE suffered peri-procedural stroke, underscoring the need for individualized risk stratification [6].For patients deemed prohibitively high-risk, CT coronary angiography provides a viable non-invasive alternative.

Percutaneous strategies, including thrombectomy, balloon angioplasty, and stenting, must balance efficacy against unique risks such as mycotic aneurysm formation and stent seeding [7].While thromboaspiration often suffices for embolic AMI, unsuccessful or high-risk cases demand multidisciplinary escalation.Definitive management hinges on surgical candidacy: nonsurgical patients may require stenting or angioplasty, whereas operative candidates should proceed to combined embolectomy/CABG and valve intervention.In critically unstable patients, stenting serves as a temporizing bridge to surgery.This decision-making complexity mandates tertiarycentre expertise and early multidisciplinary involvement to optimize outcomes.


Surgery

Surgical management of embolic AMI in IE with valve replacement and embolectomy or CABG has been reported with success in the literature.However, in many cases these patients already had indications for surgery in severe valve lesions and could not be treated with transcatheter techniques alone [8].The success of surgical embolectomy is also dependent on the site of embolus; in this case retrieval may have been difficult and access in a redo setting is potentially high risk.The safety of CABG in IE has been investigated, and a 2023 review found CABG at the time of valve replacement did not affect perioperative or long-term mortality (mean follow-up 8.2 years), but was associated with a higher risk of postoperative stroke [9].

A hybrid PCI-surgical approach is advantageous in select scenarios: patients with recent haemorrhagic strokes (where guidelines recommend delaying surgery for 30 days to avoid neurologic complications), those lacking pre-operative screening for cerebral mycotic aneurysms (potentially requiring intervention before bypass) and those with persistent bacteraemia or needing further medical optimization (e.g.heart failure) [10].In such cases, PCI serves as a temporizing measure, with urgent surgery reserved for percutaneous failures.


Conclusion

Endocarditic vegetations causing AMI are rare and difficult to manage.Percutaneous therapies, especially thromboaspiration, may be considered first line in this circumstance.Percutaneous therapies may be temporizing measures for patients awaiting surgery, or definitive treatments in nonsurgical candidates.
