Molecular interplay of ADAMTS13-MDTCS and von willebrand Factor-A2: deepened insights from extensive atomistic simulations

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare and life-threatening disease. One hallmark is severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, causing ultra-large von Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers to accumulate, leading to microthrombi and lastly to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. Despite great success in recent decades, the molecular picture of the interaction between VWF and ADAMTS13 remains vague. Here, we utilized modern replica-exchange molecular dynamics simulations with the TIGER2h method to sample a vast configurational space of the isolated ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains and the exposure to its substrate and activating cofactor -the unraveled VWF-A2 domain. The sampling of binding sites and conformations was guided and filtered in agreement with available experimental evidence. We provide comprehensive information on exosites for each domain and direct pairs of interacting amino acids, for the first time. The major binding cluster for the active site of the MP domain contrasts the previous mapping of VWF-A2 residues and reciprocal binding pockets. Two major binding modes are revealed and provide access to conformational changes of an extended gatekeeper tetrad upon overcoming local latency during substrate binding and to a dedicated recruitment mechanism. Our work adds the first molecular interaction model that places previous experimental results in perspective to better understand disease-related mutations towards improved therapies. Numerous empirical targets are proposed to verify the given binding modes, to refine the overall picture of MP binding pockets, the role of Dis binding in MP activation and the passage of the Cys-rich domain through VWF-A2, thus deepening the understanding of a highly dynamic interplay.ARTICLE HISTORY

Introduction

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare and life-threatening disease . One hallmark for TTP is severe ADAMTS13 (A Disintegrin and Metalloprotease with Thrombospondin type 1 repeats-13) deficiency (Kim et al., 2021;Petri et al., 2019). Severe deficiency can either be caused by mutations in the ADAMTS13 gene (hereditary TTP) or can be induced by anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies (immunemediated TTP) Zheng, 2015). Mechanisms, by which antibodies cause ADAMTS13 deficiency include inhibition and clearance from circulation. During ADAMTS13 deficiency, ultra-large von Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers accumulate and lead to the formation of microthrombi, resulting in microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and severe thrombocytopenia (Peyvandi et al., 2008;Zheng, 2015). VWF exists as disulfide bond linked multimers (from dimers to >100-mers) and is crucial for platelet recruitment at damaged blood vessels (Kim et al., 2021;Petri et al., 2019;South et al., 2014). Whereas too little VWF leads to a bleeding disorder called von Willebrand disease, high plasma VWF concentrations and large multimers are risk factors for thrombosis (Zheng, 2015). Hence, tight and precise regulation of both VWF concentration and VWF multimer size is necessary. The size of VWF is proteolytically regulated by ADAMTS13. Upon experiencing shear stress, the A2 domain of VWF unravels, revealing a specific binding and cleavage site for ADAMTS13 (Petri et al., 2019). Simultaneously, unraveling of the A2 domain is physically regulated: ultra-large VWF multimers experience higher shear stress, making them more prone to unfolding compared to smaller multimers (Zhang et al., 2009).

For ADAMTS13-MDTCS, multiple VWF-binding exosites are proposed to determine specificity and participate in twostage allosteric activation by VWF (Kim et al., 2021;Petri et al., 2019;Schelpe et al., 2020). While a 'global latency' state is defined by the CUB1-2 domains being bound to the Spacer module, an additional 'local latency' mode is characterized by the central gatekeeper residues blocking substrate entry to the active site cleft (Kim et al., 2021). The crystal structures of the ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains and the CUB1-2 domains have been solved and docking data of the CUB1-2 domains to the ADAMTS13 Spacer domain have recently given insights into the Spacer-CUB1-2 interface (Kim et al., 2021). However, no mechanism has yet been presented to explain how local latency is overcome. Great progress has been made in recent years in uncovering numerous mechanisms by which ADAMTS13 regulates hemostasis and thrombosis. However, a detailed molecular model of the interaction between ADAMTS13 and VWF has not yet been elucidated. Current treatment options for TTP are plasma exchange therapy and immunosuppressive agents or plasmainfusion, which reduces the overall mortality rate from 85-95% to 10-20% (Bell et al., 1991;Rock et al., 1991). Administration of recombinant ADAMTS13 has been proposed as an alternative treatment approach (Zheng, 2015). Therefore, it is critical to understand the molecular basis that governs the recognition and binding of VWF to ADAMTS13.

Here, we created a molecular model of the ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains bound to the unraveled VWF-A2 domain using our TIGER2h Kulke et al., 2018;Schulig et al., 2022) hybrid solvent replica-exchange method, a fast and accurate T-REMD derivative. The sampling of binding configurations was guided by and compared to existing mutagenesis data and crystal structures. The final proteolytic binding complex and a dedicated recruitment stage are proposed. Insights into the local latency mechanism assumed for MP activation upon Dis binding and transition between two binding states are suggested. The formerly termed 'gatekeeper triad' is extended into a 'gatekeeper-tetrad' by addition of R257.

Our model can be applied as a structural platform for the development of novel therapeutics not only in TTP, but also in other diseases associated with a disrupted ADAMTS13/ VWF axis, such as myocardial infarction, stroke and malaria (Hanson et al., 2009;Larkin et al., 2007;Matsukawa et al., 2007;Stepanian et al., 2011). For example, auto-antibody resistant gain-of-function variants of ADAMTS13 hold great potential (Jian et al., 2012).

Simulation preparation

Protein structures were taken from the RCSB data base (Burley et al., 2021), while sequence and function annotations were taken from Uniprot ('UniProt: a worldwide hub of protein knowledge', 2018) (accession codes Q76LX8 and P04275 for ADAMTS13 and VWF, respectively). The ADAMTS13-MDTCS model was built majorly from PDB ID 6QIG chain C (79-682), including the zinc ion in the active center, three calcium ions in the MP domain, and seven glycan chains distributed across the surface of MDTCS. Missing amino acids in the Cys-rich domain (454-468) were partly included from PDB ID 3GHM (452-458 and 466-469). The region 459-465 was added as a random coil. The present Q225E mutation in 6QIG was reverted with CHARMM-GUI (Jo et al., 2008). The unraveled VWF-A2 (1598-1673) domain has been obtained by stress-induced unfolding through constant velocity pulling at the terminal residues, starting from PDB ID 3ZQK chain A and following the procedure described by Dong et al. (2019). Afterwards, a representative extended state was selected.

The initial ADAMTS13-MDTCS model was first subjected to sampling with TIGER2h (Table S1, simulation 1) in absence of VWF-A2. The MDTCS model is placed into a periodic cell of 150 � 70 � 70 Å, solvated in water, and neutralized accordingly. The simulation with 12 replicas proceeded for roughly 15 ms total simulation time (including quenching cycles) and generated about 45k states. The average exchange rate was 28% with an 11.3 K average temperature change per exchange.

We selected a representative state from simulation 1 for ADAMTS13-MDTCS and placed the VWF-A2 strand covering amino acids 1598 up to 1673 centered below the lower tips of the Dis and Cys-rich domains, with the shortest distance in the range of 15 Å ( Figure 1). The N-terminal P1-P1' sequence (Y1605-M1606) points towards the MP domain, while the C-terminal is directed towards the Spacer domain. In addition, the omnipresent stretching force naturally acting on the VWF-A2 strand is mimicked by constant force pulling. This prevents the terminal ends from folding or looping back and keeps the strand gently extended. The system was placed into a larger periodic cell of 210 � 80 � 80 Å and solvated and neutralized as before. Sampling with TIGER2h and 16 replicas proceeded for a total of 22 ms (Table S1, simulation 2). We obtained a structural ensemble of roughly 58k states and the average temperature change per exchange was 9.8 K at an average acceptance rate of 27%.

Two replicates of simulation 2 were run. Simulation 3 started with front passage on the Cys-rich domain, as previously proposed by de Groot et al. (2015), and simulation 4 started with back passage. In addition, the distance for the MP domain guiding restraints is reduced to 2.5 Å to force close zinc ion binding (Table S1), which enables observation of necessary conformational changes in a backward fashion. The sampling of simulation 3 and simulation 4 proceeded for approx. 27 ms and 26 ms total simulation time, with roughly 69k and 68k structures each. The average temperature change per exchange was 10.4 K and 10.6 K, respectively, and 28% average exchange rate for both runs.


Restraints and guides

Unnaturally strong interactions between the carboxyl groups of glutamic and aspartic acid residues with the zinc ion in the active site of the ADAMTS13-MP domain arise from missing charge polarization effects in classical force fields and were suppressed by protective flat-bottom restraints that repelled these side chains at distances below 4.5 Å and a force constant of 50 (kcal/mol)/Å. 2 These included amino acids from the MP domain (E225, D252) and the A2 strand (E1598, D1614, E1615).

The accessible conformational space of the ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains during the sampling was limited by two separate RSMD flat-bottom restraints, covering either MP through TSP1 or TSP1 through Spacer, respectively. The MP and Dis domains, as well as the Cys-rich and Spacer domains, share a large interface and individual structural restraints on each domain induce extremely high restrictions on the conformational flexibility. Restraints disallowed a deviation in the backbone RMSD from the initial structure by more than 15 Å at a force constant of 1 (kcal/mol)/Å. 2 To prevent refolding of the VWF-A2 domain and to guide the interaction between both molecules, the centers of experimentally proposed exosites on the MP, Dis, Cys-rich and Spacer domains were kept in proximity to reciprocal regions on the VWF-A2 strand ( Figure 1) by soft flat-bottom restraints. Only when the upper distance limit of up to 25 Å (Table S1) was exceeded, these regions of the VWF-A2 strand could not further detach from their proposed interaction site on the MDTCS surface. The observed distances of these restraints were not limited by their maximum thresholds in simulations 2-4 but remained well below them. This indicates that no favorable interaction sites were excluded by the restraints settings. Previously proposed boundary conditions for the molecular interaction between VWF-A2 and ADAMTS13-MDTCS. The active center containing the catalytic glutamic acid and the Zn 2þ ion is expected to be occluded by a Ca 2þ binding loop built from amino acids 180-193. The arginine R193 in this loop is coordinated by two aspartic acid residues in proximity and they build a gatekeeper triad. The previously suggested exosites for VWF are highlighted at the surface of all ADAMTS13 domains (gray), except for TSP1 whose exosites are unknown. The critical positions on the unraveled VWF-A2 strand are emphasized and are expected to interact near their reciprocal counterparts at the ADAMTS13 domains. The omnipresent shear stress that is experienced by the unraveled A2 domain is to be considered in a range of 1 pN at each end of the strand (Zhang et al., 2009). Highlighted are also attachment sites for artificial sugars, that did not interfere with VWF binding but significantly impaired the interaction in one particular case (de Groot et al., 2015). This information is used to evaluate the final model, as the correct passage of the A2 strand around the Cys-rich domain is not yet fully understood.

ADAMTS13-MP residues coordinating calcium or zinc ions were preserved to the crystal structure state by additional harmonic bonds with a force constant of 1 (kcal/mol)/Å. 2 The omnipresent stretching force acting on the VWF-A2 domain naturally was considered during the sampling by constant force pulling at each terminal ends with 1 pN, preventing refolding.


Binding affinities and contact statistics

Contact statistics in complexes were evaluated by a parallel in-house code that counts individual residue contacts between both proteins and has been already proven effective in earlier works (Kulke et al., 2017;Kulke & Langel, 2020;M€ oller et al., 2017). A contact is characterized by atoms of any two residues between two selections coming closer than 2.5 Å. Thereby, persistent contacts and possibly important residues for the binding can be identified. The counts are normalized to the number of frames, to represent the average fraction of contacts per residue throughout the simulation and are used to interpret binding affinities. Affinity values greater than one indicate binding to several other residues simultaneously. On the one hand, the contact statistics can be displayed in a network-like diagram, which allows the evaluation of specific individual contacts. On the other hand, they can be averaged from the perspective of both proteins to identify important residues and surface areas in general. Increased specificity of contacts is seen when individual compounds in the network plot have high affinity. Low specificity, in contrast, is seen when there are many interactions of a residue with only low affinities, especially when these contacts occur alternately rather than simultaneously. In this case, this indicates multiple binding sites or a non-specific interaction. However, if multiple low-affinity contacts occur simultaneously, this is likely an indication of a residue that is bound in a specific binding pocket.


Clustering of binding modes

Because alternative binding configurations are found on each domain, RMSD cluster analysis using VMD is performed to extract distinct binding modes. This allows for a more accurate characterization of the contacts and validation of models based on experimental groundwork. Close contacts of the P1 position Y1605 to the zinc ion in simulations 3 and 4 were found in approx. half of the frames. During the remaining time, the zinc ion was blocked by water molecules. The states were filtered by the condition that the carbonyl oxygen atom of Y1605 must be closer than 2.5 Å to the zinc ion, hereafter referred to as 'zinc-bound' states.

After the alignment of the MP and Dis domains, RMSD cluster analysis was carried out on the important VWF-A2 region 1603-1606, searching for five clusters with a 5 Å cutoff. One strong cluster (Cluster1) of roughly 90% was returned. Further subdivision by subsequent cluster analysis with a reduced cutoff of 3 Å of the important D1614 position reveals two distinct binding modes that are discussed in more detail. The first cluster (Model1) reaches 8.22%, while the second cluster (Model2) covers 6.57%. The remaining clusters are of rapidly dropping percentages and less specific configurations.


Results and discussion


Experimental boundary conditions

We collected the major experimental evidence for the molecular interplay between ADAMTS13 and VWF molecules ( Figure 1) to feed them into our sampling. Proposed exosites of ADAMTS13-MDTCS and reciprocal regions on the VWF-A2 strand cannot be assumed to be in direct contact. Therefore, binding is only gently guided between the centers of such amino acid groups. De Groot et al. (2015) summarized the exosite information on the ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains and suggested the importance of the Cys-rich domain. In addition, insights were provided into several locations on the Cys-rich domain, where artificially adding large sugar chains could impair VWF-A2 binding significantly. This information was useful to weight the different binding configurations we obtained. Important segments on the VWF-A2 strand were massively screened by Kretz et al. (2015) and lately summarized per-domain by Petri et al. (2019), while a work by Dong et al. (2019) investigated the shear stress experienced by the VWF-A2 domain upon unfolding. Hence, for the Dis domain, residues R349-L350 and the respective counterpart on the VWF-A2 strand D1614-D1622 must be involved. For the Cys-rich domain, a hydrophobic pocket A472 to V474 has been identified and was guided into the proximity of VWF-A2 region I1642-I1651. The proposed Spacer domain exosite built from R660, R661 and Y665 is directed towards E1660-R1668 of VWF-A2. For the MP domain, the proposed gatekeepers R193, D217 and D252 and the zinc ion will come in direct contact with VWF-A2, while its L1603 and Y1605-M1606 positions will show relevance in the complexation. More universally here, distance restraints are built directly between the carbonyl oxygen atom of Y1605 and the catalytic zinc ion, which are ultimately assumed to connect.


ADAMTS13-MDTCS in isolation

Even though changes in the secondary and tertiary structure were limited during the sampling, the large flexibility of the overall tertiary structure in terms of bending and twisting motions was evident. The average backbone RMSD from the crystal structure for the whole molecule was in the range of 7.4 Å. The same measurement of the MP-Dis and Cys-Spacer domain pairs reveal 2.9 Å and 4.3 Å, respectively. A major pivot point is the TSP1 repeat, around which rotation and bending motions are enabled. Occasionally, the bend was high enough to enable slight interactions between the lower tips of the Dis and Cys-rich modules ( Figure S1). Tilting or slight rotational movements of the Dis domain at its interface with the MP domain are conspicuous and accompanied by movements of the disordered Dis strand running down the backside of MP (287-299). Here, the TSP1 repeat may act as a lever, to adjust the relative position of the Cys-rich and Spacer domains, apparently important to build the first contact with VWF-A2, and the distant MP module responsible for the actual cleavage reaction. By aligning the MDTCS domains from left to right, the proposed Cys-rich exosite position around A472 to V474 was barely accessible. It was centrally located on the inner side of the Cys-rich domain in the Z-direction and at the top, directly at the transition surface between Spacer and Cys-rich domain in the Y-direction (Figure 1). Domains did not deviate significantly from the crystal structure, which is reflected in average RMSD values of 1.1 Å, 2.1 Å, 1.8 Å, 3 Å and 2.2 Å for the ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains, respectively. The largest deviation occurred for the Cys-rich module and was caused by flexible regions at the interface to the Spacer domain. The missing sequence segments of the Cys-rich module from the crystal structure were already excluded during the RMSD calculation.


ADAMTS13-MDTCS exposed to VWF-A2

The relaxed ADAMTS13-MDTCS structure was confronted with the unfolded VWF-A2 domain to scan the possibility space of binding modes. Subsequently, we checked whether the binding fulfills the previous experimental suggestions and investigated the influence on the ADAMTS13 structure.

With the loose guiding in simulation 2 (Table S1), no spontaneous binding of the Y1605 carbonyl oxygen atom to the zinc ion within the active site of MP was observed. The strong polar interaction between water oxygen atoms and the zinc ion blocked the binding most of the time. An allosteric change in the MP domain after VWF-A2 binding the Dis domain is currently assumed that overcomes local latency. This process is likely dependent on the stabilization of the MDTCS domains by correct and sufficiently long binding of all exosites by A2 to trigger the transition. Subsequently, easier access to the zinc ion is possible. Hence, correct binding to the MP domain is dependent on appropriately binding to the other domains. However, multiple concurrent binding configurations were contained in the data. Most strikingly, the structures studied at the Cys-rich domain are divided into configurations that bind at two different interfaces, either the front or the back, and the correct interaction site cannot be determined from simulation 2 alone. Hence, additional simulations were required to investigate the binding and individual contacts at the Cys-rich domain in detail. The front or back side notion refers to ADAMTS13-MDTCS oriented from MP on the left to Spacer on the right (Figure 1). We filtered out all states which were not in compliance with the proposed interaction sites on the Dis, Cys-rich and Spacer domains (see Supporting Information) and selected two representative structures with VWF-A2 passing the Cys-rich domain on either front or back side, and ran two replicates (simulations 3 and 4) of the simulation with these starting conditions.


Passage of the cys-rich domain

To localize the exosite of the Cys-rich module, affinities were calculated separately for states exhibiting binding on either the front or the back side ( Figure 2 and Figures S2, S3 and S4). The initial positions of the VWF-A2 strand at the Cys-rich domain were very different between simulations 2, 3 and 4, starting in a central, front-side, or back-side position, respectively. The preference of simulations to produce binding states on either interface was evaluated over time, revealing a common behavior (Figure 2, bottom, right). All simulations evolve in favor of the back-side binding interface as the simulation time increases. Hence, VWF-A2 binding on the back side of the Cys-rich domain can be considered more stable and energetically favorable.

The associations for front-side binding states appear unspecific and cover a large surface area (Figure 2, left). Several interaction hot spots appear on the surface of the Cys-rich domain, but individual affinities are low ( Figure S3). Here, L458 would interact mostly with E1640, but also with multiple partners in the nearby range of I1642 to V1636. H469 interacted majorly with A1647 and I1649 and slightly with W1644 and N1646. In addition, individual, more pronounced contacts appeared between Q456 and I1642, and between Y468 and W1644.

The interactions of back-side binding states appeared more specific and were analog to the Dis domain ( Figure  S5), which is very similar in structure, despite very low sequence identity (Petri et al., 2019). Here, the same binding site at the sheet around 346-351 of the Dis domain translates directly to the same position on the Cys-rich domain residues 493-498 and a similar binding mode (Figure 2, right). The VWF-A2 strand connects to and extends this beta-sheet, thereby revealing the significance of I495 and K497 and the nearby surface area ( Figure S4).

The region on the VWF-A2 strand proposed to interact with the Cys-rich domain is captured by the sampling simulation and is slightly extended to neighboring residues, especially towards the N-terminal. Noticeably, the proposed exosite position on the Cys-rich domain, covering A472, A473 and V474, did not show large involvement in the interaction, on neither the front nor back side. Its central location between the Cysrich and Spacer domain interface makes it barely accessible for A2. We assume that replacing the sequence of 471-474 (de Groot et al., 2015) caused structural complications at the interface of Cys-rich and Spacer, instead of disrupting a binding site for A2. The hydrophobic side chains of A472 and V474 appear to be occupied by hydrophobic partners L443 from Cys-rich and L619 and L620 from the Spacer domain most of the time, with the latter located in a loop from Spacer reaching into the Cys-rich domain.

De Groot et al. (2015) tested various mutations on the Cys-rich module surface to narrow down the correct exosite region and included residues on both interfaces. As additional guidance, several positions at the Cys-rich domain surface were reported, where adding large sugar chains could have a significant influence on the binding between both molecules (Figure 1). None of the tested mutations revealed any impact on the proteolytic function. In our simulations, the binding on the front side does not have a specific configuration, so knocking out individual amino acids on the surface may have no effect. The important L458 position on the front site highlighted by our data was not previously experimentally tested. Blocking the Gly2 (H469) position did not result in significant impairment of VWF binding previously, but the frontside binding indicated large involvement of this position during the interaction (Figures 2 and 3). In contrast, the glycan attachment site with the largest effect Gly3 (H476) is only in significant contact during backside binding, which also includes interactions on the Gly4 position, where a slight impairment effect may as well be argued from the data presented by De Groot et al. Mutation of H476 showed no effect, but binding as a sheet motif across the backbone, as seen during back-side binding, may also not necessarily be due to specific side-chain interactions. The most important back side residue in our data is I495 and has not been knocked out previously.


Proteolytic binding complex (Model1)

The binding configuration for the A2 region 1601-1610 appeared similar for most zinc-bound states (Figure 4 and Figures S6, S7 and S8). Here, L185 interacts largely with the P3 position L1603 and also weaker with the P1' and P2' positions M1606 and V1607, while Q191 shows major contacts with P3' T1608 and slightly with its chain neighbors up to N1611. Next to it, V192 is strongly connecting the P1' residue M1606, which in turn also binds to V195 of MP. The P1 residue Y1605 binds to multiple targets, with the strongest being T196, followed by slightly weaker probabilities for Q197, L198, H228 and H234 indicating its location within the hydrophobic patch around the zinc ion. H234 in turn also slightly binds V1604 and V1607. Position G1609 interacts with S251 and D252, while N1610 shows slight contact with G253.

The special location of the important P3 amino acid L1603 in a pocket made mostly of the S3 residue L185, is likely responsible for the helical loop motif we observed around VWF-A2 region 1602-1607. Thereby, the placement of side chains within individual pockets on the MP surface appears very different than what was formerly proposed (Figure 4 (a)). Xiang et al (2011) proposed the S1 pocket hosting Y1605 near L151 and V195 before the crystal structure of the MP domain was made available by Petri et al. (2019). They then suggested the S1 pocket rather around L147, L151, L185, V192 and V195. However, in our model, these residues host the P1' amino acid M1606 instead, while Y1605 is located in a pocket made of H232, H228, L198, V195 and T196. De Groot et al. also proposed a deep S1' pocket around D252-P256. It is held closed by the gatekeeper triad in an inactive form and shall be revealed by motions of the calcium-binding loop (180-193) (de Groot et al., 2010;Petri et al., 2019). In our model, this pocket is rather occupied by the P3' amino acid T1608 and does not require significant motion of the mentioned loop.

Instead, only during the proteolytic binding stage, the loop left to the active site cleft performed a conformational shift, guided by a new contact between R257 and D252, with G252 and A250 stabilizing R257 in its new position. This creates room for the A2 strand to pass through. Meanwhile, the gatekeeper R193 detached from D252, while the R193 and D217 connection was only rarely lost. The R193 side chain moved to the right and towards T214 and E212, which are located in the calcium complex next to the active site of MP. The lower R193 side chain then participated in hydrophobic interactions with T1608. The prominent conformation change of R257 towards the gatekeeper D252 could not be observed for the isolated MDTCS domains in simulation 1, but the changed R193 position could be seen occasionally. We hence suggest an important role of R257 to overcome local latency and to activate the MP domain, which must result from A2 recruitment. We propose to extend the gatekeeper triad to a tetrad by adding R257. Remarkably, R257 within ADAMTS13 is as unique in the ADAMTS family as the already known R193, D217 and D252 (Petri et al., 2019). Because direct contacts of R257 with VWF-A2 are rare, the reason for the conformational change could result from changes at the MP-Dis interface due to rotational movements supported by the leverage of the TSP1 domain and the remote Cys-rich and Spacer modules.

Literature suggests that in the neighboring Dis domain the key residue D1614 is linked to R349 (de Groot et al., 2009). Indeed, our proposed proteolytic binding mode covers almost all states where this interaction is present. Thus, this is the only configuration that exhibits the expected binding between D1614 and R349. Moreover, only a small fraction of the structures without zinc binding from simulation 2 remains.

Amino acids around R349 and L350 were the most prominent binding partners to the VWF-A2 strand ( Figure S5). A large array of specific contacts thereby locks A2 to the Dis domain surface (Figure 4, bottom), with the strand attaching to the beta-sheet motif around R349 with its D1614 and I1616 residues. Meanwhile, E1615 binds to S348 and L1617 connects to S346. The region between 1618 and 1622 builds a conspicuous hairpin motif and the A2 strand continues to create a sheet structure on the inner side of Dis (318-325) (Figure 4, top). Here, major contacts were mediated between Q1624 and S325, followed by V1625 and F324, V1626 and T323, P1627 and C322, and I1628 and A321, respectively ( Figure S9).

For the Cys-rich domain, we believe that the back side region surrounding I495 is the relevant interface, as mentioned before. During the final binding stage, this interface is exclusively bound by VWF-A2 in our data. The most prominent interactions appeared from I495 to I1651 and K497 to I1649 (Figure 4 and Figure S10) and surrounding areas.

Previously, the Spacer exosite was thought to be located around R660, Y661 and Y665 because their mutations affected ADAMTS13 activity ( Figure 5; Schelpe et al., 2020).

Later, these positions have also been linked to the binding interface of the CUB1-2 domains, which mediate the global latency mode. The currently suggested mechanism includes a latent conformation of ADAMTS13 in which the C-terminal CUB domains are bound to the Spacer module. Despite the dual inhibitory effect observed when the CUB domains are bound, the mechanism and whether the CUB1-2 domains and the A2-strand share the same binding interface are not clear. Petri et al. suggested that the region around I611 on the surface of the Spacer domain is a candidate for the A2 exosite. Very recent work presented the crystal structure of the two C-terminal CUB domains and proposed the attachment site to the Spacer domain surface based on docking results. Spacer residues 364-367, 591-592 and 658-665 hosted the binding site for the CUB domains, and the region around I611 was left for possible A2 binding (Kim et al., 2021).

In our simulations, the surface area on the Spacer domain involved in interactions with VWF-A2 shows large coverage of the site (Figure 5, top) near R660, Y661 and Y665 and a smaller area around R636, both regions apparently part of the CUB1-2 interface. The additional region around I611 is not proposed to interact with the CUB1-2 domains and may hence harbor the actual VWF-A2 binding site. The reciprocal regions on the VWF-A2 strand are extended strongly towards the N-terminal, from what was formerly suggested (Figure 5, bottom), most prominently R1659. Because the individual affinities were rather low, this indicates that multiple binding configurations are contained in the data ( Figure S11). In fact, a large impact of front-or back-side passage on the Cys-rich domain appears on individual contacts. These differences are minor for the region around R660, Y661 and Y665, whereas the variance becomes larger near the transition to the Cys- rich module, making it important to understand the correct passage on Cys-rich.

The most prominent interactions for our proposed proteolytic state can be noted between two arginine amino acids (R1659 and R636), which engage in planar stacking of their side chains (Figure 4), supported by L637. The neighboring P1658 shows to be harbored in a well-shaped pocket made of S610, P613, L632 and P638 and involves more contacts in the nearby area ( Figure S12). This makes the region around I611 in fact an important binding location on the Spacer domain. Contact for the previously suggested R660 was recurrently found with E1660, also in the unfiltered data. Y661 was in contact with A1661, which in turn also connected to L591, while Y665 interacted with L1664.

VWF multimers are self-folded to prevent unwanted interactions of the VWF-A1 domain with platelets. (b) In case of blood vessel damage, VWF-A3 domains bind to exposed subendothelial collagen, and the shear stress unpacks the VWF multimers into an extended chain. Platelets can then be recruited to the damaged vessel site, and this increases the shear forces on the VWF-A2 domain further. The A2 domain completely unfolds into a linear peptide strand and exposes the binding sites for ADAMTS13 and thus, the cryptic cleavage site. (c) The CUB domains of ADAMTS13 bind to the D4-CK domain of VWF (1) and get released from the Spacer domain (2). The Spacer (3) and Cys-rich (4) domains bind their precursor exosites on the unraveled A2 domain and thereby, guide the further association. The Dis domain binds a shifted A2-exosite around D1619 (5). Binding of D1614 to the calcium ion near the active site cleft allows the transition to the MP domain (6). The A2 strand binds the MP domain in a precursor state, not ready for cleavage (7). (d) The A2 strand is shifted towards the TSP1 domain from both ends (8) and the Dis, Cys-rich and Spacer domains bind their final target sites. The A2-strand is pulled through the gatekeeper tetrad and causes an allosteric change (9) that opens the active center (local latency). The MP domain can ultimately lock to the A2-cleavage site and cut the peptide strand.

However, most of the remaining structures did not feature the expected binding of D1614 to the Dis domain as proposed by literature but rather states in accordance with Model2, where the reciprocal A2 region is shifted ( Figure S13). The alternative binding found on the Dis domain in such an excess and in addition to the conspicuous calcium-binding configuration observed in Model2, suggests a precursor state ( Figure 6). Because Model1 and Model2 were obtained by clustering D1614 near the Dis domain, the finding of rather unambiguously different binding to the distant Cys-rich and Spacer modules between the two models instead of a random mixture is, hence, very surprising. This suggests that binding to the MP-Dis or Cys-Spacer ends is a consequence of the binding mode found at the opposite end, as is the case for the two main models. Therefore, we suggest an important recruitment function of the Dis domain that is in accordance with Model2 as a precursor mode, required to guide the A2 scissile bond into the active site cleft. In our data, Dis is a key domain and harbors the largest affinities of all ADAMTS13-MDTCS domains. Slightly stronger individual contacts were observed for MP, but only due to the pulling of Y1605 ( Figure S6 and Figure S14). Besides, transition to Model1 is necessary for proteolytic function and activation of the MP domain to overcome local latency.

Contacts at the MP domain and A2 region 1601-1610 were similar in both models but less pronounced in the precursor Model2 ( Figure S15). Upon conversion of Model2 to the final proteolytic state of Model1, there is a slight pulling motion towards the C-terminus, and the contacts become more tightly locked.

For Model2, VWF-A2 shifts roughly 5 amino acids towards the N-terminal at the Dis domain compared to Model1. Hence, the previously proposed region on the A2 strand is extended strongly towards the C-terminus and up to I1628 ( Figure S5). Here, D1614 directly connects to the calcium ion next to the active site cleft in the MP domain (Figure 4(b)). K1617 builds the first contact with L351, which in turn binds strongly to L1619 ( Figure S14 and Figure S16). Meanwhile, R1618 interacts weaker with L350, and the cascade continues from L1619 with strong affinities towards R349, which is also binding to P1620, strongly to G1621, slightly to D1622 and I1623, giving R349 the largest affinity. Further, D1622 is connecting to S348 and slightly to the neighboring C347, which in turn shows strong affinities towards I1623. S347 shows also contacts to I1623 and Q1624. The final contacts appear from residue V1625 to S345 and slightly to F324, which also binds to I1623 ( Figure S16).

The functional importance of the calcium binding site next to the cleavage site in MP was suggested by Gardner et al. (2009) and besides stability reasons, both our models harbor possible explanations for a function during recruitment or activation. For Model1, the gatekeeper arginine R193 rotates out of the active site cleft and is complexed by residues near T214 and E212, the latter of which being directly involved in the calcium center. For Model2, D1614 directly connects to the calcium ion and this may help guiding the transition of A2 from the Dis domain to MP.

At Cys-rich, Model2 binds the back side interface most of the time but also contains high amounts of front-side binding states. Individual connection is observed from I495 to E1640 and I1642, while K497 barely connects to E1638 ( Figure S17).

Also, for Spacer, Model2 features a strongly shifted interface on the A2 strand, most pronounced for the region around I611 (Figure 4(c)). Here, the A2 region 1649-1651 binding to the Cys-rich domain in Model1, would instead strongly lock to the Spacer domain ( Figure S18).

We compared the relative importance of A2 residues from our models and the data made available by Kretz et al. (2015), where all possible substitutions on amino acids 1596-1668 of a VWF-A2 substrate have been screened. We expect that their penalties in substrate turnover upon mutating positions on the A2 strand, will align with our data, or show other indications for a role in the observed binding mechanisms. For the final proteolytic state (Model1), the MP binding region 1601-1607 features a remarkable superposition of peaks for P1601, L1603, Y1605 and M1606 (Figure 7). The neighboring Dis domain features similar agreement, especially for the importance of I1616. The region around 1618-1622 shows high importance in the experiment, but no significant interaction with the Dis domain in our data. Instead, the region is involved in self-interaction, when building the special hairpin motif observed (Figure 4(b)) and this could as well be impaired when mutated. This is followed by the VWF-A2 regions that bind to the inner surface of the Dis domain and the adjacent TSP1 module. Although no significant TSP1 exosite has yet been identified, the phage screening reveals a potential region between 1631 and 1640 that could significantly improve substrate recognition upon mutation (Figure 7). Contacts on TSP1 were the strongest between R409 and N1635, V1636 and E1638 ( Figure S19). Several weaker contacts appear from L428 to L1639, C411 to A1634, or Q410 to A1634 and N1635. Comparing the same data from Kretz et al. with our Model2 (Figure S20) or the overall data that was dominated by similar binding modes ( Figure S21), agreements with the data can as well be argued. Binding at the MP domain appears strongly reduced. However, this may be expected, as the final binding position is only obtained in its end state after transitioning to Model1. Direct interactions can now be seen for the hairpin region 1618-1622 ( Figure S20) and the importance of residues binding the Dis and Spacer domain is not notably off. In contrast, the TSP1 interactions have mostly disappeared, and the Cys-rich module is majorly complexed by regions of A2, that only have potential importance in the binding upon being mutated ( Figure S20, dashed). The A2 strand features a shift of roughly 5 amino acids towards the N-terminal at the Dis domain between both Models. Consistently, both models show a similar opposite shift of roughly 9 amino acids towards the C-terminal at the Cys-rich and Spacer modules. During a transition from Model2 to Model1, anchors between A2 and the Dis domain (D1622, H328) and at the Spacer domain (L1664, Y665) stay nearby and inter-domain motion compensates for most of the displacement (see Supporting Information, Movie1). The D1614 interaction moves from the calcium ion near the active site to R349 of Dis and the A2 region 1618-1622 coils into a hairpin structure. The A2 strand attaches to the inner side of the Dis domain and builds contacts at the TSP1 domain surface. In the course, the A2 strand is pulled towards the Dis module, and binding shifts largely at the Cys-rich and Spacer modules.

However, the shifts at the other domains that are accompanied by the differences in Dis binding, may also indicate a crucial role of a specific order in the recruitment of VWF-A2 across the different ADAMTS13 domains. Physiologically, the Spacer and Cys-rich domains may bind first, making an overall shift less likely to occur, compared to our simulation setup, where all domains were approaching simultaneously. Also, the specific state seen in Model1 might take time to build, and hence, they would raise in significance with increased simulation time.

Besides, we argue that the recruitment of L1603 is one of the first steps in binding MP and that the final pose is found by VWF-A2 shifting towards the C-terminal, pulling the strand through the gatekeepers and into the active side cleft. Due to the presence of strong shear forces during this process physiologically, binding shifts may generally not be uncommon.


Conclusion

We have investigated the complex interplay between ADAMTS13-MDTCS and VWF-A2 and provided the first molecular description of the proteolytic complex and insights into allosteric changes required for MP domain activation upon substrate binding. Two distinct binding modes were identified for which PDB files are provided. Model1, consistent with most available experimental data, shows the final proteolytic binding state prior to the cleavage reaction. Model2 likely represents a recruitment phase and is later transitioned to the proteolytic state.

A conformational change of MP after Dis binding was previously suggested to overcome local latency. Based on our results, we hypothesize a transition between a global recruitment-binding stage (Model2) and the final proteolytic state (Model1), which involves large-scale reconfiguration across the ADAMST13-MDTCS surface, most notably on the Cys-rich domain. This movement is accompanied by a striking conformational change of the gatekeeper amino acids. R193 reconfigures towards the calcium center adjacent to the active site cleft and is retained by T214 and E212. In addition, D252 is complexed with R257, opening an additional binding pocket between the gatekeeper loops that harbors the P3' residue T1608. We suggest adding R257 to the gatekeeper residues, making it a gatekeeper tetrad. In the final configuration, the VWF-A2 strand runs across the entire MDTCS surface, including interactions with the TSP1 domain. The final occupancy of binding pockets in the active site of MP by the VWF-A2 strand is extensively refined and contrasts with previously proposed assignments.

Data for the Cys-rich domain reveal the novel concept of a back side passage by VWF-A2 and an entirely different exosite region than formerly assumed. The previously proposed significance of A472-V474 could not be confirmed.

A new exosite region on the Spacer domain around I611 is confirmed and is harboring the unattended P1658 residue in a well-shaped pocket. Nearby, a novel prominent interaction between R636 and R1659 is identified.

The state ensembles of all simulations in this study are provided, including tools to filter for certain contacts and serve as a database of binding configurations and interactions. Upcoming studies are enabled to design experimental setups to test our proposals on various aspects of the complex binding mechanisms between the two proteins and allow to gain a deeper understanding of their biological interplay.
