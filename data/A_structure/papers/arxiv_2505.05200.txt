Semidefinite programming (SDP) provides a powerful relaxation for the maximum cut problem.For a graph with rational weights, the decision problem of whether the SDP relaxation for the maximum cut problem is exact is known to be N P -hard; however its complexity was unresolved for unweighted graphs.In this work, we extend the N Phardness result to unweighted graphs.We characterize a few classes of graphs for which the SDP relaxation is exact.For each of these graph classes, we establish conditions for uniqueness of the SDP optimum.We complement these findings by identifying two graph operations that preserve the solution rank, and in turn exactness.These results reveal how the SDP relaxation for the maximum cut problem can remain exact in arbitrarily large graphs, owing to the presence of a small structural core that governs exactness.We further address two open problems posed byMirka and Williamson (2024), by demonstrating that uniqueness of the maximum cut partition in exact relaxation does not imply uniqueness of the SDP optimum, and that exact relaxation with multiple optimal partitions may admit optimal SDP solutions lying outside the convex hull of rank-1 reference solutions.

Introduction

The Maximum-Cut (Max-Cut) problem asks the following: given a graph G = (V, E) with non-negative weights assigned to its edges, find an optimal partition of the vertex set V into two disjoint subsets such that the total weight of the edges crossing the partition (i.e., edges with endpoints in different subsets) is maximized.Over the years, this problem has generated immense interest.It is one of the first demonstrations of N P -hardness of computational problems [12] and the first optimization problem for which a semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxation was used to provide an algorithm with an approximation guarantee [9].Apart from its theoretical significance, the Max-Cut problem finds applications across various disciplines and industries including statistical physics [1], VLSI design [1] and machine learning [19].A semidefinite relaxation for the Max-Cut problem was introduced in [9,17], leading to a polynomial-time approximation algorithm.Given an input graph, the relaxation produces a positive semidefinite matrix as the optimal solution.A feasible cut can then be obtained by applying a hyperplane rounding technique to this matrix, achieving an expected value of at least α GW = 2 π min 0≤θ≤π θ 1 − cos θ ≈ arXiv:2505.05200v5[math.OC] 31 Oct 2025 0.87856 times the value of the maximum cut [9].This approximation ratio is optimal under the assumption of the Unique Games Conjecture and P ̸ = N P [13].The Max-Cut SDP relaxation is exact if and only if a rank-1 optimal primal solution exists.In practice, however, exactness is difficult to certify: the set of optimal solutions may contain higher-rank (rank > 1) solutions and solvers often return them.It is also possible that sub-optimal feasible solutions are recovered from hyperplane rounding even in cases where the relaxation is exact [11].From a theoretical standpoint, the study of conditions for exactness provides insight into the geometry of the feasible region (elliptope), revealing how combinatorial optima interact with convex relaxations.Understanding when higher-rank optima arise, and how uniqueness of solutions is preserved links Max-Cut to broader themes concerning the rank of optimal solutions in semidefinite relaxations.This motivates a systematic investigation of graph classes and structural principles that govern exactness and uniqueness in the Max-Cut SDP relaxation.


Overview and our contributions

The study of non-polyhedral relaxations for the Max-Cut problem was initiated by Svatopluk Poljak and collaborators.Mohar and Poljak [16] first proposed an eigenvalue-based upper bound, which was subsequently refined (referred to as the Delorme-Poljak bound) and evaluated on various graph families by Delorme and Poljak [4][5][6].Building on this line of work, Poljak and Rendl [17] introduced a semidefinite program to compute the Delorme-Poljak bound.Later, Goemans and Williamson [9] introduced an equivalent semidefinite relaxation (called the GW-relaxation) and established that its approximation guarantee is at least α GW using hyperplane rounding.

In this manuscript, we refer to the uniqueness of the optimal solution in the context of the primal problem (see Section 2.1); the dual optimal solution is always unique [5].We call a graph G (or a family of graphs) exact if the GW-relaxation is exact, or equivalently, if the Delorme-Poljak bound is tight.Although some exact graph families are known (summarized in Table 1), the exactness of the Max-Cut SDP relaxation is not fully understood.This manuscript addresses the following aspects of SDP exactness for the Max-Cut problem:

Relaxations for Max-Cut

The Max-Cut problem can be formulated as a quadratic integer program, which upon vector lifting yields the GW-relaxation.In its standard primal form, the relaxation is given by:
maximize z MAXCUT = 1 4 ⟨L G , X⟩ (MAXCUT SDP) subject to X ii = 1, ∀i ∈ V X ⪰ 0
Imposing rank(X) = 1 recovers the exact quadratic integer program.

MAXCUT SDP has the following dual:
minimize z MAXCUTDUAL = tr(Y) (MAXCUT DUAL SDP) subject to S = Y − 1 4 L G S ⪰ 0
The MAXCUT SDP satisfies strong duality [18], ensuring equality between its primal and dual optima, as well as complementary slackness.The GW-relaxation can be expressed in an equivalent eigenvalue form [17], often referred to as the Delorme-Poljak formulation.Let ϕ(G) denote its optimal value.ϕ(G) exhibits the following properties [4,5], which, by equivalence, are inherited by the
MAXCUT SDP optimum z * MAXCUT . -Upper bound: ϕ(G) ≥ MAXCUT(G).
-Scaling: If each weight w ij of G is multiplied by some positive real k, then ϕ(G) is also multiplied by k. -Monotonicity: If G and G ′ have the same vertices and the weight functions w and w ′ satisfy
w ij ≤ w ′ ij for all pairs of vertices, then ϕ(G) ≤ ϕ(G ′ ). -Graph Split: ϕ( G) = ϕ(G) for every split graph G of G.

Main Results

Delorme and Poljak (1993) [4] established that the decision problem of determining whether the Delorme-Poljak bound is tight (or equivalently whether the GW-relaxation is exact) for a graph with rational weights is N P -hard.However, their proof does not directly extend to unweighted graphs, and they noted that the complexity of recognizing exact unweighted graphs remains an open problem.To the best of our knowledge, no formal proof of the complexity for the unweighted case has been recorded in the literature.We provide a proof of N P -hardness for the unweighted case by an approach similar to that of Delorme-Poljak.Specifically, we construct a reduction from the following variant of the well-known Exact Sum Problem: given positive integers a 1 ≤ a 2 ≤ • • • ≤ a n , determine whether there exists a subset S ⊆ [n] such that i∈S a i = i / ∈S a i .The Exact Sum problem is N P -complete [12].We employ Lemma 1, which is also of independent interest, to extend the reduction to the unweighted case.

Lemma 1.Let G be any split graph of G.Then, G is exact if and only if G is exact.

Theorem 1.Given an unweighted graph G, the recognition problem: 'is G an exact graph' is N P -hard.

Split-decomposable graphs

In this section, we study another class of graphs, referred to as split-decomposable graphs, extending the framework developed in the preceding sections.

Definition 1 (Split-decomposable graph).A weighted graph G = (V, E, w) is called split-decomposable if there exists a split graph G of G with the following properties:

1. G contains a uniformly weighted spanning subgraph S ′ , where S ′ is isomorphic to a split graph of S, obtained by replacing each vertex of S by p ≥ 2 copies.Let w denote the uniform edge weight of S. 2. The residual edges of G, i.e., the edges in E( G)\E(S ′ ), are contained entirely within the vertex sets corresponding to the copies of a single vertex of S, and each such edge has weight at most w/p 2 .

The definition captures the idea that the structural core of G is governed by a uniform-weight S, referred to as the skeleton, which controls the main contribution to the maximum cut, while the residual edges are sufficiently light (bounded by w/p 2 ) to preserve exactness.Note that, by definition, G may be G itself.The central consequence of this setup, established in the next theorem, is that the exactness of any skeleton S guarantees the exactness of the entire graph G.

Proposition 5.If a split-decomposable graph G has a skeleton S such that S is exact, then G is exact.

Proof sketch.If S is exact, then S ′ is exact (Lemma 1) with ϕ(S ′ ) = ϕ(S).Defining S w p 2 and K p, w p 2 with uniform edge weights w/p 2 , Theorem 2 ensures that their lexicographic product is exact and attains the same value ϕ(S).Since S ′ is a spanning subgraph of this lexicographic product and G lies between these two extremes edgewise, monotonicity of ϕ implies G is exact.By Lemma 1, exactness transfers back to G.

Through Example 1, we wish to highlight that provided a decomposition of a graph G, the factorization can be utilized to sufficiently identify exactness of G.However, we do not propose a general algorithm for obtaining such a decomposition.The existence of such an algorithm which efficiently decompose a given graph G to determine its exactness remains an open direction for future research.Even though identifying exact graphs is N P -hard in general, our results on lexicographic product (Corollary 4) and split-decomposable graphs (Proposition 5) show that large graphs may admit a small structural core: in the former case, a small first factor G 1 , and in the latter case, a small skeleton.Such decomposition algorithms can thus still be highly effective.
