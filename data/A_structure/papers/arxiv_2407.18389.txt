In recent years, precision treatment strategy have gained significant attention in medical research, particularly for patient care.We propose a novel framework for estimating conditional average treatment effects (CATE) in time-to-event data with competing risks, using ICU patients with sepsis as an illustrative example.Our approach, based on cumulative incidence functions and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), achieves both asymptotic efficiency and double robustness.The primary contribution of this work lies in our derivation of the efficient influence function for the targeted causal parameter, CATE.We established the theoretical proofs for these properties, and subsequently confirmed them through simulations.Our TMLE framework is flexible, accommodating various regression and machine learning models, making it applicable in diverse scenarios.In order to identify variables contributing to treatment effect heterogeneity and to facilitate accurate estimation of CATE, we developed two distinct variable importance measures (VIMs).This work provides a powerful tool for optimizing personalized treatment strategies, furthering the pursuit of precision medicine.

Introduction

In the realm of medical research and patient care, the demand for precision medicine and personalized treatment approaches is on the rise.This need is particularly critical when dealing with life-threatening conditions such as sepsis in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, characterized by time-to-event outcomes in the presence of competing risks.To estimate the heterogeneous treatment effect across diverse patient populations and enable tailored interventions, we need a robust approach for estimating the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) for time-to-event data in the presence of competing risks.

The subdistribution hazards model by Fine and Gray (1999) is the most widely used method for analyzing time-to-event data in the presence of competing risks.To address issues of collapsibility (Gail et al., 1984), we focus on the estimand of differences in the cumulative incidence function (CIF) to infer causality.Unlike an alternative approach based on cause-specific hazards of all competing events, the subdistribution hazards model directly connects covariate effects to the CIF, simplifying the interpretation of covariates impacts on outcomes.Therefore, our targeted estimand becomes a preferred choice for estimating patient prognosis and predicting CIF (Lau et al., 2009;Koller et al., 2012;Austin and Fine, 2017).

Existing methods for estimating causal treatment effects in the presence of competing risks for time-to-event outcomes often fall short due to the challenges of adjusting for both censoring and confounding bias.Parametric methods, heavily reliant on distributional assumptions, are at great risk of bias due to misspecification.Nonparametric models for the outcome or treatment assignment can result in the slow convergence rates, and/or a lack of statistical inference.For these reasons, doubly robust estimators, based on semiparametric efficiency theory, have gained popularity.The estimators have been developed for binary and continuous outcome variables (Robins et al., 1994;van der Laan and Rubin, 2006;Kennedy et al., 2017).Doubly robust estimators provide consistent effect estimates when either the outcome model or the treatment model is correctly specified, and yield efficient estimates when both are correctly specified.Among these, targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), proposed by van der Laan and Rubin (2006), stands out for its advantageous properties in finite samples by the nature of being a substitution estimator, especially when the positivity assumption is nearly violated.For example, the CATE, defined as the difference between CIFs, should lie between −1 and 1, which could be ensured by TMLE but not by non-substitution estimators.TMLE has also been extended to survival outcomes, with additional work for adjusting for censoring (Moore and van der Laan, 2009;Cai and van der Laan, 2020;Benkeser et al., 2018;Rytgaard et al., 2023).However, while some studies within the TMLE framework have aimed at CIF estimation, they rely on estimating cause-specific hazards functions, where covariate effects do not provide direct interpretations on the effects of the CIF.Furthermore, these efforts generally focus on average treatment effects, providing limited guidance for treatment decisions across diverse patient population.

In this study, we propose a comprehensive framework that leverages TMLE to consistently estimate CATE in time-to-event data with competing risks.The primary contribution of this work lies in our derivation of the efficient influence function (EIF) for the target causal parameter, CATE, which quantifies the difference in potential outcomes defined by CIFs under distinct treatment conditions and baseline characteristics.Our proposed estimator posses doubly robustness and achieves asymptotic efficiency under mild conditions.We provide theoretical proofs and empirical validation through simulation studies.Leveraging the empirical variance of EIF, we construct confidence intervals and conduct hypothesis tests to identify overall treatment effects and treatment effect heterogeneity.Moreover, our flexible framework can incorporate various machine learning algorithms for modeling outcome and treatment mechanisms.This paper is organized as follows.In Section 2, we define the problem setting and notation for competing risks data, introducing the causal parameters of interest.Section 3 reviews the underlying theory and TMLE framework, illustrates the construction process and the properties of the proposed estimator.In Section 4, we demonstrate the practical performance and properties through simulations across scenarios and settings.In Section 5, the proposed method is applied to data from ICU patients with sepsis to estimate the CATE of steroids.

Finally, in Section 6, we provide a discussion of the findings.


Setting and Notation


Time-to-event data with competing risks

We consider a data setting that involves time-to-event outcomes in the presence of competing risks and encompasses two treatment arms.Let the observed data be denoted as
O i = X i , A i , T i , ∆ i N i=1
∼ i.i.d P for subjects i = 1, . . ., N , where X ∈ X ⊆ R p represents a vector of baseline covariates, A ∈ {0, 1} is a binary treatment assignment, T is the failure time, and C is the right-censoring time.P denotes the unknown true probability distribution of O, where our observed sample drawn from.Consider T = T ∧ C as the observed event time, ∆ ∈ {1, 2} as the event type, and ∆ = 1(T ⩽ C)∆ as the observed event type.Without loss of generality, let event 1 (∆ = 1) be the primary event of interest, and event 2 (∆ = 2) represent the competing events.The cumulative incidence function (CIF) for the primary event at time t 0 is defined as F 1 (t 0 ) = Pr(T ⩽ t 0 , ∆ = 1).Let T a and ∆ a be the potential event time and event type, respectively, had the subject been assigned treatment A = a (a = 0, 1).Then, the potential CIF for the primary event at time t 0 had the subject received treatment a is F a 1 (t 0 ) = Pr(T a ⩽ t 0 , ∆ a = 1).


Causal estimand

Our parameter of interest is the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) in prespecified subgroups by a set of predictive variables V ∈ V ⊂ X , where treatment effects differ among patients with various values of V .V are usually selected based on clinical knowledge, interests, or by a data-driven approach, e.g., by assessing the significance of interaction terms between treatment and candidate covariates through a preliminary full model of subdistribution hazards model, which include all covariates and their interactions with treatment.The CATE is the average treatment effect conditional on V = v m within each subgroup m (m = 1, 2, ..., M ), defined as the difference in potential CIF of main event:
Ψ t 0 ,m (P ) = F 1 1,m (t 0 |V = v m ) − F 0 1,m (t 0 |V = v m ) = E F 1 1 (t 0 |V = v m , L) − F 0 1 (t 0 |V = v m , L)|V = v m where F 1 1,m (t 0 |V = v m )
is the potential CIF had the subject received treatment a, possibly contrary to fact, conditional on the prognostic variables L ∈ L ⊆ X \V and predictive variables V = v m .When V = ∅, M = 1 and Ψ t 0 (P ) is the average treatment effect.

Within the potential outcome framework by Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), this parameter can be causally identified from observed data under the following assumptions: 1) Consistency: T = T a , C = C a and ∆ = ∆ a ; 2) No unmeasured confounder: T a , ∆ a ⊥ ⊥ A|V, L; 3) Positivity: 0 < Pr(A = 1|V, L) < 1 and Pr(C > t 0 |V, L, A) > 0 with probability 1; and 4) Independent censoring: T, ∆ ⊥ ⊥ C|V, L. In addition, we assume that the potential outcomes are not affected by the treatment assignments of other subjects, and only one version of the treatment (known as the stable unit treatment value assumption, SUTVA).The target causal parameter within each subgroup can be then identified as the following statistical parameter:
Ψ t 0 ,m (P ) = E {F 1 (t 0 |A = 1, L, V = v m ) − F 1 (t 0 |A = 0, L, V = v m )|V = v m } .(1)
For simplicity, the Ψ t 0 ,m (P ) is denoted as Ψ(P ), and conditioning on V = v m and subscript m are dropped:
Ψ(P ) = E {F 1 (t 0 |A = 1, L) − F 1 (t 0 |A = 0, L)} .
(2)


Targeted MLE Framework

We developed an estimation framework to construct the targeted estimator Ψ( P * n ) with doubly robust feature.This framework is based on the targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) introduced by van der Laan and Rubin (2006), along with methods extended to estimating average treatment effect for time-to-event outcomes (Rytgaard et al., 2023;Moore and van der Laan, 2009;Cai and van der Laan, 2020).When subjects are stratified according to a predetermined set of predictive covariates, our estimation procedure follows these steps:

1) Initial estimation: We begin by generating initial estimates for the nuisance parameters;

2) Targeting step: The estimators are updated iteratively to solve the corresponding efficient influence function (EIF) equations, thereby ensuring asymptotic properties; and

3) CATE estimation: Finally, we compute the CATE by averaging the updated estimates on the baseline covariates that are not used for stratification.We will delve into the details in the following subsections.The step-by-step procedure for estimating CATE is summarized in Supplementary Materials, Web Appendix A Algorithm 1.

Targeting step: iterative updates

To achieve asymptotic efficiency and doubly robust properties for the estimator, the key step in TMLE involves refining the initial estimates from Step 1 by deriving the EIF expression and solving the estimating equation.We will first explain the underlying theory and then illustrate the process of obtaining the asymptotically efficient and doubly robust estimator.

Variable importance

To investigate the key predictive variables contributing to treatment effect heterogeneity, as well as the importance of prognostic variables for treatment effect estimation within each subgroup, we calculate two variable importance measures (VIM) after constructing the estimator.

To evaluate the variable importance of predictive variables in terms of the contribution to the treatment effect heterogeneity, our VIM is calculated based on the variance of the estimated treatment effect, inspired by Hines et al. (2022).For j th predictive variable
(j = 1, 2, ..., d, d < p, recall that V ∈ V ⊂ X ⊆ R p ): VIM 1,j = var {τ (V i , L i )} − var {τ (V i,−j , L i )} var{τ (V i , L i )} , (13)
where τ (V i , L i ) is the estimated treatment effect of individual i using all predictive and prognostic variables, while τ (V i,−j , L i ) is the one with j th predictive variable excluded.We calculate VIM 1,j as the difference in variance of the estimated treatment effect when the variable is removed.

Additionally, to guide physicians in identifying critical variables for treatment effect estimation, we estimate VIM for each prognostic variable.This measure, akin to the one proposed by Pirracchio et al. (2018), evaluates the change in estimated CATE within each subgroup when one prognostic variable is omitted at a time:
VIM 2,k = Ψ( P * n ) − Ψ( P * n,−k ),(14)
where Ψ( P * n ) and Ψ( P * n,−k ) represent the estimated treatment effects using all variables and using all variables except the k th prognostic variable (k = 1, 2, ...q, q ⩽ (p − d), recall that L ∈ L ⊆ X \V), respectively.

Analysis of Sepsis Dataset


Data source and analysis

We analyzed electronic medical records data from 2012 to 2018, encompassing all ICU patients with sepsis in a healthcare system (N=60,886).We included 3,245 patients who met the eligibility criteria of a recent landmark clinical trial of steroids in septic shock, ADRENAL (Venkatesh et al., 2018), focusing on short-term steroid effects.Our primary aim was to assess the impact of steroids on reducing ICU mortality among various sepsis patients.To focus on short-term treatment effects and mitigate potential violations of the positivity assumption, we considered follow-up was capped at 10 days post-ICU admission, with discharge by this time considered a competing event.We also identified important biomarkers and indices for treatment strategy development.

Within the cohort, 885 patients (27.3%) died within 10 ICU days, with 274 (31.0%) receiving steroids.Meanwhile, 1,557 (48.0%) were discharged within the first 10 days, including 188 (12.1%) who received steroids.Treated patients had a median time to death of 1.83 days, compared to 2.79 days for untreated patients.

As presented in Web Table 1 in Supplementary Materials, we analyzed baseline characteristics of eligible patients, including demographics, comorbidities, clinical indices, and medication status.To identify potential predictive variables, a preliminary subdistribution hazards model was fitted with treatment and all covariates, revealing age, surgical history, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) as predictive variables with treatment effect heterogeneity.

As shown in Web Figure 3 in Supplementary Materials, cumulative incidence plots illustrated this heterogeneity, prompting us to categorize age (< 65 vs. ⩾ 65) and GCS (< 7 vs. 7 − 15).

Conditional on these three predictive variables, we considered gender, BMI, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and serum lactate for estimating treatment effects in each subgroup.R code for data analysis is available on Github as the link provided in Supplementary Materials.


Data analysis results

We estimated CATE using our proposed TMLE framework and compared them with Slearner.For the TMLE, we reported 95% CI based on the empirical EIF, while S-learner relied on bootstrapping.

In Figure 3, we present the results of our study regarding treatment effect heterogeneity, which provides valuable insights into distinct patient subgroups.Positive estimated CATEs indicate that administering steroids led to a higher cumulative incidence of death at day 7 compared to the placebo, and negative ones suggest potential reducing in incidence.As shown in the sixth and eighth row, patients who did not undergo surgery and had a GCS of 7 or higher (relative higher consciousness level) showed estimated CATEs significantly greater than 0, indicating that the use of steroids are not recommended for these patient subgroups.Among these people, those aged 65 or older showed an estimated CATE of 0.118 (95% CI: 0.018-0.217),while those younger than 65 years old had an estimated CATE of 0.173 (95% CI: 0.072-0.273).Similarly, for patients younger than 65 who met the criteria for undergoing surgery and had a GCS below 7, the estimated CATE was 0.144 (95% CI: 0.010-0.278),also indicating a significant increase in the cumulative incidence of death at day 7 with steroid treatment compared to the placebo.The use of steroids is not advised for this patient subgroup either.

However, for patients aged 65 or older with a GCS below 7 (relatively low consciousness level) who may have poorer health condition, regardless of surgery status, the estimated CATEs were negative, with the 95% CI including zero, suggesting no significant treatment effect.Although there is some uncertainty, it suggests the possibility of a beneficial effect of steroid treatment in these patient subgroups.Further investigations are needed to confirm potential benefits of steroids in this context.

The estimates obtained from our TMLE framework differed from S-learner's estimates in various groups.This discrepancy can be attributed to the absence of the doubly robust feature in the S-learner, and the uncertainty in whether the outcome, treatment, and censoring models were all correctly specified.

To understand the factors contributing to treatment effect heterogeneity and identify variables for accurate CATE estimation, we presented Variable Importance Measures (VIMs) for both predictive and prognostic variables within each subgroup.In Figure 5.2(A), we illustrate the variable importance of predictive variables, calculated using the difference in empirical treatment effect variance (VIM 1 from equation 13).Notably, GCS emerges as the most influential predictive variable, explaining the majority of variance in treatment effects.Omitting GCS from the analysis would reduce treatment effect variance by 63.7%.

Following GCS, age was a critical variable for detecting heterogeneous treatment effects.We also checked the contribution of prognostic variables to treatment effect heterogeneity and In Figure 5.2(B), we demonstrate the variable importance of prognostic variables, calculated using the differences in estimated treatment effects (VIM 2 in equation 14).For instance, in patients under 65 who underwent surgery and had a GCS < 7, omitting serum lactate would result in a 0.0513 difference in treatment effect estimates, a significant figure given our narrow treatment effect scale (−1 to 1).Serum lactate proves vital for estimating treatment effects in patients with lower consciousness levels (GCS < 7), while SOFA is more important for patients with relatively higher consciousness levels (GCS ⩾ 7).This underscores the importance of collecting these variables for different groups to obtain accurate CATE estimates.


Discussion

We developed a targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) framework for estimating conditional average treatment effects (CATE) in time-to-event data with competing risks.

Instead of using cause-specific hazards, our framework relies on the cumulative incidence function (CIF), which is based on subdistribution hazards.This approach facilitates a more straightforward interpretation of how covariates impact the CIF.Specifically, it allows clinical researchers to more easily understand and apply the findings to make informed treatment decisions.

Our proposed estimator exhibits a valuable doubly robust feature, ensuring the maintenance of consistent results even when the outcome model or both treatment and censoring mechanisms is misspecified.This feature has been rigorously demonstrated through both theoretical proofs and extensive simulations across diverse settings and scenarios.Using our TMLE framework for treatment decisions enhances strategy reliability, even when modeling specification are imperfect due to limited knowledge or data, which is the most cases in real-world problems.

We offer statistical inference with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests based on asymptotic linearity and normality properties, using estimated variances from the efficient influence function (EIF) we developed.Our TMLE framework is flexible, accommodating various regression and machine learning models for outcomes, treatment, and censoring mechanisms, making it applicable in diverse scenarios, including the high-dimensional data.

We explored the identification of predictive covariates and proposed two methods for ranking the importance of predictive and prognostic variables in accurately estimating CATE.

In our current research, we employed a two-stage approach: first, identifying predictive variables and subgroups based on these variables, and then estimating CATE for each subgroup.In our future research, we will focus on integrating these two stages into one.

When the data set is high-dimensional, LASSO does not significantly help with variable selection and modeling CIF, so we may need to incorporate more advanced machine learning techniques (e.g., deep neural network) to enhance CIF estimation, especially when it is challenging to correctly specify the outcome and treatment models concurrently while still maintaining the double robustness feature of CATE estimation.
