Liver cirrhosis has a higher incidence rate and mortality when complicated with bacterial infections.Concomitant bacterial infections, especially bacterial pneumonia, increase the susceptibility of cirrhotic patients to decompensation, with a higher likelihood of mortality.Bacterial pneumonia is often overlooked in patients with liver cirrhosis, although it can impact the clinical progress and outcomes.Untimely diagnosis and inappropriate antibiotic treatments are associated with poor prognosis and increased mortality.Current understanding of mechanisms and appropriate antibiotic treatments for cirrhosis-related bacterial pneumonia remain inadequate.Herein, we reviewed the epidemiology, clinical characteristics and treatment of pulmonary bacterial infections in cirrhotic patients to provide suggestions for clinical practice.

Introduction

][3] Complications of decompensated cirrhosis include ascites, gastroesophageal variceal hemorrhage, sepsis, hepatic encephalopathy and hepatorenal syndrome. 4Patients with liver cirrhosis are more susceptible to bacterial infections than the general population, 5 with 25%-50% of cirrhotic inpatients developing bacterial infections. 6][8][9] Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, urinary tract infections, bacterial pulmonary infections and soft tissue infections are the most common bacterial infections in patients with cirrhosis. 10Bacterial pneumonia is often atypical and may worsen liver diseases.Current understanding of cirrhosis and cirrhosis-related bacterial pneumonia remains inadequate.Therefore, this review focuses on the epidemiology, clinical characteristics and treatment of pulmonary bacterial infections in cirrhotic patients.


Epidemiology and characteristics of pulmonary bacterial infections in cirrhosis

][13][14][15][16][17][18][19] Bacterial pneumonia acquired outside of the hospital setting is termed community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), while bacterial pneumonia acquired after at least 48 hours of hospitalization is defined as hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) or nosocomial pneumonia. 20Most cases of bacterial pneumonia are CAP (48%), while the proportion of HAP is 26%. 11,21


CAP

Overall, positive culture results are observed in 17%-28% of patients with liver cirrhosis accompanied by pulmonary bacterial infections, with gram-positive cocci (GPC) the predominant causative agents of CAP in liver cirrhosis (75%), 6,11,22 while approximately 4.8% of patients show negative culture results, even with positive imaging. 5Table 1 lists the pathogens in CAP and HAP among cirrhotic patients.Among gram-positive bacteria, the most commonly isolated are Streptococcus pneumoniae, followed by Staphylococcus aureus and other Streptococcus species. 11,23Haemophilus influenzae is the most common detected gram-negative bacteria (GNB), followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. 24


HAP

HAP is mainly caused by GNB, or GNB plus GPC coinfections, such as gram-negative bacilli plus staphylococci. 25The isolated bacteria include P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, including methicillinsensitive S. aureus and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, S. pneumoniae, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia and Enterobacter sp. 13


Influence of bacterial pneumonia on cirrhosis

Pneumonia is known to be associated with poor prognosis, higher morbidity and higher mortality in cirrhosis, 14,15 as listed in Tables 2. Pneumonia has been related with increased incidences of sepsis (OR, 3.78; 95% CI, 3.70 -3.85) and organ failure (OR, 2.87; 95% CI, 2.81 -2.93), especially multiple-organ failure (OR, Review Article 3.59; 95% CI, 3.50 -3.68) in cirrhotic patients. 16Pneumonia and sepsis were demonstrated to be independent predictors for 28-day mortality in cirrhotic patients with infectious complications. 26Among cirrhotic patients with CAP, bivariate logistic regression analysis showed that multilobar pneumonia and septic shock at admission were significantly correlated to 30-day mortality and ICU admission. 23Moreover, cirrhotic patients with ascites had the highest mortality risk from pneumonia for overall 30-day mortality (HR, 2.95; 95% CI, 2.05-4.25)and 90-day mortality (HR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.93-3.42) 27compared with other infections.In addition, Díaz-Hernández et al. 17 found that pneumonia was an independent predictor for increased mortality at 30, 60 and 365 days of follow-up.

Pneumonia caused by MDR bacteria has been associated with prolonged hospital stay and elevated mortality in cirrhotic patients. 19,28,29Cox regression analysis showed that mortality was higher in patients infected by MDR bacteria (OR, 4.45; 95% CI, 1.131-10.528). 19Similarly, Gupta et al. 18 found that MDR infection was an independent predictor for 28-day mortality in acute decompensation of cirrhosis (HR, 1.263; 95% CI, 1.07 -2.19; P < 0.007).Furthermore, sepsis (54.9% vs 13%) and lethal sepsis are significantly more frequent in patients with carbapenem-resistant GNB infections. 30Zhao et al. 28 found that among cirrhotic patients infected with MDR bacteria, increased 28-day mortality was mainly attributed to MRSA (HR, 2.964; 95% CI, 1.175-7.478;P = 0.021).Another study showed that cirrhotic patients with MDR bacterial infections had increased model of end-stage liver disease (MELD) scores (12.6 ± 6.1vs 8.7 ± 2.4, P = 0.05) and Child-Pugh scores (41 vs 12, P < 0.001) compared with patients with non-MDR bacterial infections. 19MELD-Na scores (24.6 ± 4.8 vs 19.2 ± 5.3, P < 0.001) and chronic liver failure consortium ACLF scores (49.6 ± 4 vs 41.7 ± 3.4, P < 0.001) were also significantly higher in patients with MDR bacterial infections, and MDR bacteremia was more common in patients with more severe liver disease ( P = 0.008). 31ompared to pneumonia patients without cirrhosis, pneumonia patients with cirrhosis are usually more susceptible to bacteremia and often have higher pneumonia severity index scores. 23The MELD score was demonstrated as an independent predictor for severe bacterial infections (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.04-1.42;P = 0.01), and it is therefore a useful indicator for predicting mortality and ICU admission. 23


Mechanisms of bacterial pneumonia in patients with liver cirrhosis

Patients with decompensated cirrhosis are highly susceptible to bacterial pneumonia due to cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction/deficiency (CAID) involving innate and adaptive immune cells, as well as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), cytokines, hormones and metabolites that regulate immune cell differentiation.Moreover, changes in gut microbiota and intestinal immune disruption can lead to bacterial infections.


CAID

3][34] Portal-systemic shunting, impairment of the hepatic reticuloendothelial system, sinusoidal fibrosis and capillarization, and Kupffer cell loss or damage synergistically affect the hepatic immune function, affecting monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, T and B lymphocytes, and circulating NK cells, which attenuate the responses to bacterial challenge, increasing susceptibility to bacterial infections. 12mpaired neutrophil migration leads to increased migration of certain immunoregulatory monocytes, such as CD16 + cells, human leukocyte antigen DR-positive cells/Mer receptor tyrosine kinase-positive cells with the impaired ability of circulating monocytes and neutrophils to phagocytose pathogens, thereby reducing the killing potential of neutrophils and their production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). 35,36Increased basal neutrophil ROS production in some decompensated patients may deplete neutrophil energy and impair their ability to produce antimicrobial ROS responses, and increased expression of intercellular adhesion molecules by endothelial cells and neutrophils (CD62L or CD11b) may lead to hyperadhesion and subsequent defective transmigration of neutrophils and thus increase the susceptibility to bacterial infections. 36Gut-associated lymphoid tissue is damaged in cirrhosis, with a persistent activated state of monocytes, dendritic cells and T lymphocytes in the intestine and mesenteric lymph nodes, which leads to intestinal inflammation and impairment of the intestinal  barrier, with increased expression of proinflammatory/anti-inflammatory cytokines at the lamina propria, mucosal epithelium and mesenteric lymph nodes, and by phagocytic cells. 32,33sfunction of the intestinal barrier Bile acids are synthesized in the liver, stored in the gallbladder and delivered to the small intestine where they are metabolized by the gut microbiota.Bile acids interact with the nuclear farnesoid X receptor, which serves as a regulator of bile acids by ligand binding, and the Takeda G protein-coupled receptor, [37][38][39] to regulate metabolic functions and modulate the gut microbiota.Secondary bile acids, such as deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid, are formed by bacterial deconjugation and dihydroxylation and reabsorbed via enterohepatic circulation in the gut. 38,39Through farnesoid X receptor signaling, bile acids regulate gut bacteria and maintain intestinal barrier function. 40[39][40][41]


Bacterial translocation

Dysbiosis of intestinal bacteria in cirrhosis includes overgrowth by potentially pathogenic phyla (such as Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae) and a decrease in beneficial phyla. 42Impaired liver reticuloendothelial system, increased gut mucosal permeability and decreased tight junction proteins, accompanied with portosystemic collaterals, lead to translocation of bacteria and PAMPs from the intestinal cavity to the mesenteric lymph nodes and intestine-related lymphatic tissue, resulting in subsequent invasion of the lungs through circulatory blood flow and activation of proinflammatory signaling pathways. 43Entry of bacterial toxins to the portal or systemic circulation induces hepatic cell death 44 and lung injury.DAMPs from chronically damaged or dead liver cells accompanied with PAMPs can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors, leading to persistent systemic inflammation in cirrhotic patients. 45Increased proinflammatory responses (such as tumor necrosis factor, neurotransmitters, norepinephrine and ROS) 34 caused by bacteremia can lead to tissue damage, 14,32 disruptions of the structural and functional integrity of intestinal tight junctions, and increased paracellular permeability, 34 which may promote bacterial infections.The gut-microbiota-lung interaction can be a major factor in pulmonary bacterial infections.Moreover, the dysbiosis of intestinal bacteria induces PAMPs to activate innate immune responses and the Kupffer cells by binding to toll-like receptors. 38,46ntestinal barrier disruption leads to translocation of bacterial products, such as lipopolysaccharide and bacterial DNA, and viable bacteria to the systemic circulation, which is accompanied by systemic inflammation, immunoparesis and bacterial infections. 35,36


External factors

Common external factors associated with pulmonary infections include medications (such as glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants, and acid-suppressive agents), malnutrition, alcohol intake, and repeated hospitalizations and invasive procedures.Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) may lead to overgrowth and translocation of intestinal microflora, which is associated with bacterial pneumonia and other infectious diseases in patients with liver cirrhosis. 12,34In addition, acute lung injury associated with transfusion of blood and blood products can affect lung clearance rates and increase the risk of pneumonia. 27irrhosis-associated immune dysfunction, increased portal hypertension, dysbiosis of intestinal microbiota, reduced bile acid flow, impairment of intestinal mucosal barrier function and intestinal hyperpermeability may lead to translocation of intestinal bacteria into the bloodstream, lungs and other organs, [44][45][46][47][48][49] increasing the susceptibility to bacterial infectious diseases such as pneumonia (Figure 1). 50


Antibiotic treatment for bacterial pneumonia in cirrhosis

Timely antibiotic treatment is critical.For pneumonia in cirrhotic patients, even during sepsis or septic shock, the recommendations for antibiotic therapy are similar to what is recommended for the general population, thus following the international guidelines. 51If the pathogen cannot be identified, empirical treatment should be administered as soon as possible. 52,53Appropriate empiric antibiotic therapy is the most important maneuver to improve the prognosis of liver cirrhosis, 54 and it is an independent predictor for improved in-hospital survival (subdistribution HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.36-0.76;P = 001) and 28-day survival (subdistribution HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.42-0.84;P = 005). 11Empirical antimicrobial treatment should cover all bacteria that may lead to infection. 25,48,51he local epidemiology and likelihood of MDR bacteria should also be considered. 11Prolonged, delayed or inadequate antimicrobial treatment increases the risk of MDR or extensively drug-resistant bacteria. 48,51The treatment protocols should cover common healthcare-associated and nosocomial microorganisms for patients with a high risk of MDR infections. 45Once cultures are obtained, narrow-spectrum antibiotics should be administered as soon as possible based on the culture results of microorganisms to reduce the incidence of antibiotic resistance.Initial antibiotics should also be changed in case of antimicrobial resistance and treatment failures. 45,51


Single infections

Most cirrhotic patients (86.0%) have infections by only a single microorganism. 16Atypical pathogens should be covered in CAP treatment. 55Long-term prophylactic use of quinolones is not recommended. 55Recommended antibiotic regimens for CAP in cirrhosis are piperacillin-tazobactam or third-generation cephalosporins (eg, ceftriaxone) plus macrolide, levofloxacin or moxifoxacin. 11,48-Lactam antibiotics such as piperacillin/tazobactam and cefoperazone/ sulbactam are often used for nosocomial infection. 55Carbapenems are the antibiotic of choice in patients with risk of extendedspectrum β-lactamase-producing bacterial infections.Meropenem is the recommended carbapenem antibiotic in cirrhotic patients accompanied by hypoalbuminemia.In patients with organ failure, antibiotics with activity against oxacillin-resistant cocci are recommended. 54iperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem-ceftazidime plus ciprofloxacin ± glycopeptide should be used in patients at risk of MRSA infections. 11,48iperacillin/tazobactam or carbapenems plus a respiratory quinolone should be used against Pseudomonas species. 48Carbapenems or ceftazidime plus levofloxacin plus a glycopeptide should be applied for HAP.The third-generation cephalosporins are recommended as empirical treatment in countries with low prevalence of resistance according to the 2018 European Association for Study of Liver guidelines. 56iperacillin/tazobactam is recommended for hypoepidemic areas of MDR bacteria, while carbapenems are recommended for hyperendemic areas of extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing bacteria. 57


Multiple infections

Concurrent infections have been associated with worse outcomes and increased mortality. 45,58Patients with Clostridium difficile infection, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and pneumonia have relatively higher rates of mixed infections.Cefotaxime or ceftriaxone, penicillin, meropenem, imipenem, levofloxacin, piperacillin/tazobactam and vancomycin are recommended. 59Combination of two antibiotics such as carbapenem and glycopeptide or tigecycline is recommended for nosocomial infections with systemic inflammatory response syndrome or sepsis in accordance with local antibiotic susceptibility. 59The duration of antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated HAP is 7 days.Prolonged antibiotic treatment has not been shown to decrease rates of recurrent pneumonia, treatment failure, duration of mechanical ventilation, length of stay in hospital or mortality. 57For cirrhotic patients with severe sepsis, the duration of empirical antibiotic treatment should not be longer than 5 days; however, the total therapy duration should be 7-10 days, and a longer duration is often recommended. 60HAPassociated complications, including empyema or bacteremia, require longer durations of antibiotic treatment.Pneumonia due to Pseudomonas sp. or Acinetobacter sp. is considered a complex infection that requires at least 14 days of antibiotic treatment to avoid relapse. 57


Conclusions

Patients with liver cirrhosis have an increased risk of pulmonary bacterial infections.Bacterial pneumonia frequently leads to decompensation in the cirrhotic patients, influencing the outcome.It is also the most common factor in the development of ACLF.Early diagnosis of pulmonary infections and timely management with appropriate treatment are the best strategies to reduce the mortality of patients with liver cirrhosis.In addition to the traditional treatment strategies against bacterial infections, new therapeutic approaches should be investigated to avoid PPI-induced infections, maintain intestinal microbiota balance, provide better management of liver disease and alleviate decompensation.
