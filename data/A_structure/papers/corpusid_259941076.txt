Woodland birds and insect decline
Received 18 May 2023 Accepted 3 July 2023Birdsinsectsdeclineswoodlands
The decline of woodland birds around the world is well known. There are likely to be many causal factors acting together and interacting synergistically, such as habitat fragmentation and the invasion of exotic species. Similarly, insect declines, which likely have been occurring for some time in Australia, have been recorded around the world in recent years, especially in areas of intensive agriculture or urbanisation. Because a large proportion of woodland birds are insectivorous, we ask whether the loss of food resources could also be a driver of bird declines. We encourage more research into this.ARTICLE HISTORY

The decline of woodland birds in temperate southeastern Australia and indeed other parts of the world during the past few decades is well known (Recher et al. 1996;Zanette et al. 2000;Bennett and Watson 2011;Watson 2011;Razeng and Watson 2012 and references therein). The drivers are likely to be multifaceted and complex, including loss and fragmentation of habitat, urbanisation (in larger cities), invasive species (weeds and feral animals), ecosystem dysfunction (such as tree death), firewood removal, pesticide use and climate change among others, as well as the synergistic interaction of these factors. Here, we focus on the need for ornithologists to work more with entomologists by asking to what extent is the loss of food, such as insects, contributing to this decline? Several threatened woodland birds in south-eastern Australia mainly feed on arthropods (insects, spiders and allied invertebrates) (e.g. Speckled Warbler Pyrrholaemus sagittatus, Brown Treecreeper Climacteris picumnus, Scarlet Robin Petroica boodang, Hooded Robin Melanodryas cucullata), whereas for other species insects are an important component of their diet (e.g. Southern Whiteface Aphelocephala leucopsis, Regent Honeyeater Anthochaera phrygia). Given ongoing global attention on insect decline in recent years (e.g. Wyckhuys 2019, 2021;Wagner 2020;Wagner et al. 2021;Outhwaite et al. 2022;Rigal et al. 2023 and references therein) we urge ornithologists to not just determine the kinds of insects eaten but to become actively involved in measuring whether shortfalls of insect food are a key driver contributing to the decline of woodland birds.

A possible connection between changes in woodland bird abundance and insect abundance raises a more general and obvious question for ornithologists and entomologists alike, and that is to what extent are insects declining in Australia? Several recent global reviews have documented declines in insect abundance, diversity and biomass (e.g. Wyckhuys 2019, 2021;van Klink et al. 2020;Wagner 2020). Yet nearly all of the relevant studies are based in the Northern Hemisphere, particularly Europe and North America, where long-term monitoring programmes of insect populations, especially butterflies, are well established. The situation in Australia is vastly different. We simply do not have rigorous, long-term monitoring programmes spanning more than a decade designed to detect changes in terrestrial insect richness or abundance. Thus, without a solid baseline against which one can compare the past with the present situation, the magnitude, rate and geographical extent of insect declines in Australia and their causative role in bird declines are not well understood (Braby 2019;Braby et al. 2021).

Our best approach is to use alternative methods, such as extrapolating from what we know of insect extinction, opinions or anecdotal reports by experts, historical records from museum collections and the scientific literature, or studies that have been repeated in time (the snapshot effect). Collating data using these approaches, we see that it is not that difficult to demonstrate that, at least for some of the better-known species, distributions have shrunk and populations have declined or have been extirpated in historical times (>40 years ago) or in the recent past (10-40 years ago). In addition, a few species are known to have become extinct (Woinarski et al. 2019), although we note that the majority of insect extinctions are likely unrecorded because most species are either too poorly known or yet to be discovered and described! Until recently, it has been difficult to demonstrate trends, especially decline, in say the past 10 or 20 years due to a lack of published data. However, in a recent symposium published in Austral Entomology (Volume 60, Issue 1; see Braby et al. 2021) reporting temporal changes in Australian insect populations and others dealing with important conservation issues, four studies identified rapid declines in insect abundance in several butterflies, Bogong Moth Agrotis infusa, Green Carpenter Bee Xylocopa aerata and the Banksia Montane Mealybug Pseudococcus markharveyi in the immediate past (1-10 years) at small spatial scales (e.g. Green et al. 2021;Moir 2021). Another study by Debus et al. (2020), examining the diet of the Australian Hobby Falco longipennis, found a marked decline in the number of insect prey items (from 26% to <1%), of which the majority were Red-eye Cicadas Psaltoda moerens and Christmas Beetles Anolognathus spp, over an 8-year period during the Millennium Drought. All of these recent studies are based on a range of evidence (analysis of historical evidence and expert opinions, survey replication, and monitoring) gathered from across the southern parts of the continent and, collectively, suggest that, at least in temperate areas, the changes are probably widespread and far more severe than previously realised. Thus, declines of Australian insects are likely occurring in parts of Australia that have experienced the greatest agricultural intensification. These are areas such as the Murray-Darling Basin, the wheatbelts of southern Australia, the northern Tablelands of New South Wales, the peri-urban areas around our major cities, and isolated patches of natural bushland such as on islands and mountain tops. All of these have experienced substantial changes in avifaunal composition too (e.g. Jenner et al. 2011).

The causative drivers of these species' declines during the past decade are varied but severe drought and inappropriate fire regimes (frequent and intense bushfiresmega-fires) as a direct consequence of climate change are likely to be key (Marsh et al. 2021;Gibb et al. 2023). Other factors such as habitat loss and fragmentation from agricultural expansion, dieback and urbanisation have also been implicated. Agricultural intensification and climate change seem to be particularly dire for herbivorous insects reliant on plants (Raven and Wagner 2021). The net result of lower rainfall, especially a reduction in autumn rains following hot summers, prolonged droughts and high temperatures (heat waves) during the past two decades, is water stress or deficit, which leads to desiccation and reduced quality or palatability of larval host plants. Dry autumns lead to diminished recharge of the subsoil before the onset of the winter cold period, which may be critical for the survival, growth (and palatability) of host plants for many insect larvae. Adverse implications for springbreeding bird populations are obvious. It is not yet known whether the recent three successive La Niña events have had positive or adverse effects on insect abundances. Some species (e.g. temperate satyrine butterflies, which utilise grasses as their larval host plants) have clearly benefited in the short term as extraordinary numbers have been seen in south-eastern Australia during the past three seasons (2020)(2021)(2022)(2023).

The influence of pollution from synthetic pesticides (insecticides, herbicides and fungicides) and fertilisers as a result of intensive agriculture in rural landscapes is not well known in Australia. Extrapolating from the review of Sánchez-Bayo and Wyckhuys (2019) we see it as likely another key driver. It would seem reasonable to conclude that Australian insects are not immune to the effects of increased pesticide and fertiliser application, together with the gradual destruction of non-crop areas in these landscapes that harbour many beneficial insects, such as pollinators and predators. Again, the potential follow-on effects to bird populations are obvious.

Although we do not know the extent, magnitude and rate at which insects are declining in the woodland habitats of south-eastern Australia, decline is likely to be happening. We urge ornithologists and entomologists to develop more collaborations (e.g. Majer et al. 2002;Razeng and Watson 2012) so that we can better quantify the impact of insect food availability to threatened insectivorous woodland birds. Regardless of the extent of insect decline, every effort should be made to mitigate the effects of climate change, loss and fragmentation of habitats (including roadside verges), firewood removal, invasive species and inappropriate use of insecticides.
