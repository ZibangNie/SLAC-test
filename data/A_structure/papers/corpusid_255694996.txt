Adsorbate-driven dynamic active sites in stannosilicate zeolites
7 January 20237575A5AC0D7628260CC5D79DC2A516FB10.1016/j.fmre.2022.12.015Received 15 September 2022 Received in revised form 16 November 2022 Accepted 11 December 2022Dynamic active sites Pseudo-Brønsted acid Stannosilicate zeolites Reaction conditions Solid-state NMR
Elucidating the nature of the active sites in heterogeneous catalysts is fundamental for understanding their reactivity and catalytic performances.Although stannosilicate zeolites have tremendous application potential for catalyzing biomass-related compounds in aqueous media, the detailed local structures and features of the real active sites and their possible structural variations under reaction conditions are poorly understood to date.In this study, a dynamic transformation of framework Sn− O− Si sites to Sn− OH/Si− OH pairs and subsequently a pseudo-Brønsted acid in stannosilicate zeolites upon molecular adsorption, which is analogous to various adsorbates/reactants under working conditions, was identified by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy for the first time, which challenges the widespread assumption that the active center structures remain rigid/stable during the catalytic process.These results provide new comprehensive insights for the fundamental understanding of the dynamic and flexible active centers and involved reaction mechanisms of novel zeolite catalysts with heterometal atoms, such as Sn, Ti, and Zr.

Introduction

Understanding the detailed structures and features of active sites and their possible structural variations during catalytic reactions is a crucial step in gaining fundamental insights for improving catalyst efficiency [1][2][3][4][5][6] .As typical heterogeneous catalytic materials, zeolites have been used extensively in chemical and petrochemical industries [7][8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15] , and conventional characterization of their active site structures is normally performed in water-free environments.However, the adsorption of trace water vapor or reactant molecules on active sites is inevitable during the real "working " catalytic process, which may significantly alter the local structures and catalytic properties of the active centers [16][17][18][19][20] .Therefore, to accurately determine the structure-activity relationships of real catalytic systems, an in-depth understanding of the active site structures and variations induced by different adsorbates, such as water and reactants, is crucial and urgently required.

The discovery of zeolites with framework Si partially substituted by heterometal atoms, such as Sn, Ti, and Zr, represents a significant breakthrough in the application of heterogeneous catalysts in aqueous media [ 21 , 22 ].For example, owing to their unrivaled catalytic performance in the conversion of biomass-related compounds in water-containing media, stannosilicate zeolites have been demonstrated to be excellent catalysts for sugar isomerization [ 22 , 23 ], aldol condensation [ 24 , 25 ], Meerwein-Ponndor-Verley reduction [26] , Baeyer-Villiger oxidation [ 21 , 27 ], and several other important processes [28] .The tremendous application potential of stannosilicate zeolites has continually attracted considerable attention from industry and academia for characterizing the detailed local fine structures and features of various active centers and their structural variations in water/reactant-containing media under "working " conditions based on experimental and theoretical investigations.

It is recognized that isomorphously substituted framework Sn sites are the catalytically active centers in stannosilicate zeolites, and the hydrophobicity and moderate Lewis acidity of the tetravalent Sn atoms are responsible for their unique high reactivity [29][30][31][32][33][34] .For example, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy combined with acetonitrile adsorption demonstrated the existence of two types of framework Sn sites, that is, the "open " (SiO) 3 Sn− OH and "closed " (SiO) 4 Sn sites with distinct Lewis acid strengths [35][36][37][38][39] .Further catalytic experiments demonstrated that the "open " and "closed " Sn sites have different catalytic performances in various reactions [ 40 , 41 ].Notably, the isomorphous substitution of tetracoordinated Si with tetravalent Sn does https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fmre.2022.12.015 2667-3258/© 2023 The Authors.Publishing Services by Elsevier B.V. on behalf of KeAi Communications Co. Ltd.This is an open access article under the CC BY-NC-ND license ( http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ ) not induce a negative charge, and thus does not introduce additional Brønsted acidic protons into the framework of stannosilicate zeolites.However, their Brønsted acidity has been experimentally demonstrated to play a crucial role in many important processes, such as dihydroxyacetone dehydration to methyl lactate [22] , as also confirmed by our catalytic experiments in Fig. S1 (Supporting Information).Although this unusual phenomenon has been preliminarily explained recently [34] , the nature and local structure of Brønsted acid sites in stannosilicate zeolites remains poorly understood to date.

Thus, the objective of this study is to comprehensively elucidate the nature and dynamic evolution process of the active sites in stannosilicate zeolites upon molecular bombardment analogous to various adsorbates/reactants under reaction conditions using one-and twodimensional (2D) solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic methods.A pseudo-Brønsted acid site induced by additional adsorbates is clearly identified for the first time, which is essential for providing novel in-depth insights into the fundamental aspects of stannosilicate zeolite catalysis.


Experimental methods


Sample preparation

The Sn-MFI zeolite (Si/Sn ≈ 60) used herein was synthesized according to procedures described in previous work. [1]The H-form Al-MFI zeolite sample was obtained from the parent NH 4 -MFI zeolite (Zeolyst International, cat.no.CBV 8014; Si/Al = 40).To obtain H-form Al-MFI zeolite, the NH 4 -MFI sample was carefully deaminated by gradual heating the tube furnace in dry air at a temperature rate of 1 K/min.The sample was firstly dried at 383 K for 2 h, then calcinated at 773 K for 6 h.

Prior to the adsorption of various guest molecules, each sample was placed in a home-made glass tube and then connected to a vacuum line for dehydration treatment.Typically, this was performed by gradually increasing the temperature from room temperature to the final target temperature (673 K) at a rate of 1 K/min.The sample was then kept at 673 K under a pressure of < 10 − 3 Pa for at least 10 h and then cooled.After the sample was finally cooled to ambient temperature, a saturated amount of volatile probe molecule was introduced into the activated sample in a liquid N 2 bath.Upon reaching an equilibrium adsorption for at least 1 h, the sample was evacuated for another 1 h on the vacuum manifold to remove the excessive physisorbed probe molecules before the sample tube was finally flame-sealed.The detailed adsorption procedures of various guest NMR probe molecules can be found in our previous work.It should be noted that, for the adsorption of different loadings of H 2 O molecules ( i.e. , 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 kPa), the evacuation treatment is inhibited.The co-adsorption of trimethylphosphine (TMP) and H 2 O onto Sn-MFI zeolite was achieved by the following procedure: First, a saturated amount of TMP was introduced into the dehydrated sample following the aforementioned approach, then the co-adsorption of various loadings of H 2 O molecules onto TMP-loaded Sn-MFI zeolites was performed.Prior to NMR experiments, the sample in the sealed glass tube was transferred into a ZrO 2 rotor under a dry nitrogen atmosphere in a glovebox and then sealed with a Kel-F end-cap.


Solid-state NMR experiments

All the solid-state NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker AVANCE-III 500 MHz spectrometer operating at a Larmor frequency of 500.57,202.63, 130.44, 125.89, and 99.44 MHz for the 1 H, 31 P, 27 Al, 13 C, and 29 Si nucleus, respectively.The solid-state 1 H, 31 P, 27 Al, and 13 C NMR spectra were recorded using a 4 mm magic-angle-spinning (MAS) probe operating at a spinning rate of 12 kHz.A single-pulse sequence with a /2 pulse length of 4.0 s, a recycle delay of 5 s, and 64 scans was used for the 1 H MAS NMR experiments. 27Al MAS NMR spectrum was recorded using a small-flip-angle technique with a pulse length of 0.26 s ( /12), a recycle delay of 1 s, and 2048 scans.One-dimensional (1D) 31 P MAS NMR spectra with high power proton decoupling were recorded with a /2 pulse length of 3.2 s, a recycle delay of 15 s, and 256 scans.For the 1D 1 H→ 31 P (or 13 C) cross-polarization (CP)/MAS NMR measurements, the Hartmann-Hahn condition was achieved with a contact time of 4 ms and a recycle delay of 3 s.Two-dimensional (2D) 1 H− 31 P heteronuclear correlation (HETCOR) [ 3 ] experiments were performed with varying CP contact times (0.1-4 ms) and a recycle delay of 2 s, whereas the 2D 1 H− 13 C HETCOR experiments were carried out with a fixed contact time of 4 ms and a recycle delay of 2 s.The 2D 1 H− 31 P and 1 H− 13 C HETCOR data sets consisted of 128 t 1 × 256 t 2 points.The standard 2D 1 H− 1 H SQ correlation pulse sequence [ 4,5 ] was used to explore spatial interactions between Sn− OH and Si− OH groups in Sn-MFI zeolite, and the 2D 1 H− 1 H SQ NMR spectra were recorded with a mixing time of 50 ms and a recycle delay of 2 s.The increment interval in the indirect dimension was 80 s.Typically, 128 scans were acquired for each t 1 increment, and the 2D data sets consisted of 128 t 1 × 128 t 2 points.The 1D 29 Si MAS NMR experiments were conducted with high power proton decoupling using a /2 pulse length of 3.9 s and a recycle delay of 60 s on a 7 mm MAS probe with a spinning rate of 5 kHz.The 1D 1 H→ 29 Si CP/MAS NMR measurements were performed with a contact time of 4 ms and a recycle delay of 3 s.The chemical shifts of 1 H, 31 P, 27 Al, 13 C, and 29 Si nucleus were externally referenced to adamantane (1.91 ppm), (NH 4 ) 2 HPO 4 (1 ppm), 1 M aqueous Al(NO 3 ) 3 , hexamethylbenzene, and kaolinite (− 91.5 ppm), respectively.


Other Characterization Methods

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) pattern of the synthesized Sn-MFI zeolite under study was performed on a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with Cu K  radiation (  = 1.5418Å) in the 2  range of 5 to 35°.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of synthesized Sn-MFI zeolite were measured on a JEM-2100 microscope.STEM images were detected by a High Angle Annular Dark Field (HAADF) detector on Tecnai F20 G2 S-Twin microscope.In a typical procedure, very few sample was first dispersed in the C 2 H 5 OH solution, and then a drop of this mixture was draw and added in the Cu-grid.After the evaporation of C 2 H 5 OH, the sample was put into the chamber of TEM spectroscopy with acceleration voltage of 200 kV.

Diffuse reflectance ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrum of the dehydrated Sn-MFI sample was measured in the range of 200-500 nm with a PerkinElmer Lambda 750 UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer.

Infrared spectroscopy with pyridine (Py) adsorption was carried out using an EQUINOX55 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) (Bruker Corp.) with a resolution of 4 cm − 1 .The samples were ground to fine powders and pressed into a thin self-supporting wafer with a weight of 10 mg and a diameter of 10 mm.Before the pyridine adsorption, the samples were evacuated at 673 K for 1 h.Subsequently, the samples were exposed to pyridine for 10 min for adsorption, then the pyridineloaded samples were desorbed under vacuum at different temperatures, and the corresponding spectra were respectively recorded.


Catalytic Test

0.1 g DHA (40 wt% aqueous solution) was mixed with 8.0 g methanol in a 15 mL glass-walled vial.An appropriate amount of catalyst was added to this mixture and the reaction was carried out with magnetic stirring in an oil bath heated at 353 K for a while.Then, the reaction was quenched using an ice bath, and the catalyst was separated by centrifugation.ML were analyzed on an Agilent 6890 GC instrument equipped with an FID detector and HP-5 column.DHA was analyzed on Agilent 7890A GC instrument with a special column developed by the Research Institute of Petroleum Processing [ 1 ].Quantification was accomplished by using naphthalene as the internal standard.The yield of ML is defined as: Y ML = [ML]/[DHA] 0 × 100%, in which [DHA] 0 denotes the ini-tial concentration of DHA; [ML] is the concentration of ML after a certain reaction time.


Results and discussion


Acidic properties of Sn-MFI zeolite

The structural properties of synthesized Sn-MFI zeolite are presented in Figs.S2− S6.It is widely accepted that probe-assisted NMR is a sensitive and reliable method to obtain qualitative and quantitative acidity information regarding acid sites in various solid acid catalysts [ 3 , 6 , 40 , 42-46 ].In this study, the type of acid site (Brønsted or Lewis) in the Sn-MFI zeolite was initially evaluated by 31 P NMR using trimethylphosphine (TMP) as a probe molecule.Generally, the coupling of TMP molecule with Brønsted acid sites leads to the formation of TMPH + ionic complexes, which yield 31 P resonances within a narrow  31 P range at approximately 0 ppm.In contrast, the interaction of TMP molecule with Lewis acid sites usually extends over a significantly wider range from approximately − 10 to − 58 ppm depending on their metallic centers and acid strengths.The linear relationship between  31 P and Lewis acid strength makes TMP-assisted 31 P NMR a practical and reliable method for characterizing Lewis acidity [42] .As shown in Fig. 1 a, the 31 P magic-angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (MAS NMR) spectrum of TMP-loaded dehydrated Sn-MFI zeolite exhibited multiple characteristic peaks with  31 P values of 4, − 4, − 6, − 12, − 19, − 25, − 48, and − 60 ppm.Thereinto, the 31 P signals at approximately 0 ppm may be unambiguously ascribed to the TMPH + ionic complexes, which are closely associated with Brønsted acid sites.Meanwhile, the 31 P resonance peaks at − 12, − 19, − 25, and − 48 ppm likely originate from TMP adsorbed on Lewis acid sites with different acid strengths [42] .Furthermore, the intense 31 P signal observed at − 60 ppm is owing to physisorbed TMP, which is moderately confined inside the pore channels of Sn-MFI zeolite.Generally, it is recognized that the Lewis acidity of Sn-MFI zeolites is attributed to the tetravalent framework Sn species, which exist as "closed " and "open " Sn sites [ 33 , 34 ].However, unlike trivalent Al, the isomorphous substitution of Si with tetravalent Sn in Sn-MFI does not induce a negative charge, and thus does not introduce additional Brønsted acidic protons into the zeolite framework.Notably, the Sn-MFI zeolite used in this study was synthesized by the hydrothermal method without adding any Al source, thus the Brønsted acid sites cannot be ascribed to Si− OH− Al bridging hydroxyl groups.In this context, the aim of this study is to elucidate the origin of the Brønsted acidity in Sn-MFI zeolite.

2D 1 H-31 P heteronuclear correlation (HETCOR) NMR experiments [47] were further performed to achieve more reliable  31 P assignments for TMP adsorbed on Brønsted and Lewis acid sites.As shown in Fig. 2 a, the 2D HETCOR spectrum recorded with a short contact time (  c = 0.1 ms) exhibited only two correlation peaks at (− 4, 6.2) and (− 4, 7.3) ppm, corresponding to the 31 P resonance (F2 dimension) with  31 P at − 4 ppm and 1 H resonance (F1 dimension) with  1 H at ∼6.2-7.3 ppm, which are associated with the formation of TMPH + complexes when TMP adsorbs on Brønsted acid sites.When the contact time increases, more complex correlation peaks are expected to be detected.As shown in Fig. 2b-2d , when the contact time was extended to 4.0 ms, new correlation signals owing to TMP interactions with various acid sites were readily identified at (− 4, 2.3), (− 12 to − 25, 2.0), and (− 60, 1.5) ppm, corresponding to the couplings of TMP adsorbed on Brønsted (  31 P = − 4 ppm) and Lewis (  31 P = − 12 to − 25 ppm) acid sites, as well as physisorbed TMP (  31 P = − 60 ppm), with methyl protons observed at  1 H = ∼1.5-2.3 ppm.Moreover, additional correlation peak was observed at (− 60, 6.1) ppm at a relatively longer contact time (  c ≥ 1.0 ms; Fig. 2b-2d ), which may be ascribed to TMP physisorbed onto weakly acidic terminal hydroxyl groups via hydrogen-bonding interactions.Briefly, the combination of TMP-assisted 31 P NMR with 2D 1 H-31 P HETCOR NMR may significantly facilitate the identification of various active sites in Sn-MFI zeolite, as summarized in Fig. 1 c.


Dynamic acidic properties of Sn-MFI zeolite induced by extra water molecule

Additionally, it has been demonstrated that the adsorption of water and other adsorbate molecules dramatically alters the local structure of the surface active species in Sn-substituted zeolites [ 16-19 , 33 , 34 ].Herein, the framework Sn− O− Si bond in the "closed " Sn sites could be hydrolyzed by the attack of additional water molecule, accompanied by the formation of the "open " Sn sites with neighboring Sn− OH and Si− OH pairs.Therefore, to evaluate the variation of acidic properties during the hydration of Sn-MFI zeolite, the characterization of acidity using 31 P-TMP NMR experiments with different water loadings was also conducted.As shown in Fig. 1 a, the 31 P resonance peaks at approximately 0 ppm owing to TMPH + ionic complexes associated with Brønsted acid sites gradually increased with an increase in the water loading from 0 to 0.5 kPa.Simultaneously, the 31   S1).The  31 P NMR assignments of the hydrated Sn-MFI sample with adsorbed TMP were also verified by 2D 1 H-31 P HETCOR NMR (Fig. S7).The TMP-assisted 31 P NMR experimental results imply that the new Brønsted acid sites are formed at the expense of the Lewis acidic Sn species.As mentioned previously, the adsorption of water may induce the transformation of "closed " Sn sites into "open " sites, both of which normally function as Lewis acidity.Thus, it may be inferred that the origin for the new Brønsted acid sites in Sn-MFI zeolite is very likely related to the "open " Sn sites.Moreover, it was also found that physisorbed TMP becomes increasingly mobile and flexible upon incorporation and interaction with more H 2 O (that is, the corresponding 31 P signal shifted from − 60 to − 62 ppm).

The acidity characterization results motivated our exploration of the origin of Brønsted acid centers in Sn-MFI zeolite and their evolution mechanism during hydration.It is recognized that 1 H MAS NMR is a direct and simple method to identify the subtle chemical environment of H atoms in various H-containing species such as hydroxyl groups [ 17 , 46 , 48 , 49 ].Therefore, 1 H MAS NMR results are expected to provide invaluable information on the various surface active species during their hydration evolution.As shown in Fig. 1 d, the 1 H MAS NMR spectrum of dehydrated Sn-MFI zeolite showed four 1 H signals at 0.2, 2.0, 2.2, and 3.3 ppm.The peak at 0.2 ppm may be assigned to the Sn− OH groups, indicating the presence of "open " Sn sites in Sn-MFI zeolite [50] , even after dehydration at an elevated temperature (673 K) for at least 10 h.The 1 H signals at 2.0 and 2.2 ppm are ascribed to terminal Si− OH groups with minor distinctions in chemical environment [ 46 , 48 ].Furthermore, the broad 1 H resonance at 3.3 ppm is attributed to perturbed − OH groups owing to hydrogen-bonding interactions [ 46 , 49 ].

NMR experiments were also conducted to evaluate the evolution process of various hydroxyl groups upon hydration.As shown in Fig. 1 d and 1 e, after exposure to a small amount of water vapor (0.1 kPa), 5.0 ppm, corresponding to more complex hydrogen-bonding interaction networks, was observed immediately after the introduction of H 2 O to the dehydrated Sn-MFI sample.Upon further increasing the H 2 O Dynamic acidic properties of Sn-MFI zeolite induced by other adsorbate molecules

Although the NMR experimental results strongly imply the origin of the Brønsted acidity in Sn-MFI zeolite is attributed to the neighboring Sn− OH and Si− OH pairs in the framework "open " Sn sites, the mechanism of Brønsted acid formation from this type of hydroxyl pairs remains unclear.This is because these hydroxyl pairs do not contain the typical bridging hydroxyl groups, and the protons in the individual Sn− OH and Si− OH groups are not easily removed from the corresponding oxygen atoms.The FTIR spectrum of dehydrated Sn-MFI zeolite [ 37 , 52 ] exhibited a broader vibrational signal at approximately 3,500 cm − 1 and a sharp peak at approximately 3,730 cm − 1 , which are attributed to nest and terminal hydroxyl groups, respectively, also being consistent with the NMR results that no typical Brønsted acidic proton (at approximately 3,600 cm − 1 in FTIR) is present.

A series of frequently-used NMR probe molecules with different basic strengths were used to comprehensively elucidate the origin of Brønsted acidity in Sn-MFI zeolite.As shown in Fig. 4 a and 4 b, the 1 H MAS NMR spectrum of the dehydrated zeolites with the adsorption of weakly basic acetonitrile-d 3 (CD 3 CN) exhibited a 1 H signal at approximately 11.0 ppm owing to hydrogen-bonding interactions between CD 3 CN and the typical Brønsted acidic protons in Al-MFI zeolite [46] .In contrast, this characteristic peak was absent in the Sn-MFI sample, and a broad resonance peak originating from hydrogen-bonding interactions between CD 3 CN and the weakly acidic hydroxyl groups was observed at approximately 6.0 ppm [46] .Similar experimental results were observed when more basic 2-13 C-acetone and pyridine-d 5 were used as adsorbent molecules.

Furthermore, 2D 1 H- 13 C HETCOR NMR of dehydrated Sn-MFI zeolite with adsorbed 2-13 C-acetone was performed.As shown in Fig. 4 c, the correlation peak owing to the formation of hydrogen-bonding interaction complexes between 2-13 C-acetone molecule and Brønsted acidic .Owing to the nature of cross-polarization (CP) NMR, the more mobile 2-13 C-acetone adsorbed over weakly acidic hydroxyl groups is expected to yield a lower CP transfer efficiency during NMR (Fig. S10).Consequently, this leads to much weaker and even negligible correlations for the hydrogen-bonding interactions between 2-13 Cacetone and weakly acidic hydroxyl groups in the 2D 1 H- 13 C HETCOR NMR spectrum.For comparison, this characteristic correlation signal appeared at (223, 16.2) ppm when 2-13 C-acetone was adsorbed over Al-MFI zeolite ( Fig. 4 d), which possesses typical bridging hydroxyl groups with Brønsted acidity (Figs.S11, S12).

Moreover, it is noteworthy that the FTIR spectrum of Sn-MFI zeolite with adsorbed pyridine also showed mainly the Lewis acid signal at approximately 1,450 cm − 1 , whereas no evident peak of the typical Brønsted acid (at approximately 1,540 cm − 1 ) was detected (Figs. S13, S14).A similar phenomenon has been reported in the literature [ 37 , 39 ], in accordance with the 1  By summarizing the acidity characterization results, it is found that the neighboring Sn− OH and Si− OH pairs in the framework "open " Sn sites of stannosilicate zeolites are impotent in achieving Brønsted acidity themselves.However, this is not the case when these pairs are perturbed by different surrounding local microenvironments, for example, upon adsorption of molecules with distinct basic strengths, such as CD 3 CN, acetone, pyridine, and TMP.Although the Sn− OH and Si− OH pairs are not able to exert Brønsted acidity when interacting with CD 3 CN (proton affinity energy (PA): 779.2 kJ/mol [53] ), acetone (PA: 812.0 kJ/mol [53] ), or pyridine (PA: 930.0 kJ/mol [53] ) owing to their relatively weak basic strengths, Brønsted acidity is achieved when using strong basic TMP (PA: 958.8 kJ/mol [53] ) as an adsorbate.Thus, pseudo-Brønsted acid sites are induced in Sn-MFI zeolite.Notably, framework Si atoms in zeolites usually maintain tetracoordination, whereas framework Sn atoms are capable of readily altering their coordination state from tetracoordinated to penta-and hexacoordinated states [33] .Therefore, a mechanism involving the hydrolysis evolution of framework "closed " Sn sites to the "open " sites, followed by the dynamic transformation of neighboring Sn− OH and Si− OH pairs to pseudo-Brønsted acid sites induced by additional adsorbates with strong basicity is proposed, as illustrated in Scheme 1 .

Noted that different reactant molecules usually exert distinct basic strengths.Thus, it is expected that the real active sites of Snsubstituted zeolites may also change when catalyzing different reactions.Although weakly basic individual reactant molecules may be insufficient to achieve the Brønsted acidity of stannosilicate zeolites, some strong basic reactants are capable of realizing this.Alternatively, the aggregation of multiple adsorbate molecules may significantly enhance the basicity of the resulting cluster (e.g., the formation of water clusters by hydrogen-bonding interactions [17] ).In this context, the reaction pathway catalyzed by the Brønsted acid sites in stannosilicate zeolites are not completely negligible in many catalytic processes.Undoubtedly, this type of dynamic transformation of the active centers owing to the subtle interactions with distinct adsorbates with different basic strengths is crucial for elucidating the real structure-activity correlations in various reaction systems, and provides new in-depth insights into the fundamental understanding of catalysis by stannosilicate zeolites.


Conclusion

In summary, the nature and dynamic evolution process of the active sites in Sn-MFI zeolite were comprehensively revealed using solidstate NMR spectroscopy combined with water and various adsorbate The results reported in this study provide a new opportunity for fundamentally understanding the flexible active center properties and the acid-catalyzed reaction mechanism of various modified zeolite catalysts with heterometal atoms, such as Sn, Ti, and Zr, under real reaction conditions.Thus, these findings are beneficial for the design, modification, and fabrication of improved zeolites for practical catalytic applications.
