Does Professional Management of a Family Business Affect Its Financing Channels? Evidence from China
10.1080/1540496X.2022.2068411Family businessfinancing channelsprofessional management degree
Despite developing rapidly in the Chinese market, family firms still face financing issues. This article uses Chinese family firms that went public from 2007 to 2019 as a sample and empirically examines whether professional management of a family business affects its financing channels. Our results indicate that the degree of professional management has a significantly negative impact on the internal financing rate and a significantly positive effect on increasing the external financing rate. In addition, industry competition intensifies the positive correlation between the degree of professional management and external financing.

Introduction

The family firm plays an important role in the global economy and is one of the major existing business forms in China. Due to the key role of family business in the economic structure, family firms have been widely concerned by many scholars and experts in practice for many years. A consensus seems to have formed that what makes family business unique is the interaction of family management and ownership regimes (Gersick et al. 1997). Although early researchers on family business focused on both family and business outcomes (Dyer 2003), a steady shift has taken place toward using business-related theories to explain how families shape business decisions and outcomes (James, Jennings, and Breitkreuz 2012). The current research has yielded important insights about how family firms differ from non-family firms (Chrisman, Chua, and Sharma 2003), the strategic advantages of family involvement (Habbershon and Williams 1999), and how families and institutions interact on a global scale (Morck and Yeung 2003). Research on the family focuses mainly on the role of family members in business development (Li and Piezunka 2020) and the influence of parental characteristics on family succession (Huang et al. 2020). Others look at life cycles in the family, such as the family cycle (Combs et al. 2020). Other issues in family business, such as professionalization and stakeholders, ethics, and social responsibility issues (Debicki, C. F. Matherne, and Chrisman 2009), still have not been well researched. Professional management of a family business has become an increasingly notable phenomenon (Stewart and Hitt 2011).

Based on a framework on the agency problem in family business, this paper focuses on the perspective of the degree of professional management of a family business, differentiating degrees of professional management. Then we analyze the differences in the financing behavior of family firms at different degrees of professional management. We make the following contributions.

First, the paper enriches the research on financing a family business, especially in emerging markets. The existing studies on financing a family business focus mainly on the importance of financing resources (Koropp et al. 2014) and the impact of changes in the core member(s) on financing decisions (Gonzalez, Guzman, and Pombo 2013). However, few papers study professional management of a family business, let alone its impact on financing channels. Based on the perspective of agency theory, this paper analyzes the differences in the financing channels of family businesses at different degrees of professional management.

Second, we expand the methods for measuring the professional management of a family business to study the impact of differences in the degree of professional management of family businesses on financing decisions. The results are significant for other developing countries as they develop a private economy, especially with respect to financing a family business, intergenerational transfer of controllers, and the introduction of professional managers.


Theoretical Analysis and Hypothesis Development

Professional corporate management has become an increasingly interesting phenomenon in the development of family businesses (Stewart and Hitt 2011). The rationale for professional firm management is rooted in agency theory, which states that if managerial behavior is not regulated, managers' goal will be self-interest (Jensen and Meckling 1976). The existing literature disagrees on how to make family businesses more professional: some scholars believe that family firms can hire managers from the external manager market to realize the diversity of enterprise talents, expand the business perspective and experience, and promote the professional management of enterprises (Dawson 2011). In addition, family members might engage in self-interested behavior (Chrisman, Chua, and Sharma 2003). Non-family managers can play an additional supervisory role (Daspit and Long 2014) by helping family businesses to formulate high-quality financing strategies according to the business environment and the demands of family members, so as to improve the performance of family businesses (Dawson 2011). For family businesses, this means bringing in non-family employees, especially full-time managers (Stewart and Hitt 2011), so that firms can be transformed to professional management. At the same time, some scholars argue that the introduction of professional managers also leads to agency problems because the self-interested motives of external professional managers make it possible to maximize personal interests or act opportunistically, abandon the goal of maximizing shareholders' interests, and misuse company resources to serve personal interests (Jensen and Meckling 1976).

Housekeeper theory is also a theoretical basis for the impact of family firm governance. Originating in the social person hypothesis, housekeeper theory emphasizes that people have a strong motivation to pursue self-worth (Davis, Schoorman, and Donaldson 1997). Therefore, the housekeeper theory holds that family managers are trustworthy "housekeepers." At the early stage of a family business, family management is manifested as a tendency toward "housekeeper" behavior, and family managers will try to make good use of the owner's property, as a "housekeeper" does. However, with the expansion of the scale of family businesses, family managers act more like agents (Fan, Liu, and Yang 2016).

The operation and development of a family business is inseparable from financing, but achieving the optimal financing structure is very complicated (Orlova, Harper, and Sun 2020). Different from other types of companies, the financial decisions of family businesses are not based only on economic factors; rather, they also prioritize non-economic factors (Michiels, Uhlaner, and Dekker 2017). Other important points that they consider are the long-term impact of financial decisions and ensuring family control, continuity, and reputation (Schepers et al. 2020). The dynamic capability theory based on the resource-based view holds that, in the process of development, a firm should dynamically reorganize and construct its asset structure (Zott and Amit 2001). In the development process, financing needs are dynamic. At the early stage of development, the firm's financing needs are small, so they can rely on internal financing. However, as the firm grows in size, internal financing alone does not suffice and cannot avoid the firm's financing constraints. Therefore, the firm has to expand its financing channels, and external financing is an option for doing so.

Faced with complex financing needs, family businesses weigh various financing options when making financing decisions. Some studies have found that family businesses seek advice from professional advisers when making decisions (Strike, Michel, and Kammerlander 2018). Other studies have found that owners or managers can have a significant impact on a firm's financing decisions (Carbone et al. 2021). For a family business, financing decisions might differ depending on whether a family member serves as the CFO (Giuli, Caselli, and Gatti 2011). Gomez-Mejia et al. (2011) state that family firms are bound to face financial dilemmas unless they hire professional managers who are not family members. Family businesses often bring in professionals who are not family members to help with financing issues. Gu and Shen (2016) state that experienced professional managers have a better understanding of market rules, and they have better financing channels, usually external channels. Therefore, when family members want to expand the family business or deal with market competition, even if they are aware of the potential conflict between the personal motivation of professional managers and the family, they will consider introducing professional managers. To a certain extent, this will affect the priority on family control in the firm's previous financing. Because raising the value of the company is a worthy priority for professional managers, which requires better performance, and improvement in performance requires financial support. In order to achieve their own goals, professional managers might expand financing channels and introduce external funds (Dawson 2011). In general, family members are more inclined to adopt internal financing in order to protect the family's control over the firm, while professionals who are not family members are more inclined to adopt external financing.

Due to the differences among family firms in the professional management needs and processes at the management level, the degree of professional management at family firms varies, which in turn leads to differences in financing decisions. To sum up, when the degree of professional management at a family business is low, the company is more inclined to rely on internal financing to maintain control. However, the introduction of professional managers at a family business increases the degree of professionalism and thereby the proportion of external financing. Therefore, we posit the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: When a family business has a higher degree of professional management, it has a lower rate of internal financing.

Hypothesis 2: When a family business has a higher degree of professional management of the family business, it has a higher rate of external financing.

Fierce industry competition can intensify the operational risk of firms. When business risk increases, so does the demand for capital (Almeida, Campello, and Weisbach 2004). When the degree of market competition is high, the pricing power of firms is limited, profit margins are compressed, and the capital demand of firms is greater (Han and Zhou 2011). In order to ensure their own competitive advantage, firms often respond to this problem by accelerating innovation and research and development (Erdogan, Rondi, and Massis 2020). As innovation activities require sufficient financial support, the financing needs of family businesses are further increased (Chen, Wu, and Chen 2017). Then it is difficult for family firms to meet their financing needs only through internal financing, so they need to expand their external financing channels. The expansion of financing demand has further urge firms to improve the financing ability of managers and change the financing modes of family firms. In the previous section, we discussed how professional management raises the external financing level of a family business. Therefore, a family business expands the external financing channels by introducing professional managers, thereby changing its financing methods.

At the same time, professional managers in a competitive environment also use their own social resources and experience to raise funds as much as possible so as to obtain better performance. In summary, we believe that when the degree of industry competition changes, the financing needs of family firms change accordingly, affecting their financing structure. We thus posit our third hypothesis as follows.

Hypothesis 3: The intensity of industry competition increases the positive correlation between the degree of professional management of family firms and the rate of external financing.


Data and Model


Data

Our data comes from the CSMAR database, and the sample includes three types of family firms: (1) entrepreneurial firms run by a single natural person, which are family firms whose actual controller is an individual, and no members of the family hold shares or stock in the company or occupy a position as the director; (2) entrepreneurial firms run by several natural person in which the actual controller is a family enterprise with multiple natural persons but with no kinship relationship between the actual controllers, and no relatives of family firms with holding shares in the superior companies or holding shareholder companies or occupying a director position; (3) family firms run by several people, in which at least one is a family member, and manage, or control the listed company besides the actual controller.

We select Chinese family businesses from 2007 to 2019 as the research sample for the following reasons. First, considering the age of the family founder and succession to the second generation, we believe that, at the beginning of the twenty-first century, family businesses in China have begun to make personnel changes in their core managers, in a way that increases the degree of professional management. Second, China's accounting standards were revised in 2007, after which the accounting information of listed companies changed. Third, we end the sample in 2019 to avoid the effects of the outbreak of COVID-19, which had wide-ranging economic impacts. In addition, we omit the following from the sample: ST (special treatment) companies referring to the listed companies that have suffered losses for two consecutive years and are subject to special treatment, companies in the financial industry, and firms with unreliable or missing financial information. Ultimately, we obtain 11,133 observations of 2,086 listed family firms. All continuous variables are winsorized at 1%.


Variable Definitions

The internal financing rate (IFR) reflects the share of internal financing, measured by the ratio of a firm's free cash flow to total assets, and the calculation of cash flow follows Brown, Martinsson, and Petersen (2012). The external financing rate (EFR) is the percentage of funds that firms obtain externally, measured by the ratio of the sum of the scale of credit financing and equity financing to total assets. The scale of credit financing, following Cosci, Meliciani, and Sabato (2016), is measured by the sum of short-and long-term loans of the company; the scale of equity financing, following Li, Tang, and Zuo (2013), is measured by the sum of share capital and capital reserves.

The degree of professional management (DPM) is defined following Weng, Wang, and Lv (2014). A distinction is made in family business between a family firm managed by a family member and a firm managed by a non-family member (Singla, Veliyath, and George 2014). The degree of professional management can be represented on two axes: the number of non-family members that control a company and their professional position. Liao (2003) stated that, in terms of the power allocation of listed companies in China, boards of directors rank first, followed by senior executives and shareholders. We measure the degree of professional management based on whether a non-family or family member serves as the chairman and CEO of a family business: a value of 5 denotes that a non-family member is the general manager and chairman of the board, and the chairman and the general manager are not concurrently in charge; a value of 4 denotes that a non-family member serves as both the chairman and general manager; a value of 3 denotes that a family member is the general manager; a value of 2 denotes that the family member is the chairman of the board of directors; a value of 1 denotes that the chairman and the general manager roles are performed by family members, and the chairman and the general manager are not concurrently in charge; a value of 0 denotes that the chairman and general manager roles are performed by family members, and the chairman and the general manager are concurrently in charge.

To measure the degree of industry competition (DIC), we use the inverse of the Herfindahl index as an indicator of the degree of competition after careful consideration. When the Herfindahl index is higher, the degree of industry concentration is higher, and thus competition in the industry is less fierce. The DIC can be expressed as follows:

Besides, we control for the Year and Industry with dummy variables.

Column (3) in Table 2 shows that when the degree of professional management of family firms is higher, so is the external financing rate. The interaction term is significantly positive, indicating that the intensity of industry competition increases the positive correlation between the degree of professional management of family firms and the rate of external financing, confirming H3. When they face less competitive pressure, family firm managers are more inclined to pursue internal financing. When they have increasingly fierce industry competition, companies can no longer meet their financing needs by relying on internal financing alone. Therefore, financing pressure increases the proportion of external financing and enhances competitiveness in the industry.


Robustness Check


Replacement of the Independent Variable

Following Lane et al. (2006), we use the proportion of board members at a family business made up of non-family members (NFMP) to measure the degree of professional management at family businesses. When NFMP is higher, so is the degree of professional management. The results are shown in Table 3. The coefficient of NFMP in column (1) is significantly negative; the coefficient of NFMP in column (2) is significantly positive; the coefficient of NFMP_DIC in column (3) is significantly positive. Therefore, H1-H3 are again confirmed.


Reverse Causality

Our regression results show that the difference in the degree of professional management of family businesses affects their financing channels; however, it is also possible that family firms that are more inclined to use external financing often tend to increase the degree of professional management. In order to mitigate the problem of reverse causality, we conduct a two-stage regression using an instrumental variable (IV), that is, whether the CEO of a family business changes from a family member to a non-family member. Changing from family members to non-family members means the introduction of professional managers, a reduction in family governance, and more professional Table 3. The last column presents the regression coefficients (IV_P) of the IVs in the first stage of a two-stage least squares (2SLS regression), which are all significant at the 1% level, indicating that the IVs are significantly related to the degree of professional management (DPM). The regression coefficients of DPM and IFR, DPM and EFR, and DPM_DIC and EFR are all significant, and the coefficient signs are the same as in the previous test results. This suggests that our results remain robust after accounting for reverse causality.


Sample Selection Bias

To address any potential selection bias problems, we use the Heckman two-stage model to test the three hypotheses. In the probit regression model of the first stage, we construct the dummy variable MDPM, based on whether the independent variable DPM is higher than the median in the same industry. If it is larger than the industry median, it is coded as 1, indicating that, compared with all the family businesses in the same industry, the company has a relatively low  degree of professional management; otherwise, 0. The inverse Mills ratio (IMR) was calculated using the regression results of the first stage, and then the IMR was substituted into the second stage model for fitting. Table 5 shows the regression results for H1-H3, in which the coefficients of IMR in columns (1)-(3) are all significant, indicating a problem of selection bias in the sample. However, the coefficients of DPM and DPM_DIC are all significant, consistent with the previous results, which shows that our results remain robust to consideration of sample selection bias.


Conclusions

The correlation between the structure of financing and governance is one of the most important areas of research on firm performance. Using a sample of Chinese listed companies that went public from 2007 to 2019 and following the related literature on family firm governance, professional managers, and the structure of financing, we test three hypotheses with strict criteria and methods. Then, we discuss the correlations between professional management and the structure of financing, introducing the level of competition as an external factor that affects the structure of financing. Our results lead to the following conclusions.

First, the level of professional management has a significantly negative impact on the rate of internal financing. Second, the introduction of professional managers has a significantly positive impact on expansion in financing channels. Third, industry competition increases the degree of positive correlation between the degree of professional management of family businesses and the rate of external financing.

These conclusions have the following implications. First, the Chinese government should issue policies to stipulate that family firms need to ensure the proportion of non-family members among core members, such as board members, supervisory board members, and senior management members after listing and encourage family firms to introduce professional managers. Second, the government should establish an effective incentive and restraint mechanism and continuously optimize it dynamically and standardize the management system, strengthen the trust between family businesses and professional managers, and promote the development of the professional manager market.
