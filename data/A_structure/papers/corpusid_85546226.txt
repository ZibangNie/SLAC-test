Trends in use and attitudes towards contraception in Sweden: results of a nationwide survey
10.1080/13625187.2019.1581163Received 20 October 2018 Revised 16 January 2019 Accepted 6 February 2019ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLEAbortioncontraceptionLARCtermination of pregnancyunintended pregnancyunwanted pregnancy
Objective: The aim of the study was to compare the results of a 2013 nationwide survey of 1001 women representative of the Swedish female population with the results of a similar survey carried out in 2017, after new guidelines for contraceptive use were published in 2014. Methods: Women between 16 and 49 years of age took part in a telephone survey; questions covered demographics, contraceptive use, knowledge of and attitudes towards contraception, importance of monthly bleeding, and experience of unintended pregnancy. We compared the responses collected in 2013 with those collected in 2017. Results: Of all women in 2017, 717/1008 (71.1%) were currently using contraception; 291/1008 (28.9%) women were not. Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) was used by 311/1008 (30.9%) women, which was a significant increase from 2013 (243/1001, 24.3%; p ¼ .001). The unmet need for contraception in Sweden in 2017 was estimated to be 15.2%. A total of 783 (77.7%) women had never experienced an unintended pregnancy. A significantly higher proportion of unintended pregnancies reported in 2017 were terminated compared with 2013. Conclusion: Sweden has a large unmet need for contraception. In 2017, LARC use had increased compared with 2013. A large proportion of women had experienced an unintended pregnancy. Increasing overall contraceptive use is important to reduce the rates of unintended and unwanted pregnancies.ARTICLE HISTORY

Introduction

Sweden has the highest abortion rate in Western Europe [1], in spite of easy access to contraceptive counselling in nurse midwifery clinics and youth centres. In Sweden, most contraceptive prescriptions are issued by nurse midwives, only a small number by gynaecologists and almost none by general practitioners. Recently, subsidies have been increased for women 21 years, who receive certain contraceptives free of charge. In most regions, women 25 years pay approximately e10 per year for contraception. The abortion rate has, however, remained high at close to 20 per 1000 women [2]. Sweden differs from the other Nordic countries, which have significantly lower abortion rates [3][4][5]. In 2017, the increasing abortion rate stalled, when a lower rate was reported for all age groups except women aged 30-39 years [2].

The importance of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC; implants, intrauterine devices [IUDs] and the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system [LNG-IUS]) in reducing the rate of unwanted pregnancy and abortion has recently become clear [6][7][8][9]. Better knowledge about contraception may help to promote LARC use among those most susceptible to unwanted pregnancies. In 2014, the Swedish Medical Products Agency published new national guidelines for contraceptive use which emphasised the recommendation of LARC especially for young women [10], as it has been shown that younger women have lower adherence to user-dependent methods [8].

Official statistics in Sweden only cover collected prescriptions for hormonal contraception and thus overestimate its use, as adherence and discontinuation are unknown. In addition, use of methods not requiring prescription, such as the copper IUD, diaphragm, male condom and cycle-based methods, is unknown. In order to obtain the information required for appropriate action against the high numbers of unwanted pregnancies, a nationwide survey of 1001 women representative of the Swedish female population was conducted in 2013 [11]. The main objectives of the 2017 survey were to investigate the impact of new subsidies and compare the proportion of contraceptive users, the contraceptive methods used and the unmet need for contraception with the data gathered in the 2013 survey. Secondary outcome measures were knowledge of contraceptive methods, attitudes towards contraception and prevalence and outcome of unintended pregnancies. be sufficient to be able to conduct valid subgroup analyses of women in different age intervals. Women between the ages of 16 and 49 years were identified through the PAR Konsument consumer database, which covers 90% of all individuals aged 16 and above in Sweden. Both landline and mobile telephone numbers were used; up to 20 attempts were made before contact was deemed to have failed. Women in the contact failure group were replaced by women in the same age category. In the 2013 survey, a separate sample was drawn for women aged 16-20, in order to obtain good stratification of young women. To correct for the fact that younger women were less well represented in telephone registers their proportion was multiplied by a factor of 1.2 according to standard operating procedure. In the 2017 survey, no such trends were seen and therefore no weighting was performed. A thorough description of the sampling methodology and database is provided in Supplementary Text 1.

The distribution of the demographic characteristics of individuals in the sample was compared with the expected distribution supplied by the tax authority. The demographic characteristics that are available for comparison are age and place of residence. The tax authority does not supply statistics on current income or ethnic background to commercial companies when matched to personal identification numbers. A survey conducted by telephone therefore cannot correct for these characteristics.

All contacted women were informed about the objectives of the survey prior to questioning and assured that all personal information would be coded and not accessible to the report commissioner. All women were informed that the survey was a collaboration between a pharmaceutical company (MSD Sweden) and independent researchers. Immigrant women were excluded if they had insufficient knowledge of the Swedish language to understand the questions, as determined by the professional interviewer.

The survey contained 10 questions on demographic characteristics such as age, region and area of residence (town, city, rural, etc.), employment status, educational level, civil status, household income (including unemployment or social benefits, etc.) and ethnic background (born in Sweden, parents born in Sweden, etc.).

The survey comprised 26 questions on choice of contraceptive method, knowledge about and awareness of contraceptive methods, important criteria for choosing a contraceptive, reasons for not using contraception (for women not currently using contraception), opinion on importance of monthly bleeding, perceived problems during contraceptive use, and experience of unwanted pregnancy. Answers were open and multiple-choice. The survey's structure and questions were modelled on an Austrian contraceptive survey performed in 2012 (C. Fiala, Unpublished data) and were modified from the 2013 survey [11]. The 2017 survey took approximately 15 min to complete. We defined 'unmet need for contraception' in this context as non-contraceptive users who were not attempting to get pregnant, were not infertile or who did not have exclusively same sex partners.

Differences in proportions were analysed by Fisher's exact test.

All women could choose whether to answer the questions and could stop the telephone survey at any time. As is customary in telephone surveys, consent was obtained verbally. Written consent was not considered a necessary part of the protocol. Verbal informed consent was recorded in the subject file. Parental consent was obtained if the interviewee was a minor. Parental consent was recorded in the subject file. Ethics permission is not needed for telephone surveys when personal identification numbers are not known, as participating women cannot be traced once the interview is completed.


Results

In total, 1338 women between 16 and 49 years were contacted. A flow chart is provided in Figure 1. A number of women (210 women, 15.7%) actively stated that they did not want to take part in the survey; the percentage of refusals was similar to that of the 2013 survey (18.4%). Reasons for declining did not differ from those given in 2013. We analysed the known demographic information of women who declined to take part and found that it did not to differ from that of the general Swedish female population. Fewer immigrant women participated in the survey (8%) compared with Swedish women in the same age groups (24%) [12]. In other aspects, the participants closely represented the Swedish female population with reference to known variables.

Of all women, 717/1008 (71.1%) confirmed current use of contraception; 284/1008 (28.2%) women did not use contraception; 7/1008 (0.7%) had stopped using contraception during the last 12 months.

This change was driven by a change in contraceptive use among women <30 years of age. As in 2013, use of SARC was highest in younger women and thereafter decreased with increasing age. In 2017, use of SARC had also significantly decreased among women aged 30-39 years (p ¼ .034). Women aged 30-39 were the only group in which overall non-use of contraception had increased significantly: from 72/279 (25.8%) women in 2013 to 125/414 (30.2%; p ¼ .043) in 2017.

Among the 291 non-contraceptive users in our sample, 99 were trying to conceive, 26 had been sterilised or diagnosed infertile, or had a partner diagnosed as infertile, two were menopausal and 11 had exclusively same sex partners. This left a total of 153 non-users with a need for contraception. The unmet need for contraception was thus 15.2% (153/1008 women). In addition, 262/717 (36.5%) women in 2017 used less effective methods (methods other than hormonal, intrauterine or permanent) compared with 303/721 (42.0%; p ¼ .035) in 2013.

Discussion


Findings and interpretation

This nationwide survey shows that a continued high proportion of women in Sweden (15.2%) have an unmet need  for contraception. A total of 77.7% had never experienced an unintended pregnancy and 22.3% had had at least one unintended pregnancy. This confirms the high proportion of women in Sweden with experience of unintended pregnancy. Updated contraceptive recommendations have affected prescribing patterns and contraceptive use in Sweden. An increasing proportion of women in Sweden have an unmet need for contraception or use less effective contraceptive methods. An increasing proportion of unplanned pregnancies end in abortion. Thus, the demand for planned pregnancies is increasing.


Strengths and weaknesses

A strength of this survey is the large sample size of 1008 women. There were also few missing answers, which contributes to high data quality. The major limitation of the survey is that many women could not be contacted and therefore were replaced. However, the contact rate was better than in the 2013 survey; thus, the 2017 survey may have a more representative sample. However, selection bias cannot be excluded, as contraceptive behaviour is not only influenced by sociodemographics but also by type of partnership and lifestyle, which may also be related to not being reachable by telephone. There was a lower proportion of immigrant women in our sample than in Sweden in general, but the proportion was similar to that in 2013. Immigrant women often have lower contraceptive use [13]. For questions regarding events in the past, such as unintended pregnancy and side effects from contraception, recall bias may be a factor.


Similarities and differences in relation to other studies

Users and non-users alike stated that the most important characteristic of a contraceptive method was its effectiveness. To be able to ensure an active choice of contraceptive method, it is important that women of all ages are kept informed of new contraceptive methods and do not passively continue the method they were given when they were first counselled. In 2017, as well as in 2013, women overestimated the effectiveness of the method they had been using in the last 12 months. For all methods except a family planning application (app) a majority of women stated that their method had 'very high effectiveness'. By contrast, 5% and 17% of women, respectively, underestimated the effectiveness of LARC and sterilisation, stating that the methods had only 'high effectiveness' or less. Underestimation of the effectiveness of LARC methods has also been shown in a study from the USA [14]. Over-or underestimation of the effectiveness of methods may make women choose methods which do not meet their contraceptive needs. The unmet need for contraception in Sweden was estimated at 8.9% in 2013 and 15.2% in 2017. The difference in results may be explained by the proportion of women reporting infertility. In the 2013 survey, women selfreported infertility; however, in the 2017 survey, the alternative given was 'diagnosed infertility', which might have reduced the number of women stating infertility as a reason for not using contraception. Women who self-report infertility may constitute a subfertile group. Unfortunately, there have been no recently published estimates of the proportion of women with unmet contraceptive need in countries such as the USA and France. The most recent estimates are from 2007 for the USA (10.7%) [15] and 2012 for France (<3%) [16].

The unmet need for contraception in Sweden is difficult to explain, as contraception is subsidised for young women and is easy to access in midwifery clinics and youth health care centres. A large proportion of Swedish women use less effective contraceptive methods. A considerably higher proportion of women in Sweden (25.6% of contraceptive users) than in France (15%) use less effective methods [17]. In comparison with Sweden, the USA has a higher proportion of women using an effective method [18].

Although experienced side effects is the most common reason for not using contraception among women aged 21-29, the proportion of women not using contraception because of side effects had not increased since 2013. However, 'fear of hormones' has increased significantly from 2013 to 2017 as the reason for not using contraception. In order to balance the information on side effects which is readily available on the internet and social media, counsellors in contraception should address the fear of side effects and inform women about the positive health benefits of hormonal contraception. In Sweden, the use of alternative contraceptive methods such as contraceptive mobile apps is increasing, with one app now having more than 110,000 registered users (R. Scherwitzl, Personal communication, 2018). This may be due to a fear of hormones.

LARC use increased in Sweden between 2013 and 2017. In our survey, however, it is counteracted by a significantly lower use of SARC. These trends are supported by the official prescription register: collected SARC prescriptions have been decreasing in Sweden by close to 15% since 2006 when the register started. This decrease is not counterbalanced by the increased collection of prescribed LARCs [19]. The use of less effective methods is also reflected in the increasing use of emergency contraception. These factors and the proportion of women with an unmet need for contraception in Sweden may well account for the high abortion rate in Sweden. Since 2014, the continuous flexible regimen of combined hormonal contraception has been included in the guidelines for contraceptive use. However, few users of combined hormonal contraception use this method in spite of close to 50% of women wanting fewer or no bleeds. Information on the possibility of a continuous flexible regimen could improve acceptance and use of these methods and most likely also reduce the problems experienced and consequences of these, such as side effects and forgetting to take pills.

In 2017, there was a reduction in abortion rate in the age group in which LARC use increased significantly. Worldwide, abortion rates are highest among women aged 20-24. In Sweden, in 2017, the abortion rate was highest among women aged 25-29 years and also increased among women aged 24-34 years. Thus, Sweden is experiencing a trend of decreasing use of effective contraception and increased abortion among women aged 24-34 years. Similar abortion trends of decreasing rates among teenagers and women aged 20-24 years but increasing rates in women aged 30-34 years have also been seen in England and Wales [20]. There have been several government-initiated investigations into the relatively high abortion rate in Sweden [21][22][23][24][25]. Until now, results and recommendations from these investigations have resulted in greater subsidies for contraception among younger women, but advice to increase sexuality education in schools has been ignored.


Open questions and future research

Which national and contraceptive advice policy that is most effective in reducing unintended and unwanted pregnancy rates remains a topic for future research. More contraceptive use surveys and a greater understanding of contraceptive knowledge and how it influences contraceptive use are needed. An active national policy to reduce unintended pregnancy rates must focus on maintaining or increasing overall contraceptive use and not merely on a shift to more effective methods for women already using contraception.
