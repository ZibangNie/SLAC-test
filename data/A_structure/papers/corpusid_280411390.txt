Prognostic Value of Geriatric Characteristics in Allogeneic Haematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant Recipients Aged Over 60 Years
E037F84F1A45822ECFEA38BF70FE93DE10.1002/jha2.70115Received: 3 February 2025 Revised: 14 April 2025 Accepted: 8 July 2025
Introduction: Allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplant (alloHSCT) offers a curative option for older adults with haematological malignancies.The use of geriatric assessments has transformed the landscape of haemato-oncology care by improving risk stratification.We aim to study the prognostic value of geriatric characteristics in patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent alloHSCT at Singapore General Hospital between 2017 and 2023.Patient data were examined retrospectively.Results: A total of 66 patients were included, with a median age of 65 years and 42% aged above 65.Most patients had acute leukaemia (61%), and stem cell sources included matched sibling donor (18%), matched unrelated donor (33%) and haploidentical donor (48%).Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) was ≥ 90 in 41% of patients, and 86% had Haematopoietic Cell Transplantation-Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI) scores of 0-2.The median Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G) score was 5. A total of 12% had renal insufficiency.At a median follow-up of 32.5 months, 2-year non-relapse mortality (NRM), progression-free survival and overall survival (OS) were 21%, 55% and 58%, respectively.On multivariate analysis, age > 65 years (HR 3.84, p = 0.027) and renal insufficiency (HR 6.28, p = 0.005) were associated with increased risks of NRM.Similarly, age > 65 years (HR 2.75, p = 0.03) and renal insufficiency (HR 3.46, p = 0.01) conferred inferior OS.KPS, HCT-CI, CIRS-G, albumin, body mass index and polypharmacy did not predict for NRM and OS.Conclusion:This study supports the feasibility of alloHSCT as a treatment option for older adults with haematological malignancies.Prospective studies incorporating geriatric assessment will allow personalised transplant strategies to improve post-transplant outcomes.Trial Registration: The authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission 1This is an open access article under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial License, which permits use, distribution and reproduction in any medium, provided the original work is properly cited and is not used for commercial purposes.

Introduction

The global population is currently experiencing a rapid increase in the proportion of older adults.According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), between 2015 and 2050, the percentage of individuals aged 60 and above is expected to nearly double, from 12% to 22% [1].Haematological malignancies are more prevalent in older adults.A recent study indicates that the median age of diagnosis for acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) is 69 years, and 70 years for myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) [2,3].Allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) offers curative potential for many haematological disorders.However, due to associated toxicities, not all older adults are suitable candidates for this treatment.Although various studies have explored methods to assess patients' fitness, they each involve different patient populations, healthcare settings and treatment policies.In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis to examine the demographic and geriatric characteristics, transplant-related factors and clinical outcome predictors for older adults who underwent alloHSCT in a single institution.


Method


Patients, Transplant Care and Geriatric Characteristics

This retrospective analysis included consecutive patients aged 60 years or older who underwent reduced intensity conditioning (RIC) alloHSCT in Singapore General Hospital from January 2017 to December 2023.All patients provided written consent pretransplant.Demographic data, disease, transplant characteristics and survival data were retrieved from the institutional transplant registry database.We systematically reviewed transplant protocols and pre-transplant clinical notes from physicians, nursing staff, transplant coordinators and allied healthcare providers to collect data on geriatric characteristics including Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G), Cockcroft-Gault equation creatinine clearance (CrCl), serum albumin, body mass index (BMI) and the number of chronic medications.Pre-transplant data closest to HSCT admission were used if data were available on multiple time points.Geriatric characteristics deficit cut-off was based on existing literature [4,5].


Statistical Methods

Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier curves, while non-relapse mortality (NRM) was estimated using the cumulative incidence method for competing risk.Relapse or progression of disease was considered a competing risk for NRM.The association of clinical, transplant and geriatric characteristics with survival outcomes was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards model.

Multivariate analysis was built with known risk factors and all univariate covariates of p < 0.1.For NRM, additional univariate and multivariate analyses were done using Fine-Gray proportional subhazards model, taking into consideration competing risks.All statistical analyses were performed with Stata software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).


Result


Patient's Characteristics

A total of 66 patients were included in the study.The median age at the time of transplant was 65 years (range 60-73).Of these, 28 patients (42%) were aged over 65.The cohort consisted of 48% males and 52% females.Most patients had acute leukaemia (61%), followed by MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm (26%) and lymphoproliferative diseases (14%).The median number of prior lines of treatment was one, with 38% of patients having received two or more lines of treatment.

Regarding donor source, 18% had a matched sibling donor (MSD), 33% had a matched unrelated donor (MUD) and 48% had a haploidentical donor.All patients underwent conditioning with RIC prior to alloHSCT, with the two most commonly used regimens being fludarabine/melphalan-based (53%) and fludarabine/busulphan-based (35%).Fludarabine, used in combination with other agents, was generally dosed at 40 mg/m 2 /day for 4 days, or 30 mg/m 2 /day for 5 days, and adjusted according to renal function.Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis was with either tacrolimus or cyclosporin A. Patients with MUD also received anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) as part of GVHD prophylaxis.Among the patients who had stem cell transplant from haploidentical donors, 7    of > 7. A total of 12% of patients had renal insufficiency (defined as CrCl below 60 mL/min), including one patient with normal renal function at baseline who developed renal failure requiring continuous renal replacement therapy during conditioning chemotherapy due to complications of neutropaenic sepsis.In addition, 53% of patients had low serum albumin, defined as < 35 g/L, and 18% were underweight (defined as a BMI < 18.5).The median number of chronic medications taken was 5, with 29% of patients using ≥ 9 medications (Table 1).Frailty scores, such as HCT frailty score and CHARM score, were not used due to inadequate geriatric assessment data in this retrospective study [13,14].


Transplant Outcome

With a median follow-up of 32.5 months, 28 patients had died.Nineteen patients experienced disease relapse, which resulted in 14 deaths among them.In addition, 14 patients (21%) experienced NRM.The most common causes of NRM were infections (7 patients), secondary cancers (2 patients) and intracranial haemorrhage (2 patients).There was one mortality each of GVHD, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and one unknown cause.The 2-year PFS rate was 55%, while the 2-year OS rate was 58% (Figure 1).


Impact of Geriatric Characteristics on Survival

On univariate analysis, estimated using the cumulative incidence method for competing risks, age > 65 years was associated with a higher risk of NRM (HR 4.39, p = 0.013).Similarly, renal insufficiency was linked to an increased risk of NRM (HR 7.62, p < 0.001).Notably, both variables remained significant on multivariate analysis using Cox proportional hazards model (Table 2).On top of age > 65 and CrCl, HCT-CI, CIRS-G and albumin were added for NRM multivariate analysis.None of HCT-CI, CIRS-G and albumin were significant factors.An alternative model using Fine-Gray proportional subhazards was also tested for NRM, considering relapse or progression of disease as competing risk.In this model, CrCl was associated with inferior NRM in univariate analysis but lost its significance on multivariate analysis (Table 3).Meanwhile, age > 65 remained significant in both univariate and multivariate analysis using both models.

In terms of PFS, age > 65 years (HR 2.44, p = 0.012), renal insufficiency (HR 6.01, p < 0.001) and low serum albumin (HR 2.11, p = 0.04) were associated with inferior PFS.However, on multivariate analysis, only renal insufficiency retained its significance for PFS (HR 5.71, p = 0.006) (Table S1).

For OS, both age > 65 years (HR 3.84, p = 0.001) and renal insufficiency (HR 5.20, p < 0.001) were significant on univariate analysis.On multivariate analysis, both age > 65 years (HR 2.75, p = 0.03) and renal insufficiency (HR 3.46, p = 0.01) remained significant for OS.None of the other functional, comorbid, or geriatric characteristics were associated with OS.Patients aged > 65 years had a 2-year OS rate of 33%, compared to 76% in those aged < 65 years.Similarly, patients with renal insufficiency had a 2-year OS rate of 13%, compared to 65% in those without renal insufficiency (Table 4) (Figure 2).


Discussion

In this study, we report a 2-year NRM of 21% and a 2-year OS rate of 58% in patients with a median age of 65 years.These results are comparable to studies from other groups focusing on older adults who underwent alloHSCT (  Although it is not clear which specific ageing factors are driving the higher risks of NRM and lower OS in our study, we hypothesise that certain unmeasured geriatric-related deficits may be contributing to the observed statistics.Multiple studies have shown that comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) can detect occult geriatric deficits, allowing for timely correction and intervention.CGA evaluates seven major domains: physical/functional, cognition, mental health, nutrition, polypharmacy, comorbidity and social support.Prospective studies have shown that more detailed physical/functional assessments, such as instrumental activities of daily living and the timed up-andgo test, are predictive of alloHSCT outcomes [4,15].Other studies have found that cognitive impairment and poor mental health are associated with inferior outcomes [4,6].In our study, although low albumin was associated with inferior PFS on univariate analysis, it did not retain significance on multivariate analysis and did not affect NRM or OS.We also did not find an association between polypharmacy and survival.Incorporating CGA results may allow targeted and individualised interventions before and during transplant, potentially improving overall outcomes.A study from Spain demonstrated that prehabilitation benefited some patients who were pre-frail or frail at baseline, allowing them to become fitter by the time of alloHSCT admission [16].

Our study revealed that renal insufficiency, defined as CrCl < 60 mL/min, is a strong predictor of NRM and OS.Out of 8 patients with renal insufficiency, 7 died, with 4 succumbing to NRM (3 due to infection, 1 due to intracranial haemorrhage).Using Fine-Gray proportional subhazards model factoring in relapse or progression of disease as competing risk, CrCl was associated with inferior NRM on univariate analysis but not on multivariate analysis.This is likely due to a higher relapse rate, as 3 out of 8 patients within this group (38%) experienced relapse and died subsequently.A second explanation could be related to the small sample size of our study.A case series showed that 3 out of 5 patients with renal insufficiency experienced NRM post-alloHSCT [17].Farhadfar et al. from CIBMTR reported a large study of more than 1300 patients, showing that an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min was associated with higher NRM and inferior OS.These patients also experienced a higher risk of renal failure requiring dialysis post-alloHSCT [18].

The association between renal insufficiency and worse survival likely results from the complex physiological and pharmacological changes that occur post-alloHSCT.A variety of drugs are used during this period, including conditioning chemotherapy, calcineurin inhibitors for GVHD prophylaxis and antimicrobial agents for infection prevention or treatment.Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of these drugs may be significantly altered in the setting of renal insufficiency, leading to overdosing or underdosing, which may worsen as renal function deteriorates post-alloHSCT [19].Further studies are needed to refine dosing strategies for conditioning regimens and GVHD prophylaxis to improve outcomes in alloHSCT patients with renal insufficiency at baseline.Both HCT-CI and CIRS-G include renal function as part of their comorbidity scores, but these indices use absolute serum creatinine values without considering age, body mass, or gender variability.

In our institution, we routinely use HCT-CI and KPS to assess patients' fitness for alloHSCT.However, neither of these assessments was predictive of NRM or OS in this study, indicating their prognostic limitations in older adults.A study from Germany also did not find HCT-CI to be predictive of alloHSCT outcomes in older adults [20], and this finding was similarly supported by a multinational European study [7].KPS lacks sufficient granularity in assessing patients with mild to moderate functional deficits, which are common in older adults.A study from the Cooperative German Transplant Study Group did not find KPS to be a reliable predictor of alloHSCT outcomes [21], a finding corroborated by outcomes from the Weill Cornell Medical Center [8].

Our study has several limitations.As a retrospective study, the data were subjected to physician bias in the selection of transplant candidates, and hence might not reflect the true population of older adults with haematological malignancies.As data were obtained through existing registry and electronic records, important geriatric characteristics including physical function test, cognition evaluation, self-rated health score and psychosocial markers were mostly not available.As a result, the incompleteness of data collected may affect our findings.In addition, our cohort was considered small and would reduce the statistical power of our analysis.

In conclusion, alloHSCT is a feasible and important treatment option with curative potential for older adults with haematological malignancies.However, the selection of appropriate recipients is critical due to the high variability in fitness among older adults.CGA offers a multidimensional assessment, enabling interventions to be made prior to transplant.Future studies are needed to standardise assessment tools and develop optimal schedules for CGA, as well as to guide modifications in transplant treatment.
