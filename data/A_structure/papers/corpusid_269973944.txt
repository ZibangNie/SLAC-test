Concerns about methane (CH 4 ) emissions from rice, a staple sustaining over 3.5 billion people globally, are heightened due to its status as the second-largest contributor to greenhouse gases, driving climate change.Accurate quantification of CH 4 emissions from rice fields is crucial for understanding gas concentrations.Leveraging technological advancements, we present a groundbreaking solution that integrates machine learning and remote sensing data, challenging traditional closed chamber methods.To achieve this, our methodology involves extensive data collection using drones equipped with a Micasense Altum camera and ground sensors, effectively reducing reliance on labor-intensive and costly field sampling.In this experimental project, our research delves into the intricate relationship between environmental variables, such as soil conditions and weather patterns, and CH 4 emissions.We achieved remarkable results by utilizing unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV) and evaluating over 20 regression models, emphasizing an R 2 value of 0.98 and 0.95 for the training and testing data, respectively.This outcome designates the random forest regressor as the most suitable model with superior predictive capabilities.Notably, phosphorus, GRVI median, and cumulative soil and water temperature emerged as the model's fittest variables for predicting these values.Our findings underscore an innovative, cost-effective, and efficient alternative for quantifying CH 4 emissions, marking a significant advancement in the technologydriven approach to evaluating rice growth parameters and vegetation indices, providing valuable insights for advancing gas emissions studies in rice paddies.

Introduction

The ever-increasing global population presents a formidable challenge to the agricultural system.Forestry and land use contribute to about 20% of atmospheric greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations.This significant contribution involves non-CO 2 gases like methane (CH 4 ) and nitrous oxide (N 2 O) (FAO, 2022;Naiyer & Abbas, 2022).Radiatively active gases, including carbon dioxide (CO 2 ), CH 4 , nitrous oxide (N 2 O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6), are crucial players in trapping heat in the atmosphere (Reed & Stringer, 2016;Naiyer & Abbas, 2022).

Within the intricate realm of greenhouse gas emissions, methane (CH 4 ) stands as the second most potent contributor to global warming, boasting an exchange ratio of 28 over a 100-year period (IPCC, 2022;Zhang et al., 2022).Its infrared radiation absorption capacity exceeds that of carbon dioxide by 30 times, with two primary oxidation pathways: atmospheric photochemical reactions and biological processes in terrestrial and aquatic environments.Rice (Oryza sativa), a staple food worldwide, plays a significant role in CH 4 emissions due to cultivation practices and field inundation, which create anaerobic conditions conducive to substantial CH 4 release (Singh et al., 2022(Singh et al., , 2023)).These environmental conditions strongly influence soil CH 4 emissions by shaping the community and activity of methanogens and methanotrophs in paddy fields.Presently, various management practices, such as altering irrigation regimes from continuous flooding to alternative wetting and drying, coupled with biochar application, have markedly affected soil CH 4 production and oxidation processes, thus impacting CH 4 emissions in paddy fields (Gu et al., 2022;Iboko et al., 2023).According to Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates for 2022, rice cultivation contributes approximately 9-10% (5-38 Tg CH 4 yr-1) of global methane emissions, underscoring its significant environmental footprint on a global scale (FAO, 2022).

It is imperative to identify and quantify greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Jiang et al., 2019), and aligning with the sector-specific requirements is crucial (IPCC, 2022).However, several methodologies have been proposed for estimating CH 4 emissions in agriculture, including statistical chamber-gas chromatography, eddy covariance, and mechanistic and empirical regression models (Hu et al., 2014;Yue et al., 2019).Recently, data-intensive approaches in agricultural technology, precision agriculture, and digital agriculture have emerged to enhance agricultural productivity while minimizing environmental impact.Under this paradigm, satellite remote sensing technology has been explored as a viable option for offering precise and spatially detailed insights into CH 4 emissions, addressing the inherent challenges of monitoring methane emissions from wetlands and paddy fields with conventional methods (Cusworth et al., 2024;Liakos et al., 2018;McQueen et al., 1995;Singh et al., 2022;Tamiminia et al., 2020).However, the spatial and temporal resolution limitations in satellite remote sensing for local fields prompt using alternative methods like drones and aircraft imaging spectrometers to estimate agriculture parameters at local scales (Alvarez-Mendoza et al., 2022).These methods allow the precise estimation of agricultural parameters at regional scales and the meticulous measurement of principal sources of CH 4 emissions (Duren et al., 2019).

For this reason, employing a forecasting model presents a viable option for predicting CH 4 emissions from paddy fields, particularly through the use of ML-based models to enhance accuracy (Alvarez-Mendoza et al., 2020).This approach is crucial as it enables the efficient processing of complex and voluminous datasets, allowing for the identification of patterns that are not easily detectable through traditional methods.This strategy is essential for improving farming practices, thereby benefiting the environment and promoting sustainable agriculture.Consequently, the specific objectives of this study are outlined as follows:

1. Conduct a comprehensive analysis of environmental variables, with a focus on climatic data gathered from field sensors and drone-based sources, utilizing vegetation indices as a fundamental component in developing a GHG prediction model applicable to local scales in paddy fields.2. Achieve superior accuracy compared to existing studies that depend on satellite remote sensing for their CH4 modeling approaches.


Materials and methods


Study area and field experiment design

The study was conducted at the Alliance's regional hub for the Americas (N 3° 29′ 43.289′′, W 76° 21′10.62′′),Palmira, Colombia (Fig. 1).The site is in a dry tropical region with an annual temperature of 25-26 °C.It experiences yearly precipitation levels ranging from 1000 to 1200 mm and solar radiation from 4.5 to 5.5 kWh per square meter daily.At this location, three pools, covering approximately 380 m 2 , were established to collect GHG emissions data.The pools were planted with the FED-EARROZ 60 (F60) variety, chosen due to its widespread use in the rice sector.Closed chambers were positioned for GHG data collection, with one chamber installed for each transplanted line, resulting in three lines per pool.Sampling was conducted weekly for an hour, with a 20-min interval between samples.Samples were taken at 0, 20, 40, and 60 min for each line.To ensure consistent water depth between 2 and 4 cm in each rice pool, a 396-m 2 reservoir was employed.The NAS-300A equipment was utilized to regulate the desired water level in each pool.Additionally, each pool was equipped with one Ted Gateway (e-kakashi) device, which collected weather data, including air temperature, relative humidity, solar radiation, soil temperature, water temperature, and water depth.


Dataset collection

The first crop cycle was transplanted on March 30, 2022, with the subsequent cycle following on October 3, 2022.Harvests occurred on July 2, 2022, and January 25, 2023, approximately 94 and 114 days after transplanting.Closed gas chambers were installed on the same date as transplanting, and greenhouse gas (GHG) data were collected once a week throughout all crop cycles until harvest (refer to section "GHG emissions" for GHG emissions).Simultaneously, environmental variables were measured using the e-Kakashi device (refer to section "Environmental variables (weather variables)" for environmental variables), and multispectral drone images were captured using the Micasense Altum camera (refer to section "Drone data" for drone data).Before sowing, a soil analysis was conducted to assess the soil's chemical conditions, including available phosphorus (P bray), cationic exchange capacity (CIC), organic matter (MO), nitrate (NO 3 ), and ammonium (NH 4 ).

Maximum tillering, panicle initiation, booting, flowering, and phenological maturity were recorded for each stage (see Fig. 2).


GHG emissions

Throughout the study, we measured GHG emissions from the F60 variety using the closed chamber method.Nine chambers were strategically placed over each line in the three pools (one by line).Data collection commenced after transplantation to establish a baseline of GHG emissions under the specified conditions.Intensive sampling of GHG emissions was carried out for three consecutive days following each fertilization event.The first two fertilizations occurred during the vegetative stage of the crop's development, with the final one occurring 40 days after the second fertilization, during the reproductive stage.On days without fertilization, gas samples were collected once a week up to 1 week before the harvest.

The chambers, measuring 78 cm in height and 45 cm in diameter, have a capacity of 114 L. To ensure air homogeneity, each chamber has a batterypowered fan and a thermometer monitoring temperature (see Fig. 3).For gas sampling, a three-way valve is attached to one side of the chamber, allowing the collection of gas samples through hypodermic needles.The concentrations of CH 4 and N 2 O were calculated using Eq. 1.

Machine learning models


Auto-machine learning models (Pycaret)

The study utilized the PyCaret library to compare different machine-learning models and identify the most effective model based on key performance metrics such as R 2 , RMSE, and MAE.Table 2 provides details on the predictors about the predictors used in the input dataset.The advent of Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) libraries, such as PyCaret, marks a significant advancement in agricultural technology.PyCaret streamlines the model development process with this user-friendly API, making machine learning more accessible and contributing to its democratization within the farming sector.

The primary focus was on regression models, a subset of supervised machine learning algorithms capable of predicting continuous numerical outcomes.These models, including linear regression, polynomial regression, ridge regression, Lasso regression, support vector regression (SVR), decision tree regression, random forest regression, and gradient-boosting regression, establish relationships between independent and dependent variables to facilitate accurate predictions (Benos et al., 2021;Cedric et al., 2022;Razzaq, 2020;Sarangpure et al., 2023).

Results


Dataset and comparative analysis

First, we get orthomosaics for each date to extract the remote sensing variables from the dataset.Thus, we can compare the different variables visually and spectrally in GIS software.For example, we have lower values of NDVI during the first stages of the crop compared with the last stage (Fig. 6).Additionally, we obtained thermal maps for each stage using the thermal band (B6) in Kelvin degrees, getting higher values of superficial temperature during the first stage compared with the last stage of the crop (Fig. 7).

The dataset contains 317 records divided by line, with data collected for 28 days in the first cycle and 37 days in the second cycle.In the first part of the study, the different variables were compared with CH 4 emissions during the cycles.

Figure 8 illustrates the variation of CH 4 emissions throughout different stages of rice growth: vegetative, reproductive, and ripening.Particularly noteworthy are the significant emissions observed between the reproductive and ripening stages.The figure further highlights the relationship between variables influencing CH 4 emissions in paddy fields, such as soil temperature GDD, air temperature GDD, and water temperature GDD.These variables play a significant role in microbial processes, subsequently influencing CH 4 emissions.Additionally, the GRVI index has demonstrated its predictive capability for CH 4 emissions in paddy fields.

When evaluating predictors, two variables emerge as a potential candidate for estimating CH 4 emissions: "CH4_d_median" and "CH4_d_cum."The former represents the daily CH 4 estimates using the median statistic over the sampling period, while the latter reflects the cumulative daily CH 4 logarithm.Both variables are integral to estimating CH 4 emissions, prompting the question of determining the more suitable variable for constructing accurate predictive models.

We used the PyCaret library to estimate methane emissions in rice crops by applying more than 20 different regression models to the target variables "CH4_d_median" and "CH4_d_cum."We trained and validated the models using a cross-validation approach with 70% training and 30% test data partition.The dataset, comprising 317 records, underwent preprocessing steps, including cleaning missing values, removing duplicates, and eliminating outliers.Subsequently, the data was normalized for reliability.

Before creating the model, we conducted correlation and significance analyses to understand the relationships between environmental and remote sensing variables, aiding in determining the target variable for methane emission.Table 3 presents the performance metrics, such as MAE, MSE, RMSE, R 2 , and MAPE, for each regression model applied to "CH4_d_median" and "CH4_d_cum."These metrics are crucial in assessing each model's accuracy and predictive capability.

The comparison presented in Table 3 comprehensively evaluates how accurately each model predicts the two target variables.These results provide an unbiased assessment of each model's performance, determining which variable is more effective in predicting methane emissions during rice cultivation.


Model building and evaluation

To select the most suitable model for forecasting methane emissions in rice crops, with a particular emphasis on the variable "CH 4 _d_cum," we prioritized the identification of patterns and relationships within the dataset.


Model selection and correlation analysis

The initial correlation analysis in Fig. 9 revealed different levels of relationships between the target variable and predictor variables, including P Bray (measured in "P_Bray_mg_kg"), solar radiation median (measured in "Solar_Rad_median"), and soil temperature median (measured in "Soil_temp_median").This analysis provided a deeper understanding of the linear relationships present in the data.


Data partitioning and model evaluation

As mentioned, the dataset contains 317 records, divided into 70% for training and 30% for validation.This partitioning was crucial for accurately assessing  Vol:.( 1234567890) the model's performance using key metrics.We extensively evaluated 20 regression models available on the PyCaret platform, and the results are summarized in Table 4.The models were ranked based on performance and assessed using internal cross-validation using the k-fold method with ten folds.This approach aided in identifying the most suitable models while minimizing the risk of overfitting.Consequently, the random forest regressor emerged as the top-performing model, achieving an R 2 coefficient of approximately 0.805, an MAE of 0.08, and a MAPE of just 2.1%.The Catboost regressor secured the second-best, boasting an R 2 of 0.785, an MAE of 0.09, and a MAPE of 1.8%.Finally, the AdaBoost regressor secured the third spot with an R 2 of 0.77, an MAE of 0.10, and a MAPE close to 1.0%.This rigorous process ensures the selection of the most robust predictive models following their training.


Estimation models and validations

After selecting the optimal model for the entire dataset, we trained and validated it using the 70% and 30% partitions to assess its performance.

Therefore, the results obtained from the best model, the random forest regressor, are detailed in Fig. 10.

The random forest regressor model has shown remarkable performance, with a coefficient of determination R 2 of 0.98 during the training phase.This implies that the model has effectively adapted to the variability of the data.In the testing phase, the model achieves an R 2 of 0.95, indicating its remarkable Ability to generalize information to previously unseen data sets.These R 2 metrics imply that the model is suitable for identifying and predicting emerging patterns and relationships in the data.

In Fig. 11, the learning and validation graphs demonstrate how the training and cross-validation curves converge as the model complexity or training time increases.An acceptable sign is observed as the cross-validation curve moves closer to the training curve with the utilization of more data, indicating the model's potential to perform as well on unseen data sets as on the training data, given sufficient data.When the two curves are reasonably close, it suggests that the model has reduced variance and is robust.Therefore, the two graphs serve as evidence of the model's robustness.It is crucial to emphasize the most influential training variables that affected the predictions of the chosen model (as shown in Fig. 12).Among all the variables, phosphorus, GRVI median, soil temperature, and water temperature accumulative were identified as the most important factors in predicting the model results.


Discussion

Efforts to enhance efficiency and sustainability in agriculture rely heavily on monitoring and predicting greenhouse gas emissions, with a particular focus on methane.Rice cultivation, contributing substantially to CH 4 emissions due to the anaerobic conditions in flooded fields, underscores-the importance of monitoring and predicting CH 4 emissions for sustainable agriculture.Accurate estimation of CH 4 emissions in paddy fields is crucial for effective farm management, utilizing models derived from environmental and remote sensing variables.

Despite the perception that agricultural and environmental models may have linear characteristics compared to other predictors, integrating vegetation index data with ground truth data poses a challenge  due to its non-linear dimensional nature.To overcome this challenge, we have developed a machine learning (ML) model that establishes a connection between environmental and UAV data, testing various algorithms to predict CH 4 emissions.Building on the success of previous studies by Abbasi et al. (2021), Glenn et al. (2021), and Jiang et al. (2023), which employed AI models for predicting GHG emissions, our innovative approach integrates cutting-edge technologies.This makes it a viable alternative to statistical methods (tier 1) and process-based models (tier 3) reported by IPCC in 2019 and 2022.

In evaluating the model's accuracy and the relevance of predictors, our model achieved a high R 2 value of 0.95 and a low RMSE with the random forest algorithm.The success of our approach can be attributed to the use of cross-validation (k-fold method) within the AutoML tool, a strategy effective in mitigating potential issues related to overfitting.This method, superior to classical approaches or using the entire dataset, enabled us to train the model with 70% of the data and test it with the remaining 30% (Ghojogh & Crowley, 2019;Mohr & Van Rijn, 2023).

The results illustrated in Figs. 9 and 12 highlight the significance of CH 4 predictors.Figure 10 identifies phosphorus as a critical variable, exerting a substantial impact on CH 4 prediction.As shown in the correlation plot (Fig. 9), phosphorus has a negative correlation, suggesting that an increase in soil phosphorus could decrease CH 4 emissions into the atmosphere.Besides phosphorus, other predictors such as the vegetation index, specifically GRVI, and environmental factors like soil GDD, air GDD, and water GDD, play significant roles in influencing CH 4 levels.These variables are positively correlated with CH 4 , indicating that an increase in these factors corresponds to a rise in CH 4 emissions.Incorporating soil conditions into our model to establish the relationship between soil and CH 4 emissions, following the examples of DNDC models (Minamikawa et al., 2014) emerged as a significant determinant, enhancing predictions of CH 4 emissions along with other variables such as the GRVI median from UAV data, soil temperature, and cumulative water temperature (Fig. 12).Including available phosphorous in the soil proved crucial, significantly improving the accuracy of CH 4 emission predictions.This relationship aligns with the understanding of biochemical cycles linked to methanogenesis activity.

Studies by Lu et al. (1999) emphasized the direct impact of soil-available phosphorus on microbial activity and crop physiology, primarily through root exudates and structural changes that facilitate CH 4 transport.Additionally, Hou et al. (2020) found that fertilization without P increases CH 4 emissions compared to N and P fertilization.Conrad and Klose (2005) demonstrated that fertilization with potassium phosphate in low P stimulates CH 4 emissions by improving root methanogenesis.This finding underscores the significance of P in sustainable agriculture practices and climate change mitigation actions.They also call for a comprehensive reevaluation of current nutrient management guidelines to include the influence of P on CH 4 emissions.However, it is essential to note that the effect of P on CH 4 emissions may vary according to soil type, carbon and nutrition level, and water management, necessitating further exploration (Datta et al., 2013).

On the weather front, we diligently monitored variables across a 24-h cycle to comprehend the variations between diurnal and nocturnal conditions.Additionally, we leveraged GDD as an agroclimatic measure to gauge the growth impact and calculate CH 4 emissions.Previous studies have underscored the significance of GDD in predicting CH 4 emissions (Rehder et al., 2023;Rößger et al., 2022), given its direct influence on methanogenesis activity (Stams et al., 2019;Xu et al., 2022).In remote sensing predictors, the GRVI median holds significant importance.Previous satellite remote sensing studies have indicated that different vegetation indices, such as NDVI and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), can achieve R 2 values nearing 0.6 (Shi et al., 2020;Arai et al., 2018).Notably, our method demonstrates higher predictive accuracy, capitalizing on the superior characteristics of UAV remote sensing data to the evaluation of vegetation indices for precision agriculture, including enhanced spatial and temporal resolution compared with satellite remote sensing (Fig. 13).This affirms the efficacy of our approach in achieving more precise and insightful predictions.

This study presents a technology-driven solution that tackles the intricacies of precise evaluation and addresses the imperative to accurately quantify annual CH 4 emissions from rice paddies.It offers a cost-effective and efficient alternative to conventional data collection methods.Liu et al. (2023) assert that accurately quantifying annual CH 4 emissions from rice paddies is vital for comprehending and mitigating agricultural changes.However, the precise evaluation of CH 4 emissions remains a complex endeavor (Chaichana et al., 2018;Tariq et al., 2017).To strengthen the model's transferability, we propose that future studies involve testing under various soil and climatic conditions, thus ensuring that the model can be adjusted and recalibrated.This will help verify the model's robustness and versatility and expand its applicability globally, as suggested by Liakos et al. (2018), who highlight the importance of testing machine learning models across various agricultural scenarios to ensure their practical utility and reliability.


Conclusion

In conclusion, our model represents a groundbreaking technology-driven solution that addresses data collection's financial and logistical challenges, particularly compared to the conventional closed chamber method.Implementing remote sensing data acquisition has minimized the need for extensive field sampling and laborious laboratory analysis, significantly reducing cost and time investment.Our research findings underscore the efficacy of utilizing UAV multispectral imagery and environmental data, offering a robust framework for evaluating rice growth parameters and vegetation indices.This approach streamlines the assessment process and provides invaluable insights for advancing studies on gas emissions studies in rice paddies.


Future prospectives

Integrating an ML-based model for CH 4 predictions in paddy fields holds great potential for addressing climate change challenges.By implementing this, researchers can effectively create maps and identify zones with high emissions to establish agronomical practices that allow the reduction of this GHG gas in their fields.Moreover, ML-based models can aid the development of regional and global models to predict future methane emissions under various scenarios.The development of this model must be aligned with the policies and requirements of stakeholders aiming to meet international climate targets and ensure food security.
