Ultrasonic Sensor-based Water Leveling for Three-dimensional Water Phantom: Prototype Development
3E0DDBE35C571966E8BFFCE85BCD486510.4103/jmp.jmp_60_24Received on: 20-03-2024 Review completed on: 22-05-2024 Accepted on: 22-05-2024Three-dimensional water phantomquality assuranceultrasonic sensorswater leveling
Objectives:The purpose of this study was to develop a prototype for controlling the water level of a three-dimensional (3D) water phantom using ultrasound sensors and Arduino technology and evaluate its performance in setting up the 3D water phantom for radiation beam measurements.Materials and Methods: A prototype consisted of an Arduino Nano board and two types of ultrasound sensors (US015 and SR04).The accuracy of both sensors was tested at various distances and the performance was evaluated through statistical analysis.The distance measurement test was performed rigorously at intervals of 2 cm from 5 cm to 21 cm, measuring an average error and a maximum deviation for each sensor.Results: Both sensors demonstrated the measurement accuracy within 2 mm.When using the traditional and prototype-based setup methods, the measured photon and electron beam profiles did not show any significant difference.This result suggests the equivalent setup capability when using these two different 3D water phantom setup methods.Conclusion: The ultrasound sensor-based prototype is demonstrated as a more effective device in maintaining the 3D water phantom setup consistently compared to the traditional method, which is prone to human error, and it will aid in facilitating precise phantom setup during the commissioning and routine quality assurance (QA) of linear accelerators in radiotherapy clinics.

IntroductIon

Radiation therapy is a precise medical procedure that utilizes high-energy radiation to treat diseases such as cancer.The success of this treatment relies on accurate measurement and control of the radiation dose and distribution.Recent advances in computer processing power and technological support have led to the development of various radiation therapy modalities. [1]To use these modalities in radiotherapy clinics, accurate beam modeling and validation procedures are essential.When performing the beam modeling procedure, a three-dimensional (3D) water phantom acts as one of the crucial dosimetry devices to measure and evaluate radiation beam characteristics such as percent depth dose (PDD), and beam flatness/symmetry.

The commissioning process of a linear accelerator is a time-consuming endeavor.Evaluation of radiation beam characteristics is primarily conducted using the 3D water phantom.During this stage, parameters such as PDD, beam profiles, and output characterization are measured.

These measurements are essential for the commissioning of a radiotherapy machine in Treatment Planning Systems.Furthermore, medical physicists perform periodic quality assurance (QA) to maintain the continuous performance of linear accelerators.This QA process involves to check dosimetric, mechanical, imaging accuracy, and equipment stability.AAPM Task Group Report 142 [2] categorizes these QA procedures into daily, weekly, monthly, and annual checks, providing tolerance limits for each parameter.Accurate measurement of radiation beams requires standardized measurement methods and protocols.Reference calibration protocols such as IAEA TRS-398 and AAPM TG-51 demand radiation beam measurement accuracy within 1% annually in water. [3]Traditional setups for the 3D water phantom often rely on the subjective adjustment of the phantom's frame and an ionization chamber holder.

Recently, there has been a release of water phantom [4][5][6] incorporating autoleveling functionality such as the Beam-Scan (PTW, Freiburg, Germany) and 3D scanner (Sun Nuclear, Melbourne, FL) with automated leveling features to mitigate such subjective dependencies, albeit with challenges related to their cost and adoption.This study evaluates the accuracy of cost-effective ultrasonic sensors and develops a prototype applicable to the 3D water phantom using ultrasonic sensors and Arduino technology.Through the use of two types of ultrasonic sensors, the study adjusts the water phantom's surface level and confirms the difference depending on the setup method by measuring beam profiles for photon and electron beams.


materIals and methods


Principle of ultrasonic sensor measurement

The ultrasonic sensors [7][8][9] depicted in Figure 1 were used for water level measurements: US015 (left, green) and SR04 (right, blue).The US015 measures 45 mm × 20 mm × 12 mm, with a range of 2-400 cm and a resolution of 1 mm + 1%.The SR04 measures 45 mm × 20 mm × 15 mm, with a range of 2-400 cm and a resolution of 3 mm.Each sensor consists of four separate pins: VCC (power supply, 5V), Trig (trigger input pin), Echo (receiver output pin), and GND (power ground).A sound wave, emitted from the transmitter (trigger signal) at a frequency of 40 kHz, propagates outward and returns as a reflected wave after interacting with the medium in front.The reflected wave is received by the receiver (echo signal).The prototype measures the phase difference of signals from the ultrasonic sensors generated at the transmitter and receiver.

To calculate the distance, we utilize the following equation, [10] which incorporates temperature compensation: d = (331.5+ 0.61 t) × T where d represents the distance, t denotes the air temperature measured in degrees Celsius, and T signifies the time taken for the ultrasonic wave between the target and receiver.This integration of ultrasonic sensors, coupled with precise phase difference measurement, enables us to develop a robust and accurate system for monitoring a water level, which is crucial for various applications ranging from industrial processes to environmental monitoring.


Prototype design for water leveling

The prototype was constructed using ultrasonic sensors and Arduino Nano board, as illustrated in Figure 2. The Arduino Nano board (CH340) is a surface mount breadboard integrated with a micro-USB port, based on the ATmega328 microcontroller, featuring 14 digital I/O pins and 8 analog reference pins (A0-A7).It operates within a voltage range of 7-12 V and a clock frequency of 16 MHz.

The output data from the ultrasonic sensors can be monitored through the serial monitor provided by the Arduino software.A liquid crystal display (LCD) graphics display module (Nokia 5110) was employed to display the distance from the ultrasonic sensor to the medium [Figure 2a].The screen has a resolution of 48 × 84 pixels and consists of eight types of modules.Configuration of the modules includes VCC (power supply from 2.7 to 3.3 V), GND (ground), SCE (enable to control operation of controllers), RES (reset for LCD operation, providing 0 V), D/C (configure data formats between data mode and command mode), DIN (serial Both ultrasonic sensors (US015, HC SR04) operate at 5 V; however, a 9 V battery was used to power the prototype due to differences in operating voltage among components such as the Arduino Nano board and LCD display.Additional resistors (1K ohm, 10K ohm) were connected to meet the allowable voltage of each component of the prototype.The circuit was simulated using Fritzing software before the actual production.


Ultrasonic sensor testing and beam profile assessment

First, we evaluated the distance measurement accuracy of the sensors (US015 and SR04) using the setup depicted in Figure 3.An acrylic medium with a thickness of 5 cm, capable of reflecting ultrasonic waves, was utilized.Before commencing the distance measurement, the transceivers of each sensor were aligned with the surface of the medium.Subsequently, the medium was moved along the measuring distance to evaluate the uncertainty of distance measurement with the ultrasonic sensors.

Second, the Bluephantom2 (IBA-Dosimetry, Schwarzenbruck, Germany) 3D water phantom was positioned with the prototype [referred to as HC015 and SR04, as shown in Figure 4c] and the traditional setup method using isocenter alignment cap for a field detector [referred to as a reference, as shown in Figure 4a] was employed to evaluate the flatness and symmetry of the beam energy.The traditional water phantom setup involved adjusting the phantom frame [indicated by the red circle in Figure 4b] and installing the alignment holder for the ionization chamber (CC013, IBA dosimetry) to ensure flatness using microleveling tool [indicated by the blue circle in Figure 4b].

We investigated the influence of water level adjustments in the Blue Phantom on profile variation by comparing measurement results obtained from setups using crosshairs marked on the chamber holder and setups produced through each ultrasonic sensor in the prototype.The radiation beam measurement conditions included a source to surface distance (SSD) of 100 cm, with field sizes of 20 cm × 20 cm and 25 cm × 25 cm for 6MV photon beam [Figure 5], and electron cones of sizes A20 and A25 for the 6 MeV electron beam [Figure 6], respectively.

results


Ultrasonic sensor performance

The accuracy of two types of ultrasonic sensors was evaluated at various distances in the medium from 5 to 21 cm and the results are summarized in Table 1.Each sensor underwent ten repeated measurements at a room temperature of 23°C and the ultrasonic speed was set to 345.3 m/s on the Arduino Nano board, calculated based on the indoor temperature.For the US015 sensor, the error range was between 0.07 and 0.16 cm, with the maximum error (0.23 cm) occurring at 11 cm and the minimum error (0.01 cm) observed at 17 cm.For the SR04 sensor, the error range was between 0.16 and 0.32 cm, with the maximum error observed at 21 cm and the minimum error at 9 cm.The root mean square error (RMSE) for the US15 sensor was calculated to be 0.098 cm, and for the SR04, it was 0.189 cm.Based on the standard deviation and RMSE of the measurement error, the US15 sensor exhibits better performance in the distance measurement compared to the SR04 sensor.


Evaluation of profiles based on setup methods

We used a newly developed prototype [Figure 2b] to adjust the water level in the 3D water phantom.The OmniPro-Accept software (v7.5, IBA dosimetry) was utilized to evaluate all beam profiles while maintaining a consistent distance of 9 cm between the sensor and the water surface during the beam profile measurements with the prototype to ensure measurement accuracy.For photon beams, a measurement depth of 10 cm was set for 6 MV photon beams, as illustrated in Figure 5. Conversely, for electron beams with an energy   of 6 MeV, measurements were taken at a depth of 1.4 cm, corresponding to the D max position, as illustrated in Figure 6.Data for each beam profile were normalized to their CAX maximum dose and smoothed using a median filter to eliminate noise.

As listed in Table 2, for the 6MV photon beam profile, the maximum differences in flatness were −0.2% and −0.4% for the 20 cm × 20 cm and 25 cm × 25 cm, respectively.The maximum differences in symmetry were −0.7% and −0.5% for the 20 cm × 20 cm and 25 cm × 25 cm, respectively.In the case of the electron beam profile, the maximum differences in flatness were −0.2% and −0.4% for the 20 cm × 20 cm and 25 cm × 25 cm, the maximum differences in symmetry were −0.5% for both field sizes.

Ultimately, the maximum difference observed between the traditional setup method and the prototype setup for photon beam profiles was 0.7%, with errors between the two ultrasonic sensors consistently below 0.5%.The profiles measured in two different setups show no significant difference, indicating that the prototype developed using ultrasonic sensors can achieve a similar setup precision as the traditional method.Comparing inline/crossline profiles measured according to energy was represented on each graph, with differences observed only around the shoulder region of the profiles, while no significant differences were found in the region (over 80% dose) determining symmetry and flatness of the profiles.


dIscussIon

In previous studies, [7][8][9] performance validation of various ultrasonic sensors has been carried out.Among them, the HC015 and SR04 sensors were the most extensively discussed.In addition, in other fields, the study [11] has presented automatic water level detection utilizing ultrasonic sensors.

In this study, we developed two different prototypes of ultrasonic sensors, US15 and SR04, to evaluate their distance measurement accuracy.Through experiments performed from 5 to 21 cm, we gained insights into the performance metrics of these sensors, including average errors and maximum deviations.Our statistical analysis bolstered the reliability of these ultrasonic sensors, enhancing the confidence in our data measurements.The findings highlighted a notable increase in measurement uncertainty at a distance of 5 cm or less, attributed to interference between the sensor wavelengths.In addition, we observed an escalation in the measurement error for the SR04 sensor with increasing the distance, underscoring the imperative for the precise alignment between the sensor and the surface to ensure accurate readings.This points to the crucial need for further research on how measurement distance and surface orientation affect sensor accuracy.The RMSE metric, calculated as the square root of the average squared errors, serves as an indicator of the prediction accuracy, with a lower value signifying a higher precision.Remarkably, both sensors demonstrated measurement accuracy within 2 mm for the range of distances measured, suggesting their potential applicability requiring a fine-grained distance measurement.

Typically, a 3D water phantom is used to establish a measurement environment for evaluating the performance of detectors with various characteristics, such as ionization chambers, as seen in previous studies. [12,13]It is primarily utilized to measure beam characteristics such as energy profiles and PDD.While PDD measurements are greatly affected by the SSD, profiles are relatively more influenced by the horizontal alignment of the water level.

In this study, a method of adjusting the water level of the 3D water phantom with an ultrasonic sensor was proposed and the possibility was confirmed.We compared the beam profile using a sensor-based prototype against the traditional method.The results for flatness and symmetry show no significant differences, indicating that the new setup method using ultrasound sensors achieves a setup level comparable to traditional methods.This suggests that the new method not only matches the conventional setup in terms of reproducibility but also offers a more quantitative approach.By reducing reliance on human judgment, this method enhances the accuracy and consistency of beam profile measurements.Moreover, the adoption of precise sensor technology allows for exact adjustments in the physical position and level of the phantom, yielding consistent results in the uniformity and symmetry of the profile.This technological approach plays a significant role in improving the quality of radiation therapy and lays the groundwork for enhancing the precision of radiation treatments.Future research should involve a more comprehensive evaluation of this prototype across various experimental environments to validate its performance.These studies are expected to aid in achieving more objective evaluations and measurements during the installation product acceptance and commissioning processes of linear accelerators.In additionally, based on these research findings, if supported by the technical foundation to utilize multiple ultrasonic sensors simultaneously, it could become one of the quantitative phantom setup methods.


conclusIon

This study developed a prototype for leveling the water surface using two types of ultrasonic sensors.These ultrasonic sensors exhibited excellent measurement accuracy within a specific range.When applied to the setup of the 3D water phantom, the sensor-based prototypes yielded radiation beam profile measurement results similar to those obtained using traditional setup methods.This approach is expected to reduce human error when positioning the 3D water phantom compared to traditional methods and assists in achieving the consistent management of radiation beam quality generated from a linear accelerator, thereby establishing more precise radiation therapy.
