Feasibility of awake craniotomy for brain arteriovenous malformations: A scoping review

Background: Brain Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs) located in proximity to eloquent brain regions are associated with poor surgical outcomes, which may be due to higher rates of postoperative neurological deterioration.Current treatment protocols include stereotactic radiosurgery, transarterial embolization, and surgical resection under general anesthesia.Awake Craniotomy (AC) allows intraoperative mapping of eloquent areas to improve post-operative neurologic outcomes.Objectives: We reviewed the current literature reporting surgical outcomes and assessed the feasibility of AC for AVM resection.Methods: The PRISMA guidelines were utilized as a template for the review.Three databases including PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library were searched using a predefined search strategy.After removing duplicates and screening, full texts were analyzed.Outcomes including the extent of resection, intra-operative and postoperative complications, and long-term neurologic outcomes were assessed.Results: 12 studies were included with a total of 122 AVM cases.Spetzler-Martin grading was used for the classification of the AVMs.The asleep-awake-asleep protocol was most commonly used for AC.Complete resection was achieved in all cases except 5. Intraoperative complications included seizures (n = 2) and bleeding (n = 4).Short-term post-operative complications included hemorrhage (n = 3), neurologic dysfunctions including paresis (n = 3), hemiplegia (n = 10), dysphasia/aphasia (n = 6), cranial nerve dysfunction (n = 3), and pulmonary embolism (n = 1).Almost all neurological deficits after surgery gradually improved on subsequent follow-ups.Conclusion: AVMs may shift the anatomical location of eloquent brain areas which may be mapped during AC.All studies recommended AC for the resection of AVMs in close proximity to eloquent areas as mapping during AC identifies the eloquent cortex thus promoting careful tissue handling which may preserve neurologic function and/or predict the postoperative functional status of the patients We, therefore, conclude that AC is a viable modality for AVMs resection near eloquent language and motor areas.

Introduction

4][5][6][7] Unruptured AVMs with little to no neurological deficits harbor the greatest risk of neurological deterioration as untreated AVMs have a hemorrhage rate of 1-3 % per year. 8,9petzler-Martin grading system 10 is commonly used to guide therapeutic approaches including surgery, radiosurgery, neuro-interventional surgery, or a combination of the three (see Fig. 1).

The treatment of AVMs is based on several factors including size, grade, and location; and may involve multiple modalities.For cases of small AVMs (volume less than 12 cm 3 or maximum diameter less than 3 cm), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) may offer acceptable surgical outcomes. 11Transarterial embolization (TAE) sometimes combined with SRS, 12 or surgical resection could be offered to Spetzler-Martin Grades (SMG) I and II. 13Grade 3 and 4 AVMs are more complex and need multimodal approaches, whereas Grade 4 occipital or cerebellar AVMs need definite surgical resection. 13Grade 5 AVMs carry a higher risk and thus have to be treated conservatively. 13The surgical approach to AVMs is via exposure to AVM via craniotomy, followed by isolation and division of arterial feeders and circumferential nidus excision.The venous flow is disconnected before the closure of wound. 13VMs proximal to eloquent brain areas are associated with greater surgical morbidity and mortality. 14,15Feeding vessels could potentially be adjacent to eloquent/functional neural tissue, thus ligating the incorrect artery can result in deficits.Awake Craniotomy (AC) allows intraoperatively mapping of eloquent areas to improve post-operative neurologic outcomes. 16The anesthetic approach can include either asleep-awake-asleep or asleep-awake technique. 17,18Anesthetic management plays a significant role in the surgical outcome. 19Although commonly utilized for diffuse glioma and epilepsy surgery, AC has evolved through time to incorporate distinct lesions. 20,21Awake mapping for AVMs may be used for cortical mapping, especially for large unruptured AVMs with high Spetzler-Martin grades adjacent to eloquent areas through defining margins that allow safe, effective resection with real-time feedback to neurosurgeons.

The objective of this study is to review the current literature reporting surgical outcomes of AC for AVM resection.We formulate a scoping synthesis of the reported cases and series, and review the feasibility of implementing AC as the standard for resecting AVMs.


Methodology

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were used to conduct the review.


Search strategy

The following search terms were used '(((Brain arteriovenous malformation) OR (Eloquent cortex AVM) OR (Cerebral arteriovenous malformation) AND (Awake Craniotomy)))' through MeSH Terms and/ or if these terms were mentioned in the title or abstracts.Three databases were searched including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Cochrane Library.No further filters were applied.


Eligibility criteria

Observational studies, Case studies, case series, and clinical trials assessing or reporting the feasibility of AC for AVM resection were included in the review.

Review articles, commentaries, and meeting reports or abstracts were excluded.Studies with treatment modalities other than AC or assessing AC on other malformations were excluded.Articles not reporting adequate data on outcomes were also excluded.


Risk of bias assessment

STROBE guidelines to assess observational studies, whereas CARE guidelines were used to assess case studies and series.


Studies selection and data extraction

The results of the initial search were compiled and duplicates were removed.The remaining articles were screened by two authors.Full-text assessment and selection of final studies were done by three authors and approved by all authors.Basic study and demographic details including study type, population, mean age at presentation, mean AVM size at presentation, location, Spetzler-Martin grade, and presenting symptoms were assessed and reported.Outcomes including extent of resection, intra-operative and post-operative complications and long-term neurologic outcomes were assessed.


Results

A total of 82 articles were identified, among which 35 were from PubMed/MEDLINE, and 47 from Scopus.After removing 29 duplicates and 28 articles after title/abstract screening, 25 articles were assessed for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.12 studies were included in the review.The PRISMA Flowchart for the article selection is given in Fig. 1.


Demographics and description of AVMs

12 articles were included in the study with a total of 124 AVM cases.There were 66 Males and 58 Females with a mean age of 34.78 years.The demographics, AVM locations, and diameters in individual studies are summarized in Table 1.Most studies used the Spetzler-Martin grading for classification of the AVM; 16 cases were a grade I, 26 were SMG II, 36 were SMG III, 17 were IV, and 7 were SMG V, whereas 2 studies did not report the grading of the AVMs.The most common presentations were seizures, followed by headache, and hemorrhage; all of which may or may not be associated with focal neurologic deficits.


Anesthesia and surgical technique

Most studies followed the asleep-awake-asleep protocol for the awake craniotomy. 24,29,32Patients were initially sedated on propofol and remifentanil followed by local anesthesia for scalp and temporal muscle. 22,23,30Once the AVM was exposed, language function was assessed most often by a neuropsychologist by a set of a pre-decided battery of tests; whereas some studies also assessed motor function depending on the eloquence of the area.Some studies also conducted cortical stimulation mapping using imaging, somatosensory-evoked potentials, electroencephalography, and direct cortical stimulation (DCS).Intraoperative complications included seizures (n = 2), which were easily controlled; and bleeding which may have required transfusion (n = 4). 22,30Gamble et al. mentioned that sulcal cortex and subcortical white matter that may be eloquent are not accessible until the resection has begun, thus they argued that the maintenance of a wakeful state with language interrogation may provide important feedback to the surgeon. 27Zhou et al. also monitored Visual Evoked Potential (VEP) and Electromyography (EMG) during the AC.They reported that VEP may be used to evaluate visual function and predict visual impairment, whereas EMG monitoring may evaluate cranial nerve dysfunction. 23Wang et al. mentioned that AC for AVMs does not allow for brain relaxation by hyperventilation or pharmacologic neuroprotection, which should be taken into account in patient selection for AC. 30 AVMs are prone to bleeding; a hemorrhage may necessitate emergent conversion to general anesthesia (GA). 30


Post-operative findings

Language function was mapped in most studies, whereas motor function mapping was done in some cases. 22,29,30Complete resection was achieved in all cases except 5. 22,32 Gabarrós et al. confirmed the residual AVM on postoperative angiography treated the residual AVM with SRS. 228][29][30][31] Almost all the complications/dysfunctions gradually improved on subsequent follow-ups.The 3 cases with hemorrhage underwent a craniotomy for evacuation. 22,32Zhou Q et al reported that out of 43 patients, 4 patients had complications [hemiplegia (n = 1), hemianopia (n = 1), hemiplegia and cranial nerve dysfunction (n = 2)] that did not improve on 1 year follow-up, however, rest of the 39 patients were neurologically intact in the 1-year follow-up. 23The patient with mid dysphasia described by Gamble et al. had completely resolved by her 6 weeks follow-up. 27akhshi et al. described a case of conductive dysphasia and memory impairment 6 h after the procedure, which was due to cerebral edema, thus Dexamethasone was started that resolved the symptoms in 36 h. 28immannitya et al. reported slight difficulty of speech which completely resolved within a few days. 29Out of the 7 cases reported by Wang et al. 3 experienced deficits, all of which improved on follow-up.The mild facial weakness recovered in 3 days, left-sided weakness completely recovered in 7 days, and a patient with headache was stable at 13 months. 30A case of pulmonary arterial embolism was defined to be associated with pulmonary migration of Onyx.The patient required humidified venturi mask and high-flow nasal oxygen.The patient was discharged on day 4 and remained stable at the a 3-month follow-up. 31Gabarrós A et al. assessed 12 patients among which 5 had AC for AVM, at follow-up 3 patients were neurologically improved and 3 patients had new neurological deficits, however, the type of surgery was not specified in the patients who did not improve. 22Radiosurgery or preoperative embolization was given prior to surgery in higher grade AVMs, however, the patient with Grade IV AVM who did not receive prior interventions suffered intracerebral hemorrhage in the resection cavity due to residual AVM that was resected at the time of hematoma evacuation.This residual AVM was removed via SRS and patient had improved on the one-year follow-up.From the AC group, one patient had temporal seizures at 3 month follow-up. 22It is worth noting that all the deficits were related to the AVMs, and not the awake surgery, therefore may be similarly seen in AVM resection under GA.

Only one study compared AC to resection under GA.Zhou et al. compared the neurologic deficits in patients who underwent AVM resection in awake surgery with intraoperative neuromonitoring via somatosensory-evoked potential (SEP), maximum expiratory pressure (MEP), and brain auditory-evoked potential (BAEP); to those who had resection under GA.They reported that the short-term hemiplegia ratio among grade III patients in the intraoperative neuromonitoring group was significantly lower, however, even though the short-to long-term neurologic dysfunction was lower patients in AC group, the difference was not significant.Intraoperative neuromonitoring was reported to be is beneficial in predicting neurologic dysfunction postoperatively. 23


Feasibility of awake craniotomy

Zamorano L et al. were among the first to conduct AVMs resection in AC with interactive image-guided surgery for deep-seated cavernous malformations and reported it to be 'a very accurate and safe approach'. 25abarros A et al. reported that mapping identified the functional cortex, promoted careful tissue handling, and preserved function, thus may guide dissection to AVMs beneath the cortical surface, and may impact the decision of complete or sub-total resection.They recommended utilizing AC with intraoperative mapping for AVMs that are adjacent to the language/motor cortex, larger AVMs, AVMs with higher Spetzler-Martin grades; and patients presenting with unruptured AVMs. 22Zhou Q. et al. compared two groups: one undergoing AC with intraoperative neuromonitoring, and one under general anesthesia.They reported that short-and long-term eloquent region damage was lower in the AC group, thus it was concluded that AC with intraoperative neuromonitoring is better for preserving postoperative neurologic function. 23Ménissier A et al. concluded that resection of AVMs under AC with cortical mapping is feasible and allows safer, more comfortable, and more complete resection of AVMs located close to eloquent speech areas. 24Gamble AJ et al. reported that AC is a viable strategy to maximize the safety of resection of SMG II and III AVMs which are 5 mm or more close to eloquent language cortex areas as identified in fMRI.AC was also reported to minimize the risk of the neurologic deficit while providing a durable surgical outcome. 27Chan DYC et al. compared AC for AVM resection to SRS and reported statistically better treatment outcomes with AC. 32 Bakhshi SK et al. reported the initial case series of AC for AVMs resection from a low-middle income country, and concluded that AC provided better postoperative outcomes, lesser morbidity, shorter hospital stay, contributing to low resource utilization, thus should be an ideal modality in a resource-limited setting. 28immannitya P et al. 29 and Wang AT et al. 30 recommended functional mapping during AC to map the AVM followed by resection under general anesthesia.


Discussion

SMG I and II AVMs can be offered SRS or surgery. 13AVMs in deep or eloquent areas also benefit from SRS, with obliteration rates up to 65 % being achieved in eloquent brain areas. 34Pikus HJ et al. compared microsurgical treatment of AVMs with SRS and reported significantly lower postoperative hemorrhages, postoperative neurological deficits, lower overall mortality, and higher incidence of obliteration for those treated with microsurgery. 35A meta-analysis of 137 studies on treatment modalities of AVMs and reported the postoperative complications to be 7.4 % for surgery, 5.1 % for SRS, and 6.6 % for TAE; however, the obliteration rates were 96 % with microsurgery, 38 % with SRS, and 13 % with TAE.The hemorrhage rates were significantly greater for TAE and SRS. 36A greater mortality rate with surgical resection may also be explained by the fact that emergency cases are mostly treated with surgery, whereas SRS and TAE are often for elective cases.Chan DYC et al. compared AC for AVM resection to SRS and reported statistically better treatment outcomes with AC. 32 The surgical morbidity and postoperative complications in patients with AVMs in the eloquent brain areas is significantly higher than in cases of AVMs in the non-eloquent areas. 37Even for SMG I and II AVMs, the risk of performing surgery on non-eloquent brain is reported to be 0.6 %, which rises to 9.5 % in cases of AVMs in the eloquent brain areas. 38Large scale studies on AVMs have established eloquent proximity as significant factor contributing to surgical morbidity and mortality. 14,15Lawton MT et al. proposed expanding the SMG III into three subgroups: small or superficially draining AVM in an eloquent location which carried a risk of 2.9 %, a medium or deeply draining AVM in a non-eloquent region which carried a risk of 7.1 %, and a medium or superficially draining AVM in an eloquent region with a risk of 14.8 %. 39 Schaller C et al. hypothesized that the poor surgical outcomes for AVMs located in eloquent areas may be due to higher rates of neurological deterioration following that could prolong the hospitalization. 37herefore, preserving the neurologic function along with maximal safe resection of AVM may be necessary for better surgical outcomes in patients with eloquently located AVMs.However, it is worth mentioning that a case of SMG IV AVM who did not receive prior interventions suffered intracerebral hemorrhage in the resection cavity due to residual AVM, 22 thus higher grade AVMs need multimodal approaches.

Functional imaging can be used to localize the eloquent areas preoperatively. 40Cannestra et al. stratified patients into three groups based on fMRI activation: The minimal-risk group had fMRI activation was at least one gyrus away from the nidus, the high-risk group showed fMRI activation extremely close to the nidus, the moderate-risk group showed activation within one gyrus of the nidus without overlap.The minimal-risk group underwent surgery under GA, high-risk group were referred to radiosurgery, and the moderate-risk group underwent AC.Within the high-risk group, in 2 patients cortical stimulation and optical imaging showed eloquent cortex enveloping the nidus, therefore the resection had to be halted. 26This may be explained by the ability of AVMs to cause the reorganization of cortical regions. 41They may shift the anatomical location of the functional regions to an adjacent gyrus 42 or even the contralateral hemisphere, 43 therefore accurate functional mapping could only be done during awake surgery, as each individual artery can be temporarily clipped, checked for deficit, and ligated safely.

Unlike tumors, complete resection is necessary for AVMs.In case of neurologic deficit detected during AC, a part of the AVM is left.Gabarrós A et al. treated the residual AVM with SRS, 22 however there is a need of further studies to demonstrate the management of residual AVMs after AC.Furthermore, only one study compared AC to resection under GA thus larger trials comparing the modes of anesthesia are required to establish the standard surgical technique.

Due to the limited number of studies on AC for the treatment of AVMs, a comparatively small sample size is the first limitation of this study.There is also a lack of studies comparing AC to other treatment modalities such as SRS or TAE, because of which a comparative or quantitative analysis was not conducted.There is further need of larger trials utilizing AC for AVM resections, and studies comparing AC to other AVM treatment modalities.


Conclusion

AVMs in eloquent brain regions have a poor surgical outcome, which may be explained by greater incidence of post-operative neurological deterioration, therefore functional mapping of the eloquent areas may aid in improving surgical morbidity and mortality.As AVMs may shift the anatomical location of the eloquent regions, fMRI may have a limited role in mapping.AC, therefore may be the most accurate modality for mapping and guiding resection.The studies conducted on utilizing AC for AVM resection recommended utilizing AC for AVMs that are adjacent to or in close proximity to eloquent brain regions.Functional mapping during AC identifies the eloquent cortex thus promoting careful tissue handling which may preserve neurologic function and/or predict the postoperative functional status of the patients.We therefore recommend considering AC in the treatment for AVMs, especially for SMG II to III AVMs which are 5 mm or closer to eloquent language or motor cortex areas, however, further studies with larger samples are required for establishing the standard-of-care in such patients.
