Converting livestock waste into compost as crop fertiliser is a wise management practice that will benefit both livestock and crop farmers.This process potentially boosts farmers' income and practises good environmental techniques in achieving sustainable development goals (SDGs).However, limited knowledge and lack of extension activity cause the unawareness of this beneficial practice.Besides, some farmers may have problems with where to market the compost made due to uncertain demand, and some refuse to employ organic compost on their crops.Without any doubt, managing livestock waste into compost is a low-cost practice involving simple materials and methods.This study was conducted among 100 livestock farmers using purposive sampling in the southern region of Peninsular Malaysia, including Negeri Sembilan, Melaka and Johor.The finding found that the mean score for the adoption of livestock waste management, farmers' behaviour, external factors and farmers' perception is 3.992, 3.824, 3.694 and 4.150, respectively.All mean scores except farmers' perception show moderate levels where most are still in between managing the livestock waste wisely or not.Hence, this practice is suggested for collaboration between the private sector to encourage the farmers to engage more in managing livestock waste and producing it commercially.Besides, the role of extension service is also vital as this effort could enhance farmers' well-being and improve their livelihood.

1.

Introduction In Malaysia, the livestock business significantly contributes to food consumption and money generation.Livestock products account for 1/3 of worldwide protein consumption and can help with nutrition and hunger.Aside from that, the cattle business may create energy and plant nutrients while acting as an asset.With an increasing global population and demand for livestock products, livestock production must be outstanding in achieving the population's needs.At the same time, the emission of livestock waste, on the other hand, could also benefit the farmers and convert it into compost or biogas resources [1] [2].Adapting agriculture to be more environmentally friendly is crucial to ensure that livestock operations do not harm the environment.Livestock waste management (LWM) is also considered one of the innovation practices for environmental sustainability and income generation among breeders.Compost and biogas technology has become a trend among certain breeders to transform a better socio-IOP Publishing doi:10.1088/1755-1315/1397/1/012033 2 economic system with a decarbonised environment and attain the sustainable development goals (SDGs) [3] [4] [5].

Compost is known as organic material broken down and repurposed as fertiliser and soil improvement.Using compost from livestock and crop waste may benefit various parties, including the farmers, users and the environment.However, there are still many obstacles that need to be faced by the farmers to keep on implementing waste management on the farm, such as insufficient available information on compost quality and processing besides discouraging the use of waste in agriculture [6].Despite its advantages and benefits in agriculture, livestock waste is still not commonly employed among livestock farmers as the farmers' attitude, knowledge, and skills are still low and less aware of waste processing [7].Hence, a deeper comprehension of the farmers' decision-making process about the use of compost is required to develop a more successful promotion approach.

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) by [8] has been chosen as the foundation of this study, and scholars commonly use it in studies of the farmer's behaviour towards something.It consists of three variables, namely behavioural attitude (BA), subjective norm (SN) and perceived behavioural control (PBC), as demonstrated in Figure 1.In this study, BA can be understood as the farmers' attitude towards LWM practice based on their ability and motivational factors to do so [8].Most livestock farmers carry out their livestock activities based on the knowledge and skills or techniques of their family's beliefs.Some are trying to venture into the latest and more modern styles, but some are still practising the traditional methods [9] [10].Hence, this issue has become a barrier to economic growth due to the difficulty in accepting sophisticated and cost-effective farming innovations.


Figure 1. Theory of Planned Behaviour

On the other hand, SN is a social pressure that leads the farmers to do something according to their willingness.However, those perceptions depend on the people around them, namely close relatives who will inspire them to do certain things and be role models to themselves [8].In this study, the livestock farmers may be influenced to practice the LWM by their farming community, responsible agencies, family members and social media.Therefore, the farmer's decision to adopt livestock waste management at their farm depends on who had a more significant influence to handle this matter seriously.

Meanwhile, PBC refers to how fascinating something is thought to be done [11].According to the interaction between perceived behavioural control and behaviour, farmers are more likely to act in ways they seem to have control over, and vice versa.Therefore, it can be suggested that perceived behavioural control strongly influences awareness of adopting LWM.


2.

Materials and Methods A quantitative with cross-sectional research design has been employed in this study.A total of 100 livestock breeders around Johor, Melaka and Negeri Sembilan were selected using a purposive sampling technique among fish, shrimp, poultry, ruminants and rabbit breeders aged between 20 to 60 years old [12].A structured questionnaire was referred from the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) by [8] and adopted from previous studies conducted by scholars around the globe.The dependent variable is the adoption of livestock waste management, while the independent variables are the farmer's attitude, external factors and PBC.For the research instrument, there are five (5) 2), slightly agree (3), agree (4) and strongly agree (5).The items used were adopted from several sources, as depicted in Table 1.

Meanwhile, a pilot study has also been conducted to identify the instrument's reliability.The Cronbach Alpha value for adopting LWM = 0.834, the farmer's attitude = 0.808, external factors lead to LWM = 0.688 and the farmer's perception = 0.889.Those values are considered reliable for a study as referred to [12], strong items of a variable have a high Cronbach Alpha value.The descriptive statistics consisting of frequency, mean, and standard deviation using SPSS have been employed to identify the respondents' socio-demographic profile and the objective of this study.The mean score was calculated based on the Likert scale value from 1 to 5, and then it is categorised into three (3) categories, namely, low (1 to 2.33), moderate (2.34 to 3.66) and high (3.67 to 5).


Results and Discussion


Respondents' socio-demographic

The demographic information of this study includes gender, breeder's age and experience, education level, farming cost, and annual income.As in Table 1, most respondents were male and located in Negeri Sembilan.Meanwhile, the average age of the breeders is 42.2 years old, with 4.9 years of average experience.More than half-breeders had completed their secondary education with Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM).Furthermore, the monthly mean farming cost is Ringgit Malaysia (RM) 1,320, while the average annual income is RM 42,168.

Around the world, it is noted that farmers, breeders and even fishermen are getting older.It has become the main challenge in the agriculture sector due to youth migration to the cities for a fixed monthly salary [14].In this study, adopting proper livestock waste management is still acceptable for those ageing and experienced breeders.However, it can't be assumed that all of them could turn it into compost for income generation [1] [2].Furthermore, converting livestock waste into biogas technology is even more complicated as it needs the proper installation of a dome digester.Not all farmers are willing to do so unless they are close to the authority or the leaders in a farmer's community [2].

In a study conducted by [15] and [6], when farmers get old, it is more difficult for them to adopt new changes even though the innovation introduced can profit the farmers.[16] also added that experienced farmers are reluctant to accept recent changes.Still, it doesn't happen to those inexperienced farmers as they are willing to learn new knowledge and skills and practice it on their farms.Table 1 shows that farmers are not too old, as the average age is 42.2 years old, and had 4.9 years of experience in livestock farming.Furthermore, those educated farmers are more ready for innovation, are more open-minded, and better understand the knowledge and skills transferred [17] [6].Results also demonstrate that most farmers had completed their secondary school and the highest qualification is a Master's degree.The income of farmers may also lead them to adopt such innovations.In this study, the average annual income is RM42,168.According to [2], affordable farmers may be influenced to adopt the new practice and willing to pay for training or other costs involved.

The mean score of each variable measured towards LWM practice Three (3) independent variables were employed in this study to identify the factors towards adopting livestock waste management.The farmers' perception demonstrates the highest mean score with M=4.150, followed by farmers' attitude, M=3.824 and external factors, M=3.694.Meanwhile, adopting livestock waste management as the dependent variable had a slightly high mean score with M=3.992.According to the results above, farmers' attitude towards adopting waste management among livestock farmers is considered to have a high mean score.Those farmers are willing and ready to adopt it as a practice at their farm, but embracing innovation in livestock farming, such as converting livestock waste, is considered a new practice and involves specific procedures, including sufficient workforce and proper management [18].

Furthermore, external influences such as friends, family and farmers' associations also highly motivate farmers to adopt good livestock waste management.Those farmers with good social status and who have good networking with government agencies will be selected to get assistance regarding tools, ideas and skills [2].However, some farmers also hesitate to adopt it at their farm as it does not convince the markets.

Finally, the farmers' perception towards adopting good management practice of livestock waste also demonstrates a high mean score as this practice is considered relevant and beneficial to both livestock and crop farmers; however, it does consume good skills with the guidance from an expert or other example from the farmers' community themselves [19].


Conclusion

This study concludes that most breeders are not ready for compost and biogas technology adoption.However, they prefer handling livestock waste without pursuing any process involving the latest technologies and generating income through it.Hence, there is a lack of extension activities among the responsible agencies, including the Department of Veterinary Services and research institutes, including universities.Even though this innovation practice involves skillful and knowledgeable breeders, it still needs a higher cost to make it happen.So, funding from any agency could make this effort possible to ensure sustainable development goals (SDG).Besides, the collaboration between the breeders and other private sectors may also lead to a successful adoption rate in livestock waste management.All stakeholders must also resiliently encourage the community about the importance of environmental pollution and generating income from agricultural waste.
