Background Thermal damage during surgical procedures is a critical factor influencing patient safety and outcomes, particularly in minimally invasive laparoscopic surgeries.Advanced robotic-assisted surgical systems, such as the Anovo® Surgical System, incorporate monopolar electrosurgical tools designed to optimize precision while minimizing collateral tissue damage.This study evaluates the thermal effects of the Anovo® Hook Electrode and Curved Scissors compared to conventional off-the-shelf (OTS) tools.Methods An ex vivo study was conducted using 288 tissue samples from a swine model, including liver, kidney, and muscle tissues.Thermal effects during monopolar cutting and coagulation were evaluated at three power settings (low, medium, high) and durations (5, 10, 15 s).Histological analysis was performed on all samples to assess coagulation necrosis and thermal spread.Statistical equivalence testing was applied to compare the Anovo® devices with OTS tools.ResultsThe Anovo® devices achieved precise and consistent thermal effects, meeting equivalence criteria in 97.57% of samples.Histological analysis confirmed well-defined coagulation zones with no unintended necrosis beyond the treated areas.Thermal spread increased proportionally with power settings and activation durations, but remained within clinically acceptable limits.The Anovo® devices demonstrated performance comparable to, and occasionally superior to, OTS tools.ConclusionThe Anovo® Hook Electrode and Curved Scissors provide safe and effective monopolar electrosurgical performance with precise thermal effects.These findings support their use in robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgeries and highlight their potential to enhance surgical precision and patient outcomes.

Introduction

Thermal damage control during surgical procedures is a critical factor influencing patient outcomes, particularly in minimally invasive laparoscopic surgeries [1][2][3].Electrosurgical instruments are extensively employed in such procedures for their ability to simultaneously cut and coagulate tissues [4,5].However, these devices can inadvertently affect surrounding tissue, necessitating rigorous evaluation to ensure precise and safe application.Recent advancements in robotic-assisted surgical systems have led to the development of innovative end-effectors designed to enhance surgical precision and safety.Among these are the Anovo® Instrument ARMs Hook Electrode and Curved Scissors [6][7][8][9][10].

The Anovo® Surgical System is a robotic-assisted platform designed to support laparoscopic procedures through the integration of advanced monopolar electrosurgical tools, such as the Hook Electrode and Curved Scissors.These instruments enable surgeons to perform dissection, cutting, and coagulation with a high degree of precision and control.The use of monopolar energy is notable for its capacity to produce consistent thermal effects while aiming to limit lateral thermal spread to surrounding tissues [11].Comparative assessment with off-the-shelf (OTS) instruments provides an opportunity to evaluate the performance and safety of the Anovo® devices within the context of established surgical tools.

Despite the promising design and functionality of the Anovo® end-effectors, systematic evaluation of their thermal effects on biological tissues remains paramount.Ex vivo models, which simulate the geometric and thermal properties of human tissues, provide an invaluable platform for assessing device performance under controlled conditions [12].By adhering to stringent FDA guidelines and leveraging histopathological analyses, this study aims to benchmark the thermal performance of the Anovo® Hook and Scissors against industry-standard OTS devices [13].

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the thermal effects of the Anovo® Hook Scissors on representative tissue types.Specifically, the study aims to determine the extent of coagulation necrosis and thermal spread during monopolar cutting and coagulation under various power settings and durations.By establishing thermal equivalence or superiority relative to OTS devices, this study seeks to substantiate the safety and efficacy of the Anovo® system in a range of laparoscopic applications.


Materials and methods


Study design

This study aimed to assess the thermal effects of the Anovo® Hook and Scissors during ex vivo application of monopolar electrosurgical energy.Tissue samples from a swine model, including liver, kidney, and abdominal muscle, were used to represent varying densities and thermal conductivities similar to human tissues in clinical settings.These tissue types were selected due to their relevance in mimicking surgical scenarios where precise thermal control is critical, such as abdominal and hepatic procedures.The study was designed to compare the thermal performance of the Anovo® devices with widely used OTS reference tools under standardized conditions.

The swine were of domestic breed, mixed sex, and young adult age (details withheld due to source standardization).Tissues were harvested post-mortem, frozen immediately after procurement, and stored under controlled conditions.


Off-the-shelf instruments

The off-the-shelf (OTS) reference instruments used in this study included a Pajunk monopolar angled hook electrode and an Aesculap Metzenbaum monopolar curved scissors.The Pajunk Ceramic Tip Electrode is insulated for monopolar coagulation and enables manipulation, dissection, cutting, and coagulation of internal tissues during laparoscopic surgery.The Aesculap scissor system includes stainless steel reusable or single-use monopolar instruments for cutting and manipulating soft tissue and vessels.Key specifications of the devices are summarized in Table 1.


Source of tissue samples and ethical approval for Animal-Derived tissues

This study was conducted using ex vivo tissue samples obtained from GLPigs at Shamir Medical Center, an approved research supplier.GLPigs operates under the supervision of The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and adheres to all ethical and regulatory standards for animal research.This study was conducted in accordance with the Basel Declaration and international ethical standards for animal research.Additionally, we confirm that the study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.The use of ex vivo tissues in this study did not involve live animal experimentation, and no additional procedures were performed on the animals beyond routine tissue procurement in accordance with approved protocols.


Sample Preparation

A total of 288 tissue samples were analyzed, with 96 samples allocated to each tissue type: kidney (n = 5 donors), liver (n = 4 donors), and abdominal muscle (n = 2 donors).These samples were divided equally for testing with the Anovo® devices and OTS reference tools -two standard off-the-shelf laparoscopic electrosurgical instruments -a Pajunk monopolar hook electrode [14] and an Aesculap curved monopolar scissors [15].Samples were obtained from different regions of each organ to ensure independent sampling and avoid pseudoreplication.Each sample represented a distinct energy activation site, carefully spaced by at least 5 times the expected thermal spread, based on power setting and duration, to prevent overlap of thermal zones.Testing was conducted under two activation modes (monopolar coagulation and monopolar cutting), three power settings (low: 40 W, medium: 80 W for coagulation and 170 W for cutting, and high: 120 W for coagulation and 300 W for cutting), and three durations (5, 10, and 15 s).This comprehensive design ensured robust data collection to evaluate the thermal performance across a wide range of clinically relevant scenarios.


Experimental setup and randomization

The experiments were performed at Patho-Lab Ltd.

(Ness-Ziona, Israel), a certified research facility specializing in tissue analysis.At least 12 h before testing, tissues were defrosted at room temperature.All samples were kept in a temperature-controlled water bath at 38 °C before testing.Prior to each energy application, a thermocouple was used to confirm tissue temperature remained within physiological range (36-38 °C).After energy application, tissues were allowed to cool back to baseline temperature before the next application to simulate real-world use and allow thermal damage to fully develop.

For each test session, a random tissue sample was selected from the bath, and four activations were performed -two using the Anovo® device and two with the OTS reference tool.The order of device application (Anovo vs. OTS), as well as the assignment of power settings and durations, was randomized to reduce experimental bias.

To ensure uniformity, all electrosurgical applications were performed by a single trained operator using a standardized manual technique.A minimum rest interval of 30 s was observed between activations to allow tissue to cool and complete thermal effects to manifest.Applications were conducted on the thicker areas of the tissues to ensure consistent boundary conditions.Following activation, each treated site was trimmed, verified for proper margins, and stored in 10% formalin for histological processing.

The ValleyLab FT10 electrosurgical generator (Covidien) was used in PURE CUT mode for monopolar cutting and FULG COAG mode for monopolar coagulation.For each mode, three power levels were applied (low, medium, and high), and activations were performed at continuous durations of 5, 10, or 15 s depending on the setting.These generator configurations are consistent with those used in clinical practice with the Anovo® Surgical System.

For each test session, four activations were performed: two with the Anovo® device and two with the corresponding OTS reference tool.For each activation, one sample was used to measure length and depth, and a second was used to measure width and depth.These two orientations allowed for a complete three-dimensional characterization of the thermal effect.In each case, the depth of thermal damage was measured histologically from the surface of the tissue to the deepest extent of visible coagulative necrosis, while length and width were measured along the longest and widest dimensions of the lesion.This approach enabled calculation of worst-case thermal volume using a half-sphere model.


Histological analysis

Post-treatment, all tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin immediately after energy application.Each test condition included four energy activations-two with the Anovo® device and two with the OTS reference tool-performed consecutively within a few minutes.Samples were individually labeled, cut, and placed into specimen bags, then immediately transferred to the laboratory for fixation.To ensure consistency, all four samples from each test condition were processed and sliced as a group.

The fixation period ranged from 2 to 7 days, depending on laboratory scheduling, and followed the internal SOPs of Patho-Lab Ltd. (Ness-Ziona, Israel).Sample trimming was performed according to standardized tissue-specific instructions (liver, kidney, muscle from swine model), and paraffin embedding and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were conducted using validated protocols.

Histological assessments were conducted in a blinded manner by a qualified board-certified toxicologic pathologist.Final slides were digitized using the HAMA-MATSU NanoZoomer Scanner at 20× magnification, and measurements of thermal effects (length, width, and depth) were performed using calibrated digital imaging software and optical microscopy.Each thermal lesion was evaluated in two orientations: one for length-depth and one for width-depth.

Thermal injury depth was defined as the vertical distance from the tissue surface to the deepest point of visible coagulative necrosis.The zone of necrosis was identified histologically by characteristic features under H&E staining, including cell sloughing, cytoplasmic eosinophilia, nuclear fragmentation, and architectural disruption.In contrast, degeneration was defined by altered staining properties (e.g., increased eosinophilia) without sloughing, reflecting either reversible injury or early-stage necrosis [16].Edema was evaluated by expansion of intercellular spaces in the adjacent tissue.All observations were graded using a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 4 based on severity (0 = no change, 1 = minimal degeneration or edema, 2 = mild degeneration or necrosis, 3-4 = moderate to severe damage, and results were summarized descriptively and visually using representative images.


Statistical analysis

To assess thermal performance similarity between the Anovo® devices and the OTS reference tools, we applied a descriptive equivalence criterion, defined a priori, in which ≥ 95% of Anovo® measurements were required to fall within the 95% CI of the corresponding OTS values.This criterion was applied independently to each parameter (thermal depth and width) for both devices (Hook and Scissors).

This approach, while nonstandard, was selected as a conservative and visually interpretable strategy to assess similarity in ex vivo device performance.It reflects a high degree of thermal consistency between instruments, supporting the goal of identifying clinically acceptable equivalence in thermal spread.


Results


Thermal effects of monopolar cutting

The Anovo® Hook and Scissors exhibited precise and consistent cutting capabilities across all tissue types (liver, kidney, and muscle) (Figs. 1 and 2).At the lowest power setting (40 W) and shortest activation duration (5 s), thermal damage was minimal, characterized by localized coagulation necrosis and limited lateral thermal spread.With increased power settings (300 W for cutting) and prolonged activation times (15 s), the extent of thermal effects, including necrosis and spread, increased proportionally but remained within clinically acceptable parameters (i.e.performance comparable to FDA-cleared OTS reference tools, which serve as the clinical benchmark).

Comparative analyses revealed equivalence in 97.91% of samples (141 out of 144) when the Anovo® devices were evaluated against OTS reference tools (Pajunk angled hook and Aesculap Metzenbaum scissors) under monopolar cutting conditions.This was based on a predefined criterion for acceptable thermal damage.Among the 3 non-equivalent samples in this mode, two favored the OTS reference tools, and one favored the Anovo® device.Overall, across all test conditions, 281 out of 288 samples (97.57%) met the equivalence criterion.Histopathological assessments confirmed that the thermal damage zones were well-defined and confined to the treated areas, with no evidence of excessive thermal spread to adjacent tissues.


Thermal effects of monopolar coagulation

In the monopolar coagulation mode, the Anovo® devices demonstrated effective coagulation of tissue with localized thermal effects, indicative of potential hemostatic capability.(Figures 3 and 4).As observed in cutting mode, higher power settings (120 W) and extended activation durations (15 s) led to increased coagulation necrosis and thermal spread.However, these effects remained comparable to those produced by the OTS reference tools across all tissue types.

Equivalence testing demonstrated that 97.22% of samples treated with the Anovo® devices met predefined equivalence criteria when compared to OTS tools.Both instruments performed reliably in achieving coagulation, with no excessive thermal spread or unintended tissue damage.


Histopathological findings

Histopathological examination of treated tissues revealed that the thermal effects were confined to the intended treatment zones for both cutting and coagulation modes.At higher power settings and prolonged activation durations, mild edema and tissue degeneration were observed (Figs. 5 and 6), particularly in the peripheral zones of treated areas.Histological scoring revealed that necrosis in adjacent tissues was absent (score 0) in the majority of samples: 44 for the Anovo® Hook, 41 for the OTS Hook, and 48 each for the Anovo® and OTS Scissors.Scores of 1 (minimal) and 2 (mild) were observed in fewer cases, with no samples exceeding grade 2. Edema was also limited to grades 0 or 1 across all devices.No necrotic damage was observed beyond the intended treatment zones, confirming that all thermal effects were well-contained and within acceptable histological parameters.

In line with toxicologic pathology criteria for device evaluation, thermal injury was considered excessive if necrosis or irreversible damage extended beyond the expected energy application zone.No such excessive spread was observed.All surrounding tissues exhibited only minimal or mild changes (grades 0-2), with no sample exceeding the non-adverse threshold as defined by STP and ESTP guidelines.


Statistical analysis

Of the 288 tissue samples analyzed, 281 (97.57%) demonstrated statistical equivalence between the Anovo® devices and the OTS reference tools across all evaluated parameters.Among the seven non-equivalent samples, the deviations were minor and did not exceed a severity score of 1 on a predefined scale of 0-4.The 7 samples that did not fall within the 95% CI of the OTS reference measurements were dispersed across different tissues, energy levels, durations, and both device types, with no identifiable trend.This random distribution indicates that the occasional deviation is not indicative of systematic variability in performance.Furthermore, the Anovo® devices demonstrated superior performance in five instances, with smaller zones of thermal spread compared to the OTS tools, indicating their precision and efficacy in minimizing collateral tissue damage.


Discussion

This study demonstrates that the Anovo® Hook and Scissors achieve consistent and precise monopolar cutting and coagulation, with thermal effects confined to the targeted zones.The devices met predefined equivalence criteria in 97.57% of samples and, in several cases, exhibited smaller thermal damage zones than off-the-shelf (OTS) reference instruments.These results reflect the predictable thermal behavior of monopolar electrosurgery, with thermal spread increasing proportionally with power and activation time.While the Anovo® devices exhibited smaller thermal damage zones than the reference tools in several conditions, these differences were not evaluated for statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted descriptively.Future studies designed and powered specifically for superiority analysis will be necessary to determine whether these observed differences are clinically meaningful.

Histopathological analysis confirmed that thermal injury remained confined to the intended treatment zone, with no necrosis extending into adjacent tissue.Observed changes in surrounding areas were limited to minimal or mild edema or early degeneration (grades 0-2), and were therefore classified as non-adverse, as defined by STP and ESTP criteria.[17,18] and the recent opinion of the Toxicologic Pathology Forum of the STP on assessing and communicating adversity for implantable medical devices [19].This finding confirms that all observed thermal injuries were confined to the treatment zone, with only minimal and reversible changes (e.g., edema or early degeneration) noted in adjacent tissues.The absence of necrosis beyond the treated area further reinforces the safety profile of the Anovo® devices.

This was consistent across diverse tissue types and supports the devices' safety and precision for laparoscopic and robotic-assisted procedures.The performance of the Anovo® devices closely aligned with that of legally marketed OTS tools and complied with FDA guidance for electrosurgical instruments.The thermal damage profiles observed in this study are consistent with previously reported values for monopolar electrosurgical instruments.For example, Hefermehl et al. demonstrated that monopolar energy applied to porcine tissue at 60 W for 1-2 s produced lateral thermal spread ranging from approximately 3.5 mm to over 20 mm, depending on application time [20].Similarly, Družijanić et al. found that monopolar instruments caused significantly more lateral thermal injury than bipolar or ultrasonic devices in human peritoneal tissue [21].In contrast, our study observed lateral thermal spread and depth typically within the range of 1-2 mm under standard surgical conditions.These findings place the Anovo® instruments at the lower end of the expected spectrum, consistent with safe and controlled energy delivery.Notably, Nechay et al. also reported that thermal spread from monopolar cautery can exceed 2 cm in in vivo models if used at high power or long durations, underscoring the importance of energy modulation [22].

The clinical relevance of the Anovo® devices lies in their consistent performance across varying power settings and tissue types.These tools provide surgeons with reliable options for achieving effective coagulation of tissue, indicative of potential hemostatic capability, and dissection, while minimizing collateral thermal damage.Their performance, coupled with their equivalence to OTS devices, supports their integration into routine surgical practice, particularly in minimally invasive and roboticassisted surgeries.Furthermore, the advanced design of the Anovo® devices may offer ergonomic and technical benefits, enhancing surgical precision and efficiency in complex procedures [6][7][8][9][10].

In this study, the thermal spread produced by the Anovo® Hook and Scissors instruments was consistently comparable to that of a legally marketed OTS reference device.The designation of thermal damage as "within clinically acceptable limits" is based on regulatory standards, which consider comparability to a predicate device an acceptable benchmark for safety.While specific numeric thresholds for thermal spread are not universally defined in laparoscopic electrosurgery, the ability to match or remain within the performance range of a cleared device provides a clinically and regulatory meaningful indication of safety.Importantly, the observed thermal effects did not exceed distances likely to cause collateral injury to adjacent structures in typical laparoscopic settings.

Despite the study's robust design, several limitations should be acknowledged.First, the use of an ex vivo model does not fully replicate in vivo surgical conditions.The absence of blood flow, perfusion, and tissue healing mechanisms may exaggerate thermal spread, making this a conservative or worst-case scenario.Future studies using perfused in vivo models or dynamic tissue platforms could better approximate clinical conditions.

Second, the study examined only three types of healthy porcine tissues-liver, kidney, and muscle-which, while diverse, do not encompass the full range of anatomical or pathological variability encountered in surgery.Follow-up research should include vascular-rich, adipose, fibrotic, or neoplastic tissues to assess device performance under more complex conditions.

Third, the focus was limited to acute thermal effects.Key aspects such as intraoperative handling, real-time cutting or coagulation efficiency, ergonomics, and postoperative healing were not evaluated.These endpoints should be incorporated into future in vivo or clinical trials to assess overall surgical performance and long-term outcomes.

Fourth, the study was funded by the device manufacturer, and several authors are employees of the sponsor.While this introduces the potential for perceived bias, all histopathological analyses were performed independently and blinded by a certified external pathologist.

Finally, while thermal equivalence is a critical safety benchmark, it does not alone establish full clinical equivalence.Future clinical evaluations should include comparative studies assessing usability, procedural efficiency, and patient outcomes in live surgical settings.

The Anovo® Hook and Scissors demonstrated reliable and effective monopolar electrosurgical performance, with thermal effects comparable to or better than those of OTS reference tools.The devices exhibited precise cutting and coagulation capabilities with localized thermal damage, supporting their safety and efficacy for laparoscopic and robotic-assisted procedures.While further in vivo research is warranted to confirm these findings and explore broader applications, the results of this study highlight the potential of the Anovo® devices to enhance surgical precision and improve patient outcomes.
