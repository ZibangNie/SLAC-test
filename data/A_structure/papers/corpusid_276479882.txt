Antidepressants to Manage Osteoarthritic Pain: The Value of Pain Phenotyping
20 February 202551A44179E392629A0E514E675D98C85B10.1007/s40266-025-01182-9Accepted: 14 January 2025 /
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic condition in which pain significantly affects quality of life, often leading to reduced physical activity and disability.Globally, an estimated 595 million people are affected, with the numbers likely to increase owing to an aging population and rising obesity rates.Effective pain management is crucial, yet current treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids, often provide limited relief and come with risks.One reason for this limited success is the insufficient recognition of the importance of psychosocial factors and heterogeneity of patients with OA (such as anxiety and depression), which can exacerbate pain and its impacts.The variability in patient pain experiences highlights the potential value of pain phenotyping, which involves a comprehensive assessment of pain characteristics to tailor treatments to individual needs.Antidepressants, particularly serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), show promise in alleviating both psychological symptoms and OA-related pain, but their effectiveness varies among individuals.Therefore, further research into standardized pain phenotyping methods and their integration into antidepressant treatment is needed to improve efficacy and minimize side effects through more personalized approaches.Key Points1. OA pain is influenced by psychosocial factors, making personalized pain phenotyping essential for effective treatment.2. Antidepressants, especially SNRIs, show potential in managing OA-related pain, but further research is needed to optimize their use through tailored approaches.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic disease where pain is the predominant clinical manifestation [1].This OA pain significantly impacts patients' quality of life and psychological well-being, often leading to reduced physical activity and, in some cases, disability [2].An estimated 595 million individuals worldwide have symptomatic OA, equal to 7.6% of the global population [3].With an aging population and the growing incidence of obesity, the OA burden is rapidly increasing and is projected to rise further over the next 25 years [4,5].Additionally, OA is associated with increased mortality, exacerbating its impact on public health [6].

The pain in OA arises from a complex interplay of factors, including biological, psychological, social, and lifestyle factors [7][8][9].Currently, pain management in OA typically involves a combination of lifestyle/behavioral and pharmacological approaches [1,10].Lifestyle/behavioral approaches include physical therapy, exercise, weight management, and joint protection strategies [11].Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen are frequently used to manage inflammation and relieve pain in patients with OA [12].However, existing therapies for OA often provide only modest relief and are frequently associated with harm, highlighting the urgent need to improve treatment strategies.Two significant reasons for the limited success in OA pain management may be the insufficient recognition of the heterogeneity of patients with OA and the impact of psychosocial factors on OA pain and its effects.

Psychosocial factors frequently influence clinical outcomes in knee OA.Studies have shown that depressive symptoms are associated with increased pain intensity in daily activities, and depression predicts worse health outcomes for those with knee pain [13][14][15].This highlights depression as a key determinant of pain levels in patients with OA.Studies showed that 40.4% of patients with OA suffered from either anxiety or depression [16,17], and anxiety and depression were positively correlated with pain symptoms in OA [18,19].Many studies have reported that antidepressants not only alleviate depression and anxiety symptoms in individuals with knee OA, but also have analgesic effects [20,21].In addition to pharmacological treatments, adjunctive psychological therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), either on its own or combined with exercise therapy, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing depression and distress, while also enhancing self-efficacy and alleviating OA pain [22][23][24][25].Moreover, addressing sleep disturbances, particularly in those with comorbid insomnia, can yield significant benefits, including improvements in both short-and long-term pain, depression, and fatigue [26].Mind-body therapies reduce depression and stress, boosting self-efficacy and improving quality of life for people in a variety of ways, including influencing adaptations in thinking or regulating breathing.These therapies can also help moderate the impact of pain on stress, providing a holistic approach to managing OA symptoms [12,27].

However, it remains a challenge to reduce pain, while minimizing side effects and meeting the needs of individual patients [28].To explore the underlying mechanisms and heterogeneity of OA pain, it is essential to thoroughly examine both the psychosocial factors and multidimensional aspects of pain.Studies have found inconsistent efficacy of different analgesics in the treatment of patients with OA, suggesting that there may be different pain phenotypes in the OA disease [29,30].Selecting appropriate pain management strategies requires a grasp of the pathophysiology of OA pain and pain phenotyping to accurately treat patients with OA.Therefore, this review intends to summarize current pain phenotyping in OA and review its potential value in the treatment of OA with antidepressants.


Characteristics and Classification of Pain Phenotyping

Generally, a phenotype refers to the observable characteristics of an organism, including its physical form, structure, developmental processes, biochemical and physiological characteristics, and behavior [29].OA is a heterogeneous and complex disease, encompassing a broad spectrum of molecular and clinical phenotypes [31].Over the past decade, research has increasingly focused on pain phenotypes as a key area in OA treatment [32,33].Pain phenotypes are expanded by incorporating patient-reported trait, symptoms, and responses to standardized pain sensitivity assessments, building upon the traditional phenotype [34], domain, and measure (Table 1).The current classification of pain phenotypes is described in the following sections.


Classification of Pain Based on Pathological Mechanisms

Pain in patients with OA may reflect both injury perception driven by joint tissue lesions and neuronal sensitization within both the peripheral and central nervous system [35,36].The pain in patients with OA can be viewed as a continuum, starting with nociceptive pain from peripheral tissue damage and ongoing inflammation after joint injury or strain.Joint trauma or overload can lead directly to nerve damage or trigger inflammation, subsequently leading to small fiber neuropathy and neurogenic inflammation.Continued promotion of pain through peripheral sensitization and neuroinflammation can lead to human assumed central sensitization [6].The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) classifies chronic pain into three categories: nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain [37].These symptoms are also believed to be common in patients with OA and can cooccur, as they are not mutually exclusive.1. Nociceptive pain: defined as "pain that arises from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue and is due to the activation of nociceptors," which involves joint tissue injury, causing osteophyte formation, subchondral bone change, and synovitis [6].

2. Neuropathic pain: defined as "pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system," and the underlying pathophysiology involves peripheral and human assumed central sensitization, including burning sensations, paresthesias, tingling, heightened sensitivity to mechanical and thermal stimuli, pain from normally nonpainful stimuli, episodic pain, and numbness [6,38,39].

3. Nociplastic pain: defined as "pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain" [40].Nociplastic pain is part of a wider systemic, multisystem process that results in pain and increased sensitivity to various sensory stimuli.The main symptoms include widespread pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive dysfunction, depression, and anxiety [41,42].However, identifying pain mechanisms is complex in practice, and the presence of a continuum of pain types (from nociceptive to neuropathic to nociplastic) in OA may lead to mixed pain symptoms.There are still no clear guidelines for the treatment of patients with mixed types [43,44].


Classification of Pain based on Clinical Symptoms

Joint pain from OA is intricate and affected by numerous factors beyond just the local joint pathology [45], shown in Fig. 1 [46].Pain phenotyping has significant potential in the treatment of OA and can distinguish patient groups on the basis of observable traits linked to variations in pain experience.According to IMMPACT recommendations, risk factors for pain development and persistence in OA, including psychosocial factors (anxiety and depression), sleep patterns, responses to harmful stimuli, endogenous pain regulation mechanisms, and responses to medication are included in the definition of pain phenotypes [34].These factors can contribute to human assumed central pain sensitization with reduced descending pain inhibition.Reflecting this, several recent studies have been conducted using different phenotyping analyses to characterize pain phenotypes in OA [36].

As presented in Table 2, previous studies have employed various phenotyping approaches methods to characterize the pain phenotype in OA, as explained below:

(1) Frequency and severity of pain: the frequency and severity of pain typically increase with worsening OA disease [47].Previous studies have used Intermittent and Constant Osteoarthritis Pain (ICOAP) scores to classify pain patterns in patients with OA, such as "no intermittent and constant pain (NP)," "intermittent pain only (IP)," "constant pain only (CP)," and "both (IACP)" [48].These knee pain patterns derived from ICOAP were linked to overall pain severity, disease duration, and extent of radiographic OA (ROA) [49].Another study utilizing the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain subscale identified four pain phenotypes of OA (no pain, mild pain during activity, mild pain both at rest and during activity, and moderate pain both at rest and during activity) and found that imaging lesions were associated with worse pain phenotypes [50].

(2) Cooccurring symptoms (combining observable characteristics): among older adults with hip or knee OA, the cooccurrence of centrally mediated symptoms was observed, leading to the identification of three distinct pain phenotypes.Murphy et al. assessed the cooccurrence of fatigue, sleep problems, mood disorders, and disease burden in hip or knee OA and identified three pain phenotypes.Group 1 exhibited high pain, fatigue, sleep problems, and mood disorders; group 2 had moderate depression and fatigue but mild pain and good sleep; and group 3 had the lowest levels of fatigue and depression, but poorer sleep compared with group 2 [51].In contrast, Kittelson et al. utilized latent profile analysis combined with psychological factors, comorbidity status, pain sensitivity, and muscle strength to determine OA pain phenotype, revealing significant differences in OA severity among the identified phenotypes [52].Another study grouped knee pain patients by physical performance and exercise-induced pain and identified five pain phenotypes: low pain sensitivity to pressure pain, average pain sensitivity across most modalities, high temporal summation of punctate pain, high cold pain sensitivity, and high sensitivity to heat pain and temporal summation of heat pain.It found that those with worst pain and lowest performance had unfavorable psychological traits, increased pain sensitivity, and temporal summation [53].In addition, studies further demonstrated that perceived stress may more strongly affect physical movement pain in non-Hispanic Black adults than in non-Hispanic white adults [54,55].Cardoso determined experimental pain phenotypic characteristics on the basis of health assessment (HAS) and quantitative sensory testing (QST) and classified older adults with knee OA into five categories: low pressure pain sensitivity, average pain sensitivity, high temporal summation (TS) of punctate pain, high cold pain sensitivity, and high heat pain sensitivity/high TS of heat pain.The study demonstrated significant differences between the least sensitive clusters relative to the other clusters [56].Finan et al. classified knee OA into four groups on the basis of patient-reported outcome (PRO) information, which included agreement between pain and structural changes, symptoms of anxiety and depression, quantitative sensory testing (QST), sleep, and pain catastrophizing.The groups were categorized as follows: low pain/low knee OA grade, high pain/high knee OA grade, low pain/high knee OA grade, and high pain/ low knee OA grade.The study found that pain sensitivity was significantly higher in the high pain/low knee OA grade and lower in the low pain/high knee OA grade group [57].Another study identified five pain phenotypes  ① high prevalence of emotional problems and low prevalence of structural damage; ② high prevalence of structural damage and low prevalence of emotional problems; ③ low prevalence of emotional problems and low prevalence of structural damage QST; CES-D; PCS ① low-moderate proportion of people with both pressure pain sensitivity and facilitated TS; ② low proportions-to-none with pressure pain sensitivity and facilitated TS; ③ high proportion with pressure pain sensitivity and moderate proportion with facilitated TS; ④ high proportion of facilitated TS but very low proportion-to-none with pressure pain sensitivity in patients with OA by analyzing psychological assessments, pain characteristics, QST, physical function, and quality of life.These phenotypes included "low pain," "average pain," and three "high pain" groups sensitive to different pain modalities, such as punctate, heat, and temporal summation.It was found that high sensitivity to punctate, heat, and pressure pain corresponded with higher levels of clinical pain [58].Additionally, studies have defined knee OA phenotypes on the basis of pain symptoms, primarily using cross-sectional data and focusing on pain sensitivity [36].Two large prospective cohort studies have documented longitudinal relationships between pain phenotypes and pain intensity over time.Pan et al. used latent class analysis to identify pain phenotypes on the basis of factors, such as body mass index (BMI), sex, emotional issues, education, comorbidities, pain sites, and structural pathology.They identified three distinct groups: group 1, with high emotional problems and low structural injuries; group 2, with high structural injuries and low emotional problems; and group 3, with both low emotional problems and low structural injuries.These findings indicated that psychological and structural factors interact to intensify pain perception [59].Carlesso et al. used psychometric measures and QST to identify four phenotypes, and these pain phenotypes were characterized primarily by varying levels of pressure pain sensitivity and facilitatory temporal summation-categorized as low/absent, moderate, or high.These variations in sensitization reflect different measures of pain susceptibility [32].

In conclusion, OA can be categorized into various pain phenotypes on the basis of objective measures and PRO information, where the choice of input variables [especially WOMAC, QST, and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D)] is of some importance for cluster assignment [60].However, as yet, there is no consistent or standardized methodology.


Antidepressants as Potential Therapeutic

Agents in Osteoarthritis  [63].Human assumed central sensitization in patients with OA was significantly associated with depression, and dysfunction caused by depression exacerbates patients' perception of pain, resulting in more severe pain [64,65].A recent meta-analysis found a moderate positive link between the severity of osteoarthritis pain and depression (r = 0.36, P < 0.001) [19].Consequently, treating depression in patients with OA has gained attention, with antidepressants thought to also alleviate pain by influencing neural pathways in the central nervous system.A systematic review showed that the use of antidepressants for knee OA resulted in nonclinically meaningful improvements in mean pain and function, but there were meaningful differences in those reporting clinically meaningful response (≥ 50%) [66] (Table 3).This perspective aligns with findings from Hawker et al., who explored the link between pain and depression in elderly individuals with OA.Their research indicated that pain increases fatigue and disability, which in turn elevates depressive symptoms, though depressive symptoms do not increase pain.Additionally, there is a bidirectional relationship between depressive symptoms and fatigue, a challenging result to quantify in OA, but one that may help explain the modest average improvements in pain and function seen in some studies [67].Currently, the main classifications and functions of commonly used antidepressants for the treatment of OA are as follows.


Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs)

Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs) inhibit the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, enhancing the levels of these neurotransmitters.This dual action could be used to regulate mood and provide pain relief [68].Common drugs in this class include duloxetine (Cymbalta) and venlafaxine (Effexor).Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) and the American College of Rheumatology recommend duloxetine for managing chronic musculoskeletal pain, including OA pain [69].A meta-analysis showed that the duloxetine group experienced significant 24-h average pain reduction compared with the control group [mean difference (MD), − 0.74, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.57, P < 0.001], but the potential clinical impact still warrants further investigation [70].One systematic review reported that duloxetine significantly improved quality of life [standardized mean difference (SMD): 0.40; 95% CI 0.26-0.53][71], and that the duloxetine group scored 11.3 points (95% CI 5.8-16.8)higher on the pain domain of the KOOS/HOOS compared with the control (P < 0.001) [72].The study evaluated the efficacy of duloxetine at the individual level using the IMMPACT and OARSI-Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) responder criteria, showing that the duloxetine group reduced pain intensity and improved physical functioning compared with the placebo group (30% responders: 70.1% versus 54.9%, P = 0.003) [73].However, some results did not support these findings [74].Additionally, one study proposed that venlafaxine could be effective for OA pain, but further validation is necessary [75].The typical side effects of SNRIs were gastrointestinal problems, fatigue, sexual dysfunction, and somnolence [71].


Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) function by blocking the reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine, while also impacting acetylcholine and histamine systems [4].This broadspectrum mechanism is believed to help alleviate chronic pain, making TCAs, such as amitriptyline (Elavil) and nortriptyline (Pamelor), options.TCAs are the most used medications for chronic pain patients [76].Still, their analgesic effect in OA appears limited, and studies have shown that nortriptyline does not significantly alleviate pain in patients with OA (95% CI − 0.26 to 12.6, P = 0.06) [77].In addition, TCAs may lead to side effects, such as dry mouth, confusion, drowsiness, cardiac conduction abnormalities, urinary retention, weight gain, nightmares, and orthostatic hypotension [66,77,78].


Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)

Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) work by inhibiting the reuptake of serotonin in the brain, which increases its availability in the synaptic cleft.Common SSRIs include fluoxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram.Although SSRIs are primarily prescribed for depression, they have also demonstrated some efficacy in managing chronic pain conditions, such as OA.One study showed significant pain relief in fluoxetine group towards the end of treatment (MD, 0.24, 95% CI − 0.47 to 0.95; ≥ 50% reduction in pain: MD, 1.55, 95% CI 1.32-1.82)[79].Although SSRIs were the most prescribed drugs for depression, they are not recommended as first-line treatments for chronic pain owing to the increased risk of hemorrhages, such as gastrointestinal bleeding and hemorrhagic stroke, associated with SSRIs [78].

In addition, beyond antidepressants alone, it should be paired up with other forms of treatment.Research suggests that the combination of duloxetine and S-flurbiprofen plaster (for local pain relief) for chronic pain due to OA is as effective as, or even more effective than, the combination of duloxetine with conventional NSAIDs [80].

In brief, antidepressants could lead to an improvement in average OA pain, with the most studies on duloxetine, but there is heterogeneity in different trials, and they also can have adverse effects.Further studies could consider antidepressants for populations selected on the basis of pain phenotyping.For example, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of specific OA pain phenotypes, longitudinal cohort studies looking at the natural progression of pain and response to antidepressants, and case-control studies comparing treatment outcomes across phenotypic groups.In addition, crossover trials could be considered to assess the efficacy of antidepressants in relieving OA pain in participants with different phenotypes.


The Impact of Pain Phenotyping on Antidepressant Efficacy

Pain and depression are often comorbid conditions whose effects are not clearly directional and are likely to be bidirectional [81], thus complicating diagnosis and treatment.Antidepressants, particularly SNRIs, have shown efficacy in treating various types of pain, including OA pain [82].High-certainty evidence indicates that using antidepressants for knee osteoarthritis leads to an improvement in 24-h mean pain, but the effectiveness of these medications can vary significantly among patients with OA, highlighting the potential of pain phenotyping to improve treatment targeting and response [66], which involves categorizing pain on the basis of its characteristics and underlying mechanisms, including the concomitant presence of depression, sensitization, and other psychological/other factors.However, to our knowledge, only one study to date has specifically focused on identifying different responses to antidepressants according to patients and disease characteristics.

A randomized clinical trial found that patients with OA with human assumed central sensitization symptoms (e.g., widespread pain, fatigue) experienced significantly more pain relief with duloxetine compared with patients without human assumed central sensitization symptoms (neuropathic pain) [72].Specifically, a total of 111 patients with OA with centralized pain were randomized in the study.

Approximately 44% of patients reported feeling much improved in duloxetine group, compared with the control group (P < 0.001).Patients in the duloxetine group experienced an improvement of 11.3 points (95% CI 5.8-16.8) in the pain domain of the knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score/hip dysfunction and osteoarthritis outcome score (KOOS/HOOS; P < 0.001).Additionally, knee patients experienced greater improvement than hip, with an 18.7-point increase (95% CI 11.3-26.1)for knee patients versus a 6.0-point increase (95% CI −2.6 to 14.5) for hip patients.This study highlighted the potential importance of identifying human assumed central sensitization as a pain phenotype, suggesting that pain phenotyping may guide the use and selection of patients more likely to benefit from antidepressants.

Theoretically, personalized treatment based on pain phenotyping has the potential to improve patient outcomes.Personalized treatment plans considering an individual's specific pain phenotype can lead to more effective pain relief, reduced side effects, and improved quality of life.However, the actual benefits of this approach, including enhanced treatment adherence and enhanced efficacy of antidepressants in managing osteoarthritis pain, have yet to be fully validated.Therefore, further research into standardized pain phenotyping methods and their integration into antidepressant treatment is needed to improve efficacy and minimize side effects through more personalized approaches, thereby filling this critical knowledge gap.


Future Directions and Implications

Advancing the use of antidepressants in managing osteoarthritic pain requires refining pain-phenotyping techniques.Future research should focus on developing sophisticated tools for precise and standardized pain phenotyping, which will help in understanding the heterogeneity of patients with OA and in considering all relevant psychosocial factors in the experience of pain and its impacts.This includes incorporating culturally contextualized assessments and utilizing mixed methods, such as large prospective cohort studies supported by advanced technologies, such as AI.Such advancements could lead to a more accurate understanding of pain mechanisms and treatment responses, allowing for more targeted and effective use of antidepressants in patients with OA.

In addition, there is a need to explore beyond the mere efficacy of antidepressants.Identifying subgroups of patients who respond best to specific treatments and studying combination therapies could offer valuable insights.Standardizing the assessment of pain phenotypes and tailoring treatment regimens to individual patients will enhance personalized pain management.However, further research is needed to determine the best methods for pain phenotyping and to evaluate whether these techniques can improve care, particularly before integrating them into routine practice.


Conclusions

The application of pain phenotypes to guide OA treatment, including the use of antidepressants, is an emerging area of research.However, more studies are needed to develop standardized methods for pain phenotyping and to assess how these phenotypes can effectively enhance the efficacy of antidepressant treatments for OA.
