Introduction:Running speech sampling is an essential component of a paediatric voice evaluation, in that it should provide the examiner with a representative vocal sample of the child's everyday voice use outside of the clinic setting.Current speech sampling practices, consisting of reading tasks, informal conversation sampling and the voice question ('Tell me about your voice problem'), may not elicit consistent samples or samples of sufficient length to allow the examiner to make a reliable and valid judgement about vocal quality.The aim of this study is to compare the voice quality of children produced in response to existing running speech sampling methods and narrative elicitation tasks commonly used in language sampling activities, which are hypothesized to elicit longer units of connected speech.Methods: Fifty-three children participated in the following tasks: prolonged vowels /a/ and /i/, the CAPE-V sentences.The reference standard running speech sampling tasks were: the voice question, an informal conversation sample and the Rainbow Passage.The comparison running speech sampling tasks were: My First Day, the Bus Story Test and the Test of Narrative Language-2.All tasks were audio-recorded.The voice samples were independently rated by two trained speech-language pathologists using the CAPE-V instrument.Inter-rater reliability was acceptable for all perceptual tasks (ICC 0.901-1.00).CAPE-V ratings were considered the primary comparison measure; smoothed cepstral peak prominence was considered a secondary comparison measure.Diagnostic indicators, sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values were calculated in order to evaluate the levels of diagnostic decision-making of the comparison tasks compared to the existing reference standard tasks.Results: On perceptual analysis, weak to strong correlations (r = 0.291-0.697)were observed between reference tasks, and weak to very strong correlations between reference and comparison tasks (r = 0.200-0.747).When diagnostic utility was compared, one or more indicators were at an acceptable level between the conversation sample and the narrative tasks, and the voice question and three out of four narrative tasks.Conclusions: A narrative elicitation procedure, such as a re-tell or story generation task, may be suitable for eliciting a running speech sample in paediatric voice evaluations.Both the acoustic properties of the vocal signal and the perceptual properties of This is an open access article under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivs License, which permits use and distribution in any medium, provided the original work is properly cited, the use is non-commercial and no modifications or adaptations are made.

Introduction

A running speech sample is an essential component of a voice assessment.It serves the purposes of eliciting symptomatology, especially that which emerges with vocal use and/or fatigue, and providing a representation of an individual's everyday, speaking voice, used in settings outside the clinic (Oates 2009).Various methodologies are used to elicit running speech: the Consensus Auditory-Perceptual Evaluation of Voice (CAPE-V) protocol recommends asking the client, 'Tell me about your voice problem' or 'Tell me how your voice is functioning' ('the voice question') (Kempster et al. 2009).The recommendation is for a minimum of ten to twenty seconds of 'natural conversational speech' (Kempster et al. 2009).Perceptual evaluation is an important tool in the three-pronged approach to diagnosis of voice disorders, with acoustic analysis and assessment of the impact of the voice on quality of life.Additionally, instrumental analysis, including laryngeal visualization and aerodynamic analysis, is an essential component of voice evaluation, alongside the three-pronged approach.

In order to produce a running speech sample of 20 s in length, an individual must produce speech at the discourse level of linguistic processing.However, individuals may not reflect on their voice at all, or may not reflect in enough detail to produce 20 s of speech (Connor et al. 2008;Golub et al. 2006).Consequently, many practitioners use informal, questioning techniques to elicit a speech sample, of at least 20 s of natural conversational speech, for the purposes of perceptual voice analysis (Lee et al. 2004;Reynolds and Fleury 2022).To account for individuals who may not produce sufficient running speech samples in response to such prompts, the authors of the CAPE-V recommend that a client's vocal quality be monitored throughout the assessment session, presumably with a view to adding these observations to the perceptual component of the evaluation (Kempster et al. 2009).This practice assumes that the client can, and will, engage in conversation and other verbal interactions throughout the session.

In children, informal conversation sampling techniques do not consistently yield sufficiently lengthy running speech samples (Reynolds and Fleury 2022).For some young children, engaging in a conversation with an unfamiliar adult may be beyond developmental expectations (Paul 2007).Other children may not have sufficient language skills for this task or may be reluctant for other reasons.These may include: traits such as shyness, which are known to affect performance on expressive language assessment tasks; or the development of executive functioning abilities, such as metanalytical skills (Connor et al. 2008;Melnick and Kucker 2023;Paul 2007).In order to overcome these limitations, a common recommendation is to elicit running speech from a reading sample (Patel et al. 2018).However, this assumes that the individual has attained a certain level of literacy.Indeed, many of the reading passages commonly used, such as The Rainbow Passage (Fairbanks 1960), require a reading level of a typically-developing junior high schooler (Reilly and Fisher 2012;Reynolds and Fleury 2022).Further, listeners judge the perceptual qualities of a 'reading voice' differently from those of voices used in conversational tasks, thus calling into question the ecological validity of reading tasks (Zraick et al. 2005).While existing practices may be adequate for adults and some children, many children will not be able to meet the cognitive and linguistic demands of the tasks used in current speech sampling practices, comfortably or at all.It is essential to understand what level of support is required for all children to produce a running speech sample.The speech sample should be of sufficient length to allow accurate perceptual evaluation of its properties, as well as represent the child's everyday speaking voice.

Other protocols for assessing children's speech are extant; for example, the Pediatric Radboud Dysarthria Assessment (pRDA) and the Park Play scene (Patel and Connaghan 2014;Ruessink et al. 2022).The Park Play scene is a picture description task; the pRDA is a pediatric adaptation of a test used to elicit speech in adults with motor speech disorders and includes reading tasks.While these tasks were developed to elicit speech from children, they were designed for use with individuals with motor speech disorders (Patel and Connaghan 2014;Ruessink et al. 2022).The considerations are therefore slightly different to those of voice disorders; for example, articulation and prosody, while important considerations in voice, are key markers in dysarthria (Patel et al. 2018).These tasks are designed to elicit certain speech targets in children, but discourse-level production of the targets is not required (Ruessink et al. 2022).A discourse-level, running speech sample is essential in the evaluation of voice, as it provides an insight into how individuals use their voices outside the clinic setting (Kempster et al. 2009, forthcoming;Oates 2009).Some speech-language pathologists develop informal measures for assessing speech in children, based on their situational needs, yet the psychometric properties of these are not always strong (Limbrick et al. 2013).Informal sampling is an important tool for speech-language pathologists, but it does not consistently produce a 10-to-20 s running speech sample (Reynolds and Fleury 2022).

Narrative sampling tasks are communication tasks that can be completed with various levels of scaffolding: generating a story about a given topic, re-telling a familiar story or re-telling a story after hearing it told by a clinician (Southwood and Russell 2004).Such practices elicit a unit of discourse, which is essentially a running speech sample.Using a narrative sampling task as a way to elicit a unit of running speech in paediatric voice evaluations may pose several advantages over existing practices.Because of the presence and nature of scaffolding, narrative tasks may be more accessible to individuals of varying abilities across the lifespan (Mooney et al. 2021;Spencer et al. 2015).Narrative tasks can be used to evaluate various aspects of linguistic performance and are believed to represent an ecologically valid representation of a child's everyday communication skills (Southwood and Russell 2004).Conversely, a narrative sample collected as part of a voice evaluation has the potential to provide valuable information about a child's language skills, as well as provide a sufficiently long unit of running speech from which to make a judgement of vocal quality.However, it is currently unclear whether the vocal qualities used by children in narrative sampling tasks are equivalent to the vocal qualities used in existing speech sampling tasks.

Further, while there is no reason to suspect that the majority of children with dysphonia present with co-morbid language difficulties, some populations of children who are at risk of dysphonia are also at risk of communication differences.Language delay, including executive function difficulties, is are well-documented consequence of prematurity, for example, even at moderate-tolate preterm birth (Sandoval et al. 2022;Woythaler 2019).Children with neurodevelopmental disorders such as Down Syndrome and congenital high airway obstruction syndrome may experience difficulties with receptive or expressive language, or both (Cleland et al. 2010;Jeong et al. 2020;Kent and Vorperian 2013).Even in the absence of obvious risk factors, a narrative sample could be a useful tool for screening language skills in the general paediatric population.

The CAPE-V is widely used in adult and paediatric voice evaluations around the world.The CAPE-V was designed to standardize the vocal stimuli elicited in voice evaluations to increase the accuracy and reliability of listener judgement across clinical contexts (Kempster et al. 2009;Zraick et al. 2011).Of note are recent findings that use of the CAPE-V varies in clinical practice (Lodhavia and Kempster forthcoming).A revised version has recently been published (CAPE-Vr), removing the voice question prompt and replacing it with a question about travel, amongst other changes (Kempster et al. forthcoming).This change reflects the difficulties associated with the voice question.The travel question is reflective of the type of question asked in informal conversation sampling and may therefore yield similarly inconsistent speech in children.Thus, there remains a need to investigate running speech sampling practices in children, and such investigations could provide valuable information to improve consistency and participation by children of all ages and abilities.

There is a need to investigate speech sampling practices that both provide sufficient support to children with varying needs and abilities and facilitate the production of a 10-to-20 s running speech sample.Narrative elicitation practices may fulfil this need, but the vocal qualities produced by children completing these tasks (e.g., story generation, narrative re-tell, procedural re-tell) have yet to be determined.To date, there is no research in the literature investigating voice quality in children's narratives.This study aimed to determine whether various discourselevel speaking tasks (cf.word or sentence-level speaking tasks), specifically those in the narrative genre, would elicit a clinicallyuseful running speech sample in paediatric speakers.It was hypothesized that:

1.The voice question would elicit neither a meaningful response, defined as reflecting the child's opinion of their vocal quality, nor a running speech sample of at least 10 s in length.

2. The voice quality of children in narrative tasks is consistent with voice quality in conversational speech.

3. The voice quality of children when producing reading passages (The Rainbow Passage and My First Day) would not be consistent with the other, discourse-level speaking tasks.


Method


Participants

Fifty-three participants were recruited from the community via social media, word of mouth and through personal and professional contacts of the researchers.The snowball effect was considered a benefit to recruitment in this study.Study data were collected in northern New York and the Chicagoland area.At the time of study participation, children were aged between 5.58 and 12.92 years old (median = 9.17 years; IQR 7.58-10.92).Distribution of sex assigned at birth and dysphonia severity can be seen in Table 1.The study was conducted under informed consent from the child's caregiver, and assent from the child where appropriate.

Ethics approval was received from the Institutional Review Board of the relevant institutions.

Children were eligible for the study if they were aged between 5 and 13 years and were able to participate in the study tasks with appropriate support from an adult (e.g., scaffolds as allowed in the testing materials).Multilingual children were eligible for study participation if they were able to participate in the study in English language.Children who did not speak English or who presented with a medical condition precluding them from assenting to study participation were not eligible for study participation.


Speaking Tasks

The standard voice symptomatology tasks of two prolonged vowels (/a/ and /i/) and the CAPE-V sentences were elicited from each participant.In terms of running speech samples, the tasks extracted from existing protocols, that is, the voice question, informal questioning about a topic of interest to the participant, and the Rainbow Passage (Fairbanks 1960), were used as reference tasks.The comparison tasks were: My First Day (Ben-David et al. 2016), the Bus Story Test (Renfrew 2010) and the production tasks from the Test of Narrative Language (2nd edition) (TNL) (Renfrew 2010).In order to standardize the comparisons, the running speech samples produced in the different conditions were concatenated with the prolonged vowels and the CAPE-V sentences, so that only the running speech sample differed.Samples were de-identified and randomly ordered for the purposes of conducting ratings.

The Bus Story narrative re-tell test was chosen because the normative sample includes children as young as 3 years 6 months, thus potentially increasing the generalizability of these findings (Renfrew 2010).My First Day is a reading passage that was designed to minimize variability in vocal productions associated with semantic and syntactic qualities of existing reading passages (Ben-David et al. 2016).The TNL is a standardized assessment of discourse-level communication that uses different narrative sampling methodologies, including narrative re-tell, story generation from a picture sequence, and story generation following a structural model (Gillam and Pearson 2017).The battery of comparison speaking tasks was curated to ensure that a variety of sampling techniques, suitable for a variety of ages, would be evaluated in this study.Each of these tasks is a discourse-level speaking task, with differing cognitive demands because of the task instructions and scaffolding required (e.g., reading tasks require decoding skills; narrative tasks require the child to create a mental image of the scenario, for cohesion and reflect this in their oral response).


Recording Conditions

Recordings were made by the first, third and fourth authors in a quiet university environment with an ambient noise level of <50 dB.A headset-mounted, Røde lavalier microphone was positioned at a 45 • to 90 • angle, 4 to 5 cm away from participants' mouths.Recordings were collected using a Zoom H2N Handicorder at a sampling rate of 44.1 Hz into a .wavformat.

3 Data analysis


Perceptual Analysis

The CAPE-V protocol was used to conduct the perceptual evaluations.This protocol recommends that the following tasks be used: prolonged vowels /a/ and /i/, the CAPE-V sentences, and a 10 to 20-s running speech sample.The protocol recommends that the running speech be elicited by using the Voice Question.

All perceptual analyses were conducted by the first and second authors, who are speech-language pathologists with greater than 10 years' experience in the assessment and treatment of voice disorders across the lifespan.The ratings were conducted across several joint rating sessions, with each listener rating the voices independently.Ratings were compared at the end of each session.Prior to each rating session, a joint listening exercise was performed in order to increase consistency of judgements and establish a common anchoring benchmark.Inter-rater reliability was calculated for each speaking task, using a twoway mixed effect, consistency intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC).


Acoustic Analysis

Voice samples were checked to ensure a signal-to-noise ratio of at least 32 dB.Smoothed cepstral peak prominence (CPPs) was selected for acoustic analysis as the measure is robust in both prolonged vowels and connected speech (Antonetti et al. 2020).All acoustic analyses were conducted using the program Praat (Boersma and Weenink 2021).In order to clean the voice samples, the pitch contour was used as a guide to trim out non-speaking portions of continuous speech samples, as well as remove examiner voices (where relevant).The middle section of each of the prolonged vowels was isolated (either 2 or 3 s, depending on the overall length of the sample).The first twelve seconds of running speech were extracted from each of the discourse-level tasks, after non-speaking portions of the sample were removed (Infusino et al. 2015).CPPs were calculated, using the protocols described by Infusino and colleagues for the stimuli (i.e., length of samples, preparation of samples including removal of non-voiced segments), and Murton and colleagues for the software settings (Infusino et al. 2015;Murton et al. 2020).


Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows version 29.Investigation of the strength of relationships between reference (i.e., informal conversation sample, Rainbow Passage and voice question) and comparison tasks (i.e., My First Day, the Bus Story and the TNL-2) were conducted using correlational analyses.Due to the data distribution, Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficient was used to compare both perceptual and acoustic vocal qualities across tasks.A p value cutoff of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.The strength of the correlation coefficients was interpreted based on the scale proposed by Chan and detailed by Akoglu (Akoglu 2018;Chan 2003).The diagnostic metrics of sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value, and sum of predictive values were calculated.A cutoff of 80% for sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values was used, as prescribed by Dollaghan (2007).The disease was considered to be absent when the mean CAPE-V rating was <5; a voice sample with a mean CAPE-V rating of 5 or greater was considered to be dysphonic.Interpretation of ICC values was based on Koo and Li (2016).

Analysis of Reference Tasks

Perceptual and acoustic comparisons of the reference tasks can be seen in Table 2. Acoustic correlations were strong to very strong for the relationships between each task (r 2 = 0.694, 0.695, 721).While the correlation between the perceptual ratings of the conversation sample and the Rainbow Passage was weak (r 2 = 0.291), the correlations between the conversation sample and the voice question (r 2 = 0.506), as well as the voice question and the Rainbow Passage (r 2 = 0.697), were strong.Diagnostic performance of the reference standard tasks was evaluated using the metrics of sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values, and the corresponding data are presented in Table 3. Relationships were strongest between the voice question and the Rainbow Passage, and weakest between the Rainbow Passage and the conversation sample.This suggests that diagnostic decisions may vary, based on which of the existing running speech sampling protocols is selected for inclusion in a voice evaluation.


Perceptual Analyses

Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficient was used to compare perceptual ratings of voice quality on the reference and speaking tasks.All comparison tasks were strongly correlated with the conversation sample (r = 0.417-0.657),while more variability was seen with the voice question (weak to very strong; r = 0.200-0.693)and the Rainbow Passage (weak to strong; r = 0.200-0.599).The relevant pairwise comparisons are presented in Table 4.The diagnostic performance of each comparison task was compared to that of each reference task by calculating the metrics of sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values.The metrics were calculated using SPSS, and these data are presented in Table 5.


Inter-rater Reliability

An ICC of >0.9 was achieved for each task, details of which can be seen in Table 6, indicating acceptable agreement between raters for the perceptual analyses.Given the acceptable inter-rater reliability, the mean of the CAPE-V score rating (i.e., rater 1 score +rater 2 score/2) assigned by both raters was used as the outcome measure to analyze the perceptual data.


Acoustic Analysis

Smoothed CPP was calculated separately for each speaking task.Descriptive statistics for CPPs can be found in Table 7.Consistent with previous literature, CPPs values for prolonged vowels were higher for prolonged vowels than for connected speech tasks   (Murton et al. 2020).Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficient was calculated for each reference task and speaking task, and the relevant pairwise comparisons can be seen in Table 8 ( Maryn et al. 2010).Strong to very strong correlations were found for each task.


Task Type Comparisons

Perceptual analysis of task type showed that the reading tasks were strongly correlated (ρ = 0.599, p < 0.001), as were the story generation (ρ = 0.648, p < 0.001) and re-tell tasks (ρ = 0.659, p < 0.001), suggesting some effect of task type.Stronger correlations were observed in the acoustic analysis for the reading tasks (ρ = 0.879, p < 0.001), story generation (ρ = 0.913, p < 0.001) and narrative re-tell tasks (ρ = 0.664, p < 0.001).


Qualitative Analyses

Of the 53 participants, 25 (47.1%)responses to the voice question did not elicit a unit of connected speech.Alternative responses included silence, laughter, a one-or two-syllable utterance (e.g., 'good', 'it's okay') or a gesture (e.g. a thumbs up, a shrug).These data were unable to be perceptually analyzed, resulting in a smaller sample size for this task.The age range of these participants was 5;6 to 11;5 years.Nine children (17%) were unable to participate in one or both of the reading tasks and participated in these tasks via sentence repetition from the examiner.A further 14 participants (26.4%) read with hesitancy.Just over half of the participants were able to complete the reading passages with fluency and confidence (50.9%).


Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the properties of running speech sampling tasks in the evaluation of paediatric voice.Diagnostic performance of existing reference standard tasks was determined and compared to the diagnostic performance of narrative elicitation practices.The voice quality of children when responding to different elicitation practices was compared, with a view to determining whether narrative sampling procedures that are currently used in the evaluation of paediatric language would be suitable for eliciting a running speech sample as part of a voice evaluation.The notion of using narrative sampling procedures to elicit running speech samples in paediatric voice evaluation is not new (Lee et al. 2004).However, in the two decades since publication of a major, formal and detailed screening protocol in paediatric voice, this is the first study, to the authors' knowledge, which has investigated vocal quality in children across a range of discourse genres.

With a few exceptions, the present findings demonstrate moderate to very strong correlations between the voice quality used by children when producing both the reference standard tasks and the comparison tasks.While ideally, strong to very strong correlations would give greater confidence in the criterion-referenced validity of the comparison tasks, the correlations are equivalent to those of the reference standard tasks, in that they range from moderate to strong.This suggests that current sampling practices lack consistency and that judgements of dysphonia severity may vary, depending on the running speech sampling task selected by the examiner.

It should be noted that the sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values of the comparison tasks were also inconsistent when compared against the reference standards.However, comparisons between the reference standard task yielded similar inconsistencies, with no comparator demonstrating acceptable values across the four statistics.No task emerged with consistently acceptable (>80%) values across all tasks, although the total sensitivity and specificity were adequate for comparisons between the conversation sample and the McDonald's Story, the Late for School Story and the Alien Story, as well as the voice question and the Alien Story.Thus, the comparison tasks, while imperfect, perform similarly to the reference standard tasks-which are also imperfect.

Given that the difficulties associated with the reference standard tasks-the inconsistent participation in the voice question, different vocal qualities when reading compared with conversational speech, and the inconsistency in production of conversational speech-substituting a task with more predictable output, that is strongly correlated with vocal qualities used in conversational speech, may be preferable in the practice of evaluating paediatric voice.Therefore, substituting one of the comparison tasks, or task types, could provide adequate information to a listener seeking to make a judgment of the presence, nature and severity of dysphonia.

This practice would be advantageous in that an examiner can display confidence that children seeking voice evaluations will be able to participate in the task.The task should yield a running speech sample that reflects everyday speaking quality to at least the same degree as current procedures.Additionally, the narrative sample produced could be analyzed if there were concerns about receptive and expressive language skills or used as a screener to determine whether further evaluation of language skills is warranted.The practice does not add any time to the evaluation process, and it may even streamline it somewhat.

Current language sampling practices in language (and therefore, voice) evaluations take a minimum of 15 min (Manning et al. 2020).Conducting a narrative re-tell may be completed in less time, depending on the properties of the stimulus task, providing a time advantage as well as a sampling reliability advantage.


Hypothesis A. The voice question

Given that almost half of the children who participated in this study did not produce a running speech sample in response to the voice question, it cannot be said that this is a reliable way of obtaining a running speech sample in paediatric voice evaluations.These children represented the age range of the larger sample; therefore, it is difficult to predict based on age the utility of the voice question.Some children were able to produce both a long unit of speech and a meaningful reflection on the operation of their voice.However, some of these participants interpreted the voice question as requiring a response describing how the vocal mechanism operates.Previous findings suggest that children as young as four may reflect on their vocal quality (Connor et al. 2008;Verduyckt et al. 2011); these findings provide some confirmation, but with a caveat relating to the child's abilities.Formal surveys of voice-related quality of life in children will help to explore these issues in a clinical setting (Adriaansen et al. 2022;Amir et al. forthcoming).If further and more indepth reflection were needed, the formal voice question may be successful, but the likelihood decreases with decreasing age.Therefore, in order to accurately and consistently survey a wide age range of children's free-form reflections about their voices, there is a need to develop unambiguous prompts, in plain language, to scaffold such thought processes.


Hypothesis 2. Narrative tasks compared to other, discourse-level tasks

While both story generation and narrative re-tell tasks were moderately correlated with conversational speech as the reference standard, a re-tell procedure may be preferable in voice evaluations because of the greater control over the stimulus material.That is, stimuli can be developed to include items that may elicit particular laryngeal behaviours of interest to the diagnostic process, similarly to the way in which the CAPE-V sentences were developed.In the meantime, a retell task such as those used in this study could be used to elicit a running speech sample in paediatric voice assessments.Some information about narrative macro-and micro-structure would also be available for analysis (Gillam and Pearson 2017;Renfrew 2010).

Correlation of the stories in the TNL-2 with the Bus Story tended to be weakest.The Bus Story was included because of the basal age of the normative sample and its potential application to a wider age range of children (3;6 years).The Bus Story is a test that was developed in England, using a British English dialect (Renfrew 2010).It is used in the United States (Gabig 2008;Spencer et al. 2015); however, these current data suggest that there is something about the Bus Story that may prompt children to use different vocal qualities.It could be that some of the words, and possibly syntax, were less familiar to the participants in this study (e.g., use of terms such as 'mended' and 'splattered'), required increased cognitive load to contextualize, and impacted voice quality.

Diagnostic decision-making was most inconsistent when the Rainbow Passage was used as the reference standard, with acceptable diagnostic utility having been demonstrated for My First Day, but not for any other task.This is supported by the correlational analysis, where the reading passages and the more literate narrative tasks were strongly correlated.This suggests that vocal quality during reading passages was rated differently from that of other tasks, and that vocal quality may differ, depending on the language level of the task.

Substituting the Alien Story, McDonald's Story or the Late for School story for the conversation sample, and the Alien Story for the voice question, yielded the most consistency in diagnostic decision-making.The advantage of using the narrative tasks in this context is participation: all participants were able to participate in both tasks, whereas approximately half were unable to produce a running speech sample in response to the voice question.


Informal Conversation Sampling

Informal conversation sampling was used as the reference standard running speech task.The conversation samples were strongly correlated with all other running speech tasks.This suggests that, regardless of the context and level of scaffolding provided to children in narrative tasks, they tended to use a vocal quality that was similar to that of their conversational voices.However, consistent with previous literature (Reynolds and Fleury 2022), the conversation samples were of inconsistent length and topic.It was difficult to predict the length of time that would be required to elicit a running speech sample, as well as the topic of conversation that would prompt the child to speak for at least 20 s.Therefore, there may be value in introducing narrative tasks to paediatric voice evaluations, in terms of both a reduction in time taken to complete the task and increased confidence in the length and quality of the running speech sample.


Task Type

These data suggest that story-generation tasks yield vocal qualities that are most strongly correlated with conversational speech.Narrative re-tells, however, are also strongly correlated.When recommending speech sampling practices, it is important to consider the purposes of using a narrative task for running speech sampling in voice evaluations.The paediatric language literature points to narrative re-tell tasks as being preferable to generation tasks, in terms of completeness of story structure and use of linguistic devices; story generation tasks are also associated with shorter units of language (Merritt and Liles 1989).Therefore, re-tells may provide the following: a longerrunning speech sample, a more representative sample of a child's language skills, and more information to appraise when considering the dual purpose of language sampling.Additionally, the higher level of scaffolding provided in the stimulus may result in an increased yield of target concepts, which could represent speaking behaviours of relevance to voice evaluations (Bowles et al. 2020).


Hypothesis 3. Reading passages

The Rainbow Passage, when used as a reference standard, was moderately to strongly correlated with both My First Day and the Alien Story.Correlations between other tasks were weak, suggesting that listeners judge reading voices differently from other tasks.It is hypothesized that cognitive load affects vocal functioning (Pyfrom et al. forthcoming), and although the data investigating this hypothesis are limited, this may account for some of the variance seen in this study.Just over half of the participants were able to read the passages with confidence, defined by the researchers as without hesitation and without a change in vocal quality.The correlation between the Rainbow Passage and the Alien Story was not predicted but is perhaps related to the formality of the narrative task.The Alien Story is elicited from a picture, following a model of a complex episode (Gillam and Pearson 2017), which is arguably one of the more literate fictional narrative genres.It may be that the increased complexity of this task posed similar cognitive demands on participants as the reading passages, resulting in similar vocal qualities.

Diagnostic statistics were also comparable across reading tasks, suggesting that a reading passage could be used to elicit a running speech sample in clinical populations who can read.The increased imageability of My First Day, compared to the Rainbow Passage, may decrease cognitive load for less-confident readers.However, only half of the children were able to engage with the reading passages with a degree of confidence.Perceptually, the vocal qualities of many children were inconsistent with the vocal qualities used in conversational speech.Reading passages may be useful in eliciting particular vocal targets, but they did not appear to elicit ecologically valid voice use in this paediatric sample.My First Day may be preferable to the Rainbow Passage in situations where a reading sample is deemed appropriate for evaluating voice quality.


Acoustic Analyses

All comparison tasks were strongly to very strongly correlated with the reference standard tasks.This suggests that the acoustic properties, as measured by CPPs, of the voices produced by children are equivalent across discourse-level tasks, or at a minimum, that diagnostic information is not lost by changing the speaking task.Acoustic assessment is an essential component of a voice evaluation, given that acoustic measures are objective and therefore unbiased, and are sensitive to minor changes in vocal quality.This preliminary analysis suggests that diagnostic acoustic information is not lost when the discourse-level stimulus varies between reading, informal conversation sampling, story generation and narrative re-tells.Further investigation is needed into diagnostic accuracy, however.


Limitations

This study could be considered an exploratory study due to several limitations.First, the study population was sampled via convenience sampling and may have yielded bias in factors relevant to voice and/or communication, such as socioeconomic status or participation in medical insurance; this may limit the ecological validity of the findings.The study protocol was designed to be investigated with a normative sample of children's voices, to determine whether the new running speech sampling methodologies prompted dysphonic productions in normophonic speakers.However, some vocal deviation was observed in the sample; this is perhaps not unexpected, given the high prevalence of dysphonia in childhood (Verduyckt et al. 2011).Thus, the data analysis was extended to include investigations of diagnostic metrics, rather than simple correlations alone.Further, the perceptual nature of the analysis in this study could be said to exhibit the same difficulties as perceptual analysis of voice in general: subjectivity and decreased reliability.However, due to extensive training procedures, inter-rater reliability statistics were acceptable for the purposes of this study.It is recommended that these findings be considered preliminary, and further investigations, with a larger sample size, multiple rates and a wide range of children with known vocal pathologies, should be considered.


Conclusion

Reference standard tasks do not consistently yield running speech samples of sufficient length, nor vocal quality, to be deployed confidently in paediatric voice evaluations.Participation in reading activities and responses to the voice question are inconsistent and proved troublesome for up to half of the children in this sample.

The narrative sampling tasks of story generation and re-telling provide lengthy units of running speech, the vocal qualities of which are correlated with those of conversational speech.Narrative tasks are more scaffolded and may decrease cognitive load and increase participation, as well as be completed in a shorter timeframe.This means that clinicians using narrative elicitation can be more confident that their voice sampling practices will consistently yield information relevant to voice evaluations, as well as a discourse sample for screening of communication skills.Further investigations are needed to confirm these findings, as well as extend the understanding of the vocal targets needed in running speech for a reliable and valid judgement of perceptual voice quality.
