Psychometric evaluation of a novel measure of trauma-related eating to cope
19 December 2024B0C0CDDD52753D2D74015E1630AC794A10.1016/j.xjmad.2024.100102Received 11 September 2024; Received in revised form 5 December 2024; Accepted 15 December 2024 Journal of Mood & Anxiety Disorders 9 (2025) 100102PTSD Disordered eating Coping Comorbidity Measure development
Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and disordered eating often co-occur, and this comorbidity is linked to a heightened clinical burden compared to either condition alone.One possible explanation for this co-occurrence is the self-medication model, which proposes that individuals may use food to cope with PTSD symptoms, a concept we refer to as trauma-related eating to cope.Although there is both theoretical and empirical support for this construct, no specific measure has been developed until now.In this study, we aimed to create and validate a novel measure, the Trauma-Related Eating to Cope (TREC) questionnaire, among a diverse sample of 726 trauma-exposed undergraduate students (M age =22.87,SD=7.20; 49.9 % White; 77 % women).Our data support the 20-item TREC questionnaire as a four-factor scale that can be used to evaluate eating to cope with symptoms specific to each of the four DSM-5 PTSD symptom clusters (χ 2 (164)= 330.23, p < .001;CFI= .959;TLI= .953;RMSEA= .038).The study findings indicate that the TREC questionnaire is a more precise and targeted tool for measuring eating to cope with PTSD symptoms compared to existing measures of eating to cope motives.Strong evidence was found for the construct and criterion validity of the TREC questionnaire, particularly in relation to PTSD symptoms and a range of disordered eating behaviors, including binge eating, caloric restriction, and emotional eating.These results underscore the potential of the TREC questionnaire for future research on selfmedication and its clinical utility as a screening tool for identifying individuals with PTSD who are at risk for developing pathological eating behaviors.

Trauma exposure is a risk factor for developing eating disorders (EDs) [1][2][3].This has been found across ED diagnoses.For instance, striking findings from the National Comorbidity Survey-Replication Study showed that trauma exposure was ubiquitous (100 %) across men and women with bulimia nervosa (BN) and anorexia nervosa (AN) diagnoses, and extensive among women (90 %) and men (98 %) with binge eating disorder (BED) [4].These findings are consistent with a large body of more recent work demonstrating the high proportion of trauma exposure across individuals diagnosed with AN [5], BN [6], and BED [7].One plausible explanation linking trauma exposure to EDs is the habitual use of maladaptive eating behaviors (e.g., binging, restriction) to cope with trauma-related distress.This is consistent with research showing that the majority (66 %) of individuals with an ED also meet the criteria for lifetime posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [4].The link between traumatic stress and EDs is further demonstrated by the markedly different pooled prevalence estimates of EDs among the general population (1.69 % [8]) compared to individuals with PTSD (25 % [9]).

PTSD-ED comorbidity is associated with greater psychiatric and medical burden compared to ED alone.For example, individuals with comorbid PTSD-ED tend to present with higher ED symptom severity, are more likely to drop out of ED treatment, and are more likely to experience symptom relapse compared to ED patients without PTSD [10][11][12].Additionally, individuals with PTSD-ED tend to have worse medical outcomes compared to those with ED alone, including an increased odds of a wide range of medical conditions (e.g., gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, hepatobiliary, endocrine, metabolic, respiratory, neurologic, and sleep disorders; infectious diseases; anemia; cancers) [13].The high prevalence and immense public health impact of comorbid PTSD-ED underscore the need for improved understanding surrounding the etiology of this comorbidity.

PTSD has also been linked to food addiction [14][15][16][17], a phenotype which is related to but distinct from ED and more closely mirrors substance use disorder.Food addiction research is rapidly gaining traction, as increasing evidence suggests that ultra-processed and highly palatable foods trigger similar neurobiological (e.g., dopaminergic sensitization) and behavioral (e.g., enhanced motivation) mechanisms as other addictive substances (e.g., illicit substances, alcohol) [18].Consumption of highly palatable ultra-processed foods greatly increases risk for metabolic syndrome [19][20][21], a cluster of conditions related to insulin resistance (e.g., obesity, hypertension, elevated blood sugar, excess abdominal weight) which, together, drastically increase the risk for chronic disease and death [22].The prevalence of metabolic syndrome among those with PTSD is striking (nearly 40 % [23]) and converging evidence points to metabolic syndrome as a potential mechanism linking PTSD to subsequent medical morbidity [24], as well as to accelerated biological aging [25] and early death [26].High rates of PTSD-ED comorbidity, coupled with evidence highlighting the addictive properties of ultra-processed foods and strong links between and PTSD numerous chronic diseases influenced by metabolic processes (e.g., cardiovascular disease, Type II diabetes, dementia; [27][28][29]), point to the need to explore extensions of the self-medication model to food-related behaviors.

The notion that eating to cope with trauma-related distress (e.g., PTSD symptoms) increases risk for PTSD-ED comorbidity is consistent with the self-medication framework for understanding PTSD-related comorbidities.The self-medication model suggests that PTSD contributes to the development of other disorders through a pattern of maladaptive strategies used to cope with (i.e., "self-medicate") PTSD symptoms that eventually develop into pathological forms of behavior [30].Most of the self-medication literature to date has focused on the use of alcohol or drugs to cope with PTSD symptoms [31][32][33].However, existing research about the use of food to manage negative emotions more broadly provides compelling preliminary evidence for the applicability of the self-medication model to PTSD-ED comorbidity.

There is a consistent body of work showing that both binge and restrictive eating behaviors are commonly used to manage negative mood states [34,35].For instance, research has shown that some people increase their food intake (e.g., emotional eating, binge eating [36][37][38][39]), whereas others intentionally decrease their food intake (i.e., dietary restriction [40][41][42]) to regulate negative affect.Moreover, individuals who exhibit disordered eating behaviors have been found to have higher levels of affect dysregulation compared to the general population [43] and there is evidence that both binging behaviors and restriction behaviors may be used as a means to soothe and regulate negative emotions [37,41,39,42].This research aligns with multiple models of disordered eating behavior, including the affect regulation model, which postulates that binge eating serves to reduce negative emotions by using food as a source of comfort and distraction [44]; the escape theory, which proposes that binge eating serves as a means to escape from emotional distress that stems from feelings of inadequacy [45]; and the masking hypothesis, which argues that people who diet tend to overeat as means to assign emotional distress as coming from overeating behaviors rather than less controllable factors [46].Consistent with the self-medication model, these models suggest that the negative reinforcement that accompanies the use of eating behaviors to temporarily relieve emotional distress increases the likelihood of repeated use of eating behaviors to manage negative affect which, over time, develops into pathological patterns of behavior (i.e., EDs).This likely explains why the use of eating behaviors to cope with negative mood has been found to increase the risk for EDs [47].Food-related coping motives have also been shown to mediate the relationship between negative affect and food addiction [48].

Similar to EDs, PTSD is also associated with difficulties in emotion regulation [49], and prior research has demonstrated that individuals with PTSD who experience dysregulated and negative affect often use disordered eating behaviors to cope [16,50].The recognition of food-oriented coping and its role as a risk factor for developing EDs, particularly among those with PTSD, highlights the importance of explicitly studying the use of eating-to-cope as a mechanism by which individuals with PTSD are at an increased risk for developing an ED.To date, research on this topic has been limited by the use of measures of eating to cope with negative affect broadly (e.g., anxiety, depression, overwhelming emotions) to infer the use of food to cope with PTSD symptoms [50,48,51]; however, eating to cope with PTSD symptoms specifically has yet to be explicitly studied.To our knowledge, there are currently no self-report survey tools available which assess the use of eating behaviors to cope with PTSD symptoms specifically.

We sought to fill this gap by developing and testing a novel measure of eating behaviors to cope with PTSD symptoms specifically, which we termed the Trauma-Related Eating to Cope (TREC) questionnaire.The TREC questionnaire consists of 20 items developed to obtain information regarding past-month frequency of eating to cope with each of the 20 PTSD symptoms, as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5 th Edition (DSM-5 [52]), with the specific wording related to each PTSD symptom modeled directly after the frequently used and validated PTSD Checklist-5 [53].The questionnaire was designed by psychologists specializing in PTSD and comorbid disorders with the intent to more effectively identify individuals at risk for developing comorbid PTSD-ED via the use of maladaptive eating behaviors to cope with PTSD symptoms.Improving understanding of PTSD-specific motives to engage in various maladaptive eating behaviors is a crucial first step in developing prevention and intervention strategies that may reduce the risk for developing an ED following trauma.The present study sought to psychometrically evaluate the newly developed TREC questionnaire via four aims.

First, we sought to investigate the factor structure of the TREC questionnaire items.It was hypothesized that the TREC questionnaire would evidence four subscales corresponding with the use of eating behaviors to cope with the four symptom clusters of PTSD (i.e., intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, arousal).It was further hypothesized that these four factors would load onto a single higher-order factor indexing overall trauma-related eating to cope.

Second, we sought to examine the distributional properties of the TREC questionnaire and compare them to an existing measure of eating to cope with negative affect more broadly, the coping subscale of the Palatable Eating Motives Scale (PEMS-Cope) [54].It was hypothesized that the TREC questionnaire would provide greater specificity than the general measure of eating to cope, such that it would be less frequently endorsed and less evenly distributed among the sample compared to the PEMS-Cope.We also sought to test omnibus differences in TREC questionnaire total scores by probable PTSD caseness.It was hypothesized that TREC total scores would be higher among probable PTSD cases versus controls.

Third, we sought to externally validate the TREC questionnaire first by examining the associations between the TREC questionnaire and the PEMS-Cope and comparing how the two constructs relate to PTSD using a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework.It was hypothesized that the TREC questionnaire and PEMS-Cope would be correlated yet distinct constructs, and that the PTSD factors would be more strongly associated with the TREC questionnaire higher order factor compared to the PEMS-Cope factor.Moreover, each TREC questionnaire factor was hypothesized to be most strongly associated with the PTSD symptom cluster (i.e., factor) that it was designed to represent.For example, we hypothesized that the arousal factor of the PTSD measure would be most strongly associated with the TREC questionnaire item querying frequency of eating to cope with arousal symptoms when compared to all the other PTSD factors.It was also hypothesized that each PTSD factor would significantly predict the TREC questionnaire higher-order factor.

Fourth, we aimed to externally validate the TREC questionnaire in relation to three domains of disordered eating (i.e., cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating).It was hypothesized that the TREC questionnaire higher-order factor would be positively associated with all three domains of disordered eating behaviors.


Method


Participants and procedures

Participants were 726 trauma-exposed undergraduates from a large urban public university.The Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5) [55] was used to determine eligibility for the study.Participants who endorsed exposure to any of the potentially traumatic events listed on the LEC were considered eligible for the study.Participants were recruited from a psychology participation pool and compensated with course credit.Data were collected via an online self-report survey that took approximately 45 minutes to complete.Of the 881 participants who enrolled in the study, 844 completed the survey in full (4.2 % began but did not complete the entire survey) and, of these, 726 (86 %) passed four out of the five attention checks dispersed throughout the survey (e.g., "How many days are there in a week?").Thus, the final analyzed sample was comprised of these 726 individuals, who identified primarily as women (77 %) and consisted of 49.9 % White, 36.6 % Black or African American, 5.4 % Asian, 0.7 % American Indian/Alaskan Native, 0.7 % Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, and 6.7 % Other identifying individuals.Participants ranged in age from 18 to 59 years old (M=22.87,SD=7.20).All study procedures were approved by the university's institutional review board.


Measures 1.2.1. Trauma-related eating to cope

The newly developed 20-item TREC questionnaire was created by psychologists with expertise in PTSD and associated disorders, with the goal of more effectively identifying individuals who may be at risk for developing comorbid PTSD-ED by using maladaptive eating behaviors as a way to manage PTSD symptoms.Response options ranged from 0 ("Never") to 4 ("Always").TREC questionnaire items were developed to obtain information regarding how often people used food (e.g., binge eating, restriction) to cope with each of the 20 PTSD symptoms, during the past month, as defined by the DSM-5 and modeled after the PCL-5.Detailed information about the scale instructions, items, and response options is presented in Table 1.Cronbach's alphas for the total score and subscales are reported in the Results under Aim 1.


General eating to cope motives

The coping subscale of the Palatable Eating Motives Scale (PEMS-Cope) [54] was used to assess eating to cope with general negative affect (e.g., "to cheer up when you're in a bad mood").Adapted from the Drinking Motives Questionnaire-Revised [56], participants were asked to rate the frequency of "eating tasty foods and drinks" (instead of "drinking alcohol") for each of the listed motives on a 1 ("Almost Never/Never") to 5 ("Almost Always") scale.The PEMS-Cope has demonstrated strong psychometric properties [54,57] and evidenced good internal consistency within the present sample (α=.89).


PTSD symptoms

Past month PTSD symptom severity was assessed using the PTSD Checklist-5 (PCL-5) [53].The PCL-5 consists of 20 items that align with the PTSD symptom criteria outlined in the DSM-5 [52].Responses are recorded on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 ("Not at all") to 4 ("Extremely").The PCL-5 has shown strong internal consistency (α =.94) and test-retest reliability (r = .82),as well as good convergent (rs = .74to.85) and discriminant (rs = .31to.60) validity [58].Cronbach's α from the present sample indicated high internal consistency (0.96).A PCL-5 cutoff score of 33 [59] was used to determine probable PTSD caseness.


Disordered eating behaviors

The Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire-18 [60] is an 18-item self-report survey designed measure three broad dimensions of disordered eating behavior: cognitive restraint (e.g., caloric restriction; "I deliberately take small helpings as a means of controlling my weight"), uncontrolled eating (e.g., binge eating; "Sometimes when I start eating, I just can't seem to stop"), and emotional eating (e.g., "When I feel lonely, I console myself by eating").Raw composite scale scores were transformed to a 0-100 common scale [((raw scorelowest possible raw score)/possible raw score range) x 100], with higher scores indicating greater disordered eating behavior across each category.There is existing evidence of good convergent and discriminant validity across the three scales [60] and there was evidence of good internal consistency in the present study (cognitive restraint α=.79; uncontrolled eating α=.88; emotional eating α=.87).


Trauma exposure

The Traumatic Life Events Questionnaire (TLEQ) [61] was administered as a detailed assessment of trauma history.The TLEQ is a 23-item self-report questionnaire which assesses frequency and timing of a range of potentially traumatic events (e.g., natural disaster, assault, accidents, illness/injury).The TLEQ has good test-retest reliability and good convergent validity with interview assessments of trauma exposure [61].A lifetime trauma load variable was created by summing the frequency endorsements for each trauma assessed in the TLEQ.


Data analytic plan 1.3.1. Aim 1

Confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were used to first evaluate the factor structure for the TREC.Structural equation modeling (SEM) analyses were performed using Mplus 8.10 software [62].Maximum likelihood robust (MLR) estimation was used to account for data non-normality.Given the TREC questionnaire was developed to assess eating behaviors to cope with DSM-5 PTSD symptoms, the measurement model specified a four-factor solution (Model 1), modeling the use of food behaviors to cope with the four DSM-5 symptom clusters of PTSD (i.e., intrusion, avoidance, alterations in cognition and mood, arousal).A higher-order factor model accounting for the shared variance among the four TREC latent factors was then estimated (Model 2).Model fit was evaluated based on three omnibus fit indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis Index (TLI) "close to".95 and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ .06[63].


Aim 2

Descriptive statistics (e.g., item endorsements, frequency distributions) were examined for the TREC (trauma-related eating to cope) and compared to those for the PEMS-Cope (eating to cope with negative mood more broadly) in the full sample and across probable PTSD cases and controls.Descriptive analyses were conducted in R 4.2.2 [64] and SPSS Version 28.Pearson and point-biserial correlational analyses were conducted to examine how TREC relates to relevant demographic variables (i.e., gender, race), trauma load, PEMS-Cope, PTSD symptoms, and disordered eating behaviors; t-tests compared the endorsement levels of relevant variables across probable PTSD cases and controls.Given the low endorsement rates (4 %) of individuals identifying in categories other than cisgender, a binary variable representing cisgender men and women was included in the point-biserial correlation analysis.


Aim 3

To examine the association between trauma-related eating to cope and eating to cope with negative affect more broadly, we employed a correlated higher-order factor model (Model 3), whereby the higherorder TREC factor, which accounted for the shared variance among the four TREC latent factors, was correlated with the PEMS-Cope common factor.Next, both the TREC higher-order factor and PEMS-Cope common factor were regressed onto the four oblique PCL-5 common factors in the same model, while allowing their residuals to correlate (Model 4).Given our interest in determining how the PTSD symptom clusters relate to the four TREC subscales, another model was specified which conditioned the four TREC latent factors onto each of the four oblique PCL-5 common factors (Model 5).To determine how the PTSD symptom clusters relate to overall trauma-related eating to cope, the four oblique PCL-5 common factors were then used to predict the higherorder TREC latent factor (Model 6).


Aim 4

We also sought to test how variation in trauma-related eating to cope may predict disordered eating behaviors.Therefore, we completed our conceptual self-medication model by regressing the TREC higher order factor onto the four oblique PCL-5 common factors, as before, and regressed the three TFEQ common factors onto the TREC higher order factor (Model 7).


Results


Aim 1

A four-factor model indexing eating to cope with symptoms aligning with the four PTSD symptom clusters fit the data well (χ 2 (164)= 330.23, p < .001;CFI= .959;TLI= .953;RMSEA= .038).All indicators loaded strongly onto their respective factors, with all standardized factor loadings > .8(see Table 1).A higher-order factor model with the four TREC latent factors loading onto a single higher-order TREC factor also fit the data well (χ 2 (166)= 357.88, p < .001;CFI= .953;TLI= .946;RMSEA= .040),with all first order factors loading onto the higher order factor at > .9. Reliability analyses at the manifest level further showed evidence of excellent internal consistency across all TREC items (α=.98), as well as across items within the four TREC subscales indexing eating to cope with the four symptom clusters of PTSD: intrusion (α=.95), avoidance (α=.90), negative alterations in cognition and mood (α=.95), and arousal (α=.93).


Aim 2

Table 2 provides descriptive information for the study variables.The average total composite sum scores for the TREC questionnaire, PEMS-Cope, the three TFEQ subscales, and lifetime trauma load were all significantly higher among individuals exceeding the suggested PCL-5 diagnostic cutoff for probable PTSD (n = 269; 38.21 %) compared to those who did not.All primary constructs were positively correlated (Table 3).Women reported higher levels of both general and traumarelated eating to cope compared to men, and black racial identity was negatively associated with each of the three disordered eating domains.TREC and PEMS-Cope total scores were moderately correlated (r = .57,p < .001)but were not multicollinear.

Whereas the majority (71.77%) of the sample reported at least some level of eating to cope with negative affect broadly (PEMS-Cope), only 44.48 % of the sample reported at least some level of trauma-related eating to cope, χ 2 (1, N = 726) = 73.34,p < .001.Histograms

comparing the distributions of the TREC and PEMS-Cope revealed that the PEMS-Cope was more dispersed than the TREC (Fig. 1), such that PEMS-Cope scores were normally distributed and TREC scores were skewed (2.07) and kurtotic (3.85).Additionally, the distributions of TREC and PEMS-Cope scores among probable PTSD cases were substantially more dispersed compared to probable non-PTSD controls (Fig. 2), as further demonstrated by evidence of extreme kurtosis of the TREC scores (11.61) among the non-PTSD controls compared to almost no kurtosis (-.005) among the probable PTSD cases.A total of 65.06 % of individuals exceeding the PCL-5 cutoff for probable PTSD endorsed at least some trauma-related eating to cope.


Aim 3

The correlated higher-order factor model used to test the association between the TREC and PEMS-Cope latent factors demonstrated good fit (χ 2 (270)= 583.70, p < .001;CFI= 0.948; TLI= 0.942; RMSEA= .040),and indicated that the latent factors were correlated as expected (ρ=0.62,p < .001).Next, to evaluate whether measures of traumarelated eating to cope versus eating to cope with negative affect more broadly are differentially associated with PTSD symptoms, the four oblique PCL-5 common factors were treated as external predictors of both the TREC higher-order factor and PEMS-Cope common factor, while allowing their residuals to correlate.The model demonstrated good fit (χ 2 (926)= 2210.93,p < .001;CFI= 0.926; TLI= 0.921; RMSEA= 0.044).Whereas none of the PCL-5 factors significantly predicted the PEMS-Cope common factor, all but one (negative alterations in cognition/mood) of the PTSD latent factors significantly predicted the TREC higher-order factor (see Fig. 3 for the full validation model).

The model exploring associations between the TREC and PCL-5 common factors fit the data well (χ 2 (712)= 1719.63,p < .001;CFI= 0.932; TLI= 0.926; RMSEA= .044).A summary of the path coefficients between the PCL-5 and TREC latent variables is provided in Table 4. Contrary to the hypothesis that each PCL-5 factor would predict its analogous TREC factor (e.g., PTSD intrusion symptoms would predict eating to cope with intrusion symptoms), only the intrusion and arousal PCL-5 factors significantly predicted the TREC intrusion and arousal factors, respectively.The arousal factor was the only PCL-5 factor that was significantly associated with all four TREC factors.Follow-up bivariate analyses of manifest variables demonstrated that all TREC subscales were positively associated with their analogous PCL-5 subscale, as well as all other PCL-5 subscales (Table 5).

Next, the TREC higher order factor was regressed onto the four oblique PCL-5 common factors (Model 6).This model fit the data well (χ 2 (726)= 1814.78,p < .001;CFI= 0.927; TLI= 0.922; RMSEA= .045).All of the PCL-5 factors significantly predicted TREC higher-order factor, with the exception of the factor representing negative alterations in cognition and mood (Table 4; Fig. 3).There was a small negative association between the PCL-5 avoidance factor and the TREC higherorder factor, suggesting lower levels of trauma-related eating to cope in the presence of PTSD avoidance.This effect was no longer significant, however, after covarying for the effect of gender on TREC (β=-.155,p = .056).Furthermore, follow-up bivariate correlations at the manifest level indicated that all PCL-5 factors were positively associated with the TREC total score (Table 5).


Aim 4

The final validation model extended Model 6 to test whether traumarelated eating to cope significantly predicted a range of disordered eating behaviors, while accounting for the effect of PTSD on traumarelated eating to cope (Model 7).Overall, this model demonstrated acceptable fit (χ 2 (1575)= 3470.58,p < .001;CFI= 0.920; TLI= 0.917; RMSEA= .041).Standardized path coefficients are provided in Fig. 4. Consistent with results from Aim 3, all PCL-5 factors except for the negative alterations in cognition and mood factor significantly predicted the TREC higher order factor.Moreover, trauma-related eating to cope was significantly and positively associated with the three TFEQ latent factors representing cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating.Given the TREC total score was associated with gender and all three disordered eating outcomes were associated with race at the bivariate level, a follow-up sensitivity model was conducted which conditioned the TREC higher order factor on gender and the disordered eating factors on race.Overall, the pattern of results remained consistent, except for that the small negative association between the PCL-5 avoidance factor and the TREC higher order factor was no longer    significant (β=-.154,p = .057).


Discussion

Preliminary evidence suggests that disordered eating behaviors are commonly used to manage negative mood states [34,35] and that food-related coping increases risk for EDs [47].This information points to the self-medication model as a potentially useful framework for understanding how and why individuals with PTSD are at increased risk for developing an ED (e.g., AN, BN, BED), which has important treatment and prevention implications.The existing research is limited by its focus on food behaviors to cope with negative affect broadly (e.g., depression, anxiety), rather than with PTSD symptoms specifically, making this a critical void to fill.

The present study sought to address this gap by developing and validating a self-report measure assessing the use of food to cope with symptoms of PTSD.We termed this new measure the Trauma-Related Eating to Cope (TREC) questionnaire.As expected, our findings suggest that the TREC questionnaire is a more precise measure of eating to cope motives compared to existing measures of eating to cope with negative affect more broadly (i.e., the PEMS-Cope).Additionally, findings demonstrate support for the external validation of the TREC questionnaire, both with regard to PTSD and a range of disordered eating behaviors, including uncontrolled eating, restricting, and emotional eating.Findings relevant to our four aims are discussed in turn.

We first evaluated the factor structure of the TREC questionnaire.Our CFA results supported a four-factor solution for the TREC questionnaire.This is consistent with how the TREC questionnaire was designed, which was to assess the use of eating behaviors to cope with the four PTSD symptom clusters defined in the DSM-5 and modeled after the PCL-5: intrusions (e.g., distressing memories of the traumatic event), avoidance (e.g., avoiding memories, thoughts, or feelings about the event), negative alterations in cognition and mood (e.g., persistent negative emotional state), and arousal (e.g., hypervigilance).Thus, the shared structure of the TREC and PCL-5 reflects the goal of capturing the unique ways in which individuals use eating to cope with trauma-related symptoms.The TREC's design inherently ties its scores to the presence and intensity of PTSD symptoms.By prefacing each item with "how often did you use food to cope with:", the TREC explicitly captures coping behaviors rather than symptom severity alone.This allows the TREC to differentiate individuals based on the behavioral manifestation of trauma-related eating to cope, even among those with similar PTSD symptom levels.Notably, the high correlations we observed among the four factors suggest that it is appropriate to use the total score on the TREC questionnaire as well as the subscale scores in future research.

Our second aim was to investigate the distributional properties of the TREC questionnaire and how these properties compare to those of an existing, general measure of eating to cope with negative affect, the PEMS-Cope.In our trauma-exposed sample, the endorsement rates for any item on the TREC questionnaire were considerably lower than those for any item on the PEMS-Cope.This is especially noteworthy considering that the TREC questionnaire contains four times as many items as the PEMS-Cope, providing supportive evidence that the TREC questionnaire may be a more specific measure of eating to cope with PTSD symptoms, and not more general eating to cope with negative affect.All our participants had histories of trauma exposure.Consistent with the greater PTSD literature, which has found that the majority of traumaexposed individuals do not subsequently develop PTSD [65], our results suggest that most trauma-exposed individuals would not develop problematic eating habits specifically related to their having experienced trauma.Rather, those who develop PTSD symptoms may be at unique risk of either developing new or maintaining existing maladaptive eating patterns, as a means to cope with PTSD symptoms.The 0 inflation that was observed for the TREC reflects its validity.While it is true that individuals without trauma exposure or PTSD symptoms would score 0 on the TREC, this design aligns with its purpose as a symptom-specific coping measure.The TREC is not intended as a general measure of eating to cope, but as a targeted tool to understand how individuals engage in eating as a coping mechanism specifically in the context of trauma-related symptoms.

Further, this difference between high rates of endorsement of general eating to cope motives and lower endorsement of eating to cope with PTSD symptoms specifically confutes the common misconception tainting the self-medication literature to date that eating to cope with negative affect is synonymous with eating to cope with trauma-related symptoms specifically.Moreover, when comparing the frequency distributions of TREC questionnaire and PEMS-Cope endorsement among probable PTSD cases versus controls, the distributions of TREC questionnaire and PEMS-Cope total scores were substantially more dispersed among probable cases compared to controls, which demonstrated high positive skewness and kurtosis.Higher endorsement in probable PTSD cases versus controls is expected, given that the TREC questionnaire was designed to specifically assess eating to cope with symptoms related to PTSD and therefore should not be highly endorsed by individuals not experiencing high levels of these symptoms.

Our third aim was to investigate the association between traumarelated eating to cope and eating to cope with negative affect more broadly and to examine whether these two constructs are differentially associated with PTSD symptoms.As expected, TREC questionnaire and PEMS-Cope scores were moderately correlated at the manifest and latent  levels.These results provide additional evidence that eating to cope with PTSD symptoms and eating to cope with general negative affect are related, but distinct, constructs.When comparing the predictive effects of the four PCL-5 common factors on the TREC questionnaire and PEMS-Cope common factors in the same model, results showed that all but one of the four PCL-5 factors (negative alterations in cognition and mood) were associated with the TREC questionnaire, but that none of the PCL-5 factors were associated with the PEMS-Cope factor.These findings provide further support for the use of the TREC questionnaire as a more specific measure of eating to cope in the context of PTSD.

We then sought to assess the external validity of the TREC questionnaire with respect to PTSD symptoms.Bivariate associations between the TREC total score, its subscales, and the PTSD symptom clusters at the manifest (measured) level demonstrated that the TREC total score was positively associated with all PTSD symptom clusters and that each TREC subscale was positively correlated with its analogous PCL-5 subscale, indicating robust relationships between the TREC and the dimensions of PTSD symptoms it was designed to measure.The SEM modeling these associations at the latent level showed that two of the four TREC factors (those indexing eating to cope with intrusion and arousal symptoms) were significantly associated with their analogous PTSD symptom factors.The two subscales indexing eating to cope with avoidance symptoms and negative alterations in cognition and mood were not significantly associated with their analogous PTSD symptom clusters, though they were significantly associated with intrusion and avoidance, respectively, and they both were associated with arousal symptoms.Moreover, all PCL-5 factors except for negative alterations in cognition and mood significantly predicted the TREC questionnaire higher-order factor.Interestingly, when analyzed using an SEM framework, the negative alterations in cognition and mood (NACM) factor was the only PCL-5 factor that did not significantly predict trauma-related eating to cope at the first order (TREC subfactors) or second order (higher-order TREC) levels.This finding was somewhat surprising given that eating to cope has been strongly associated with symptoms included in this cluster (e.g., negative affect, shame, and blame).However, the absence of significant associations for NACM in the SEM model likely reflects the influence of covariance across PTSD symptom clusters, which is accounted for in the latent framework.Specifically, NACM shares substantial variance with other PTSD symptom clusters (e.g., intrusion, avoidance, hyperarousal), as evidenced by high intercorrelations among the PCL-5 factors (.79 − .92).In the SEM model, this shared variance is partitioned, allowing the model to estimate the unique contributions of each PTSD symptom cluster to trauma-related eating to cope.As a result, NACM's association with the TREC is likely attenuated, as other symptom clusters that are more directly traumaspecific (e.g., intrusion, avoidance, hyperarousal) explain a greater proportion of the variance in trauma-related eating behaviors.This finding may lend credence to the trauma-specificity of this novel questionnaire.For instance, whereas the PTSD symptom clusters that tend to be more unique to PTSD (i.e., intrusion, avoidance of trauma memories and cues, arousal) were associated with the TREC questionnaire, the NACM symptom cluster is largely comprised of symptoms that overlap with a number of other internalizing disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety) and is, therefore, less "PTSD-specific".Thus, this finding further suggests that the TREC questionnaire measures a construct that is distinct from eating to cope with negative affect more broadly, as described above.

In contextualizing our results, it is important to consider previous investigations of the PCL-5.Although the PCL-5 measurement model based on the DSM-5 symptom clusters fit our data well, other models have been supported by previous studies.In particular, one study of 412 trauma-exposed college students found that a seven-factor "Hybrid model" that separated the NACM factor into two factors fit better compared to other proposed factor structures, including the DSM-5 model [66].The two mood factors in the Hybrid model included negative affect (e.g., having strong negative beliefs and having strong negative feelings) and anhedonia (e.g., loss of interest and trouble experiencing positive feelings).These two facets of the negative alterations factor may be differentially related to trauma-related eating to cope, which could account for our unexpected findings and is an important area for future research.

Our fourth aim was to assess the external validity of the TREC questionnaire with respect to disordered eating behaviors.As  hypothesized, TREC questionnaire scores predicted a range of disordered eating behaviors, including uncontrolled eating, food restriction, and emotional eating in the final validation model, which included the predictive effects of PTSD on trauma-related eating to cope.The TREC questionnaire higher order factor was most strongly associated with uncontrolled eating in this model.In addition, the TREC questionnaire higher order factor was moderately associated with emotional eating, providing additional evidence that these are related but distinct constructs.Although trauma exposure and PTSD have been most strongly associated with eating disorders characterized by binging and purging [4], it has been suggested that restriction could serve as a means by which to regain a sense of control following trauma exposure [67].This is consistent with the present findings, which showed a positive, albeit smaller, association between TREC scores and food restriction.Taken together, our findings from the final validation model support the self-medication model of PTSD.Specifically, results support the theoretical model that the reinforcing effects of binging, restricting, or eating highly palatable food for the specific purposes of alleviating PTSD symptoms could lead to the development of more persistent patterns of pathological eating behaviors over time.

Findings should be interpreted in light of several important limitations.TREC items were not pilot tested before administration and analysis of the TREC questionnaire, an important step in measurement development to assess content validity and internal reliability of items to be included in a measure [68].However, the TREC items were conceptually constructed by leading experts in PTSD-related comorbidity, and were modeled after a well-known, validated, and commonly utilized measure (i.e., PCL-5), and this measure demonstrated excellent internal reliability within the present sample.Although the TREC and PCL-5 factor structures based on the DSM-5 fit the data well in our study, several alternate PCL-5 factor structures have been proposed, and it is worth exploring whether using a different factor structure could further improve the TREC.Data were cross-sectional and cannot be used to infer causation.Future studies using a within-subjects repeated design would help to strengthen external validity of the TREC questionnaire and offer a promising direction for further elucidating associations between PTSD symptoms, TREC, and eating-related outcomes.We did not assess eating disorder symptoms/criteria; investigating associations between TREC scores and specific eating disorder symptoms is an important area for future study, as is exploring associations between TREC scores and other food-related outcomes, including food addiction and food-related chronic diseases (e.g., metabolic syndrome, type II diabetes).

The lack of association between the TREC higher order factor and the PCL-5 NACM factor in our SEMs arguably limits the criterion validity of TREC questionnaire.Criterion validity is defined as the extent to which an individual's score on a measure is associated with other variables one would expect them to be associated with [69].Therefore, given the intended purpose of the TREC questionnaire is to assess an individual's eating motives with regard to alleviating (all) symptoms of PTSD, the lack of association between the TREC higher order factor and the PTSD symptom cluster defined by the largest number of symptoms is potentially problematic.However, TREC was associated with the PTSD NACM cluster at the bivariate level.The discrepancy between significant bivariate associations and the SEM results is a common phenomenon in multivariate modeling.At the bivariate level, correlations capture the total association between two variables, encompassing both unique and shared variance.In contrast, SEM disentangles this shared variance, focusing on the unique relationship of each predictor after accounting for its overlap with other predictors in the model.Thus, while NACM demonstrates a significant bivariate relationship with the TREC, its unique contribution may be masked in the SEM model due to the strong, shared associations between PTSD symptom clusters.

Additionally, given the present study sample was comprised of primarily woman-identifying college students seeking course credit for participation, the generalizability of the present findings to other groups is currently unknown.Additional important directions for future research include replication of the study findings in diverse samples as well as tests of measurement invariance of the TREC questionnaire.Specifically, descriptive statistics showed that TREC levels were higher among women compared to men, but that there did not appear to be race-related differences in level of TREC.Tests of measurement invariance are an important next step in determining whether the TREC factor structure and factor loadings differ across various demographic groups.

Our results also have several important clinical implications.Trauma-related eating to cope might be one mechanism that accounts for PTSD-ED comorbidity, as well as PTSD-physical health comorbidities such as metabolic disorders and related conditions.As trauma-related eating to cope appears distinct from eating to cope with general negative affect, this is an important treatment target for trauma-exposed individuals with eating disorders or clinically significant disordered eating.Given the high rates of trauma exposure as well as comorbid PTSD among individuals with eating disorders, integrated treatments may be most effective [70].The bidirectional associations between PTSD symptoms and disordered eating illustrated by the self-medication model further underscore the need for such treatments that would target trauma-related eating to cope in order to best treat individuals with PTSD-ED.The TREC questionnaire could be a valuable measure in future self-medication research and serve as a clinically useful screening tool for both PTSD and disordered eating.
