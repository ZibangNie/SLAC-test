Photocleavable Guide RNA for Photocontrolled CRISPR/Cas9 System

Objective: The development of CRISPR/Cas-based gene-editing systems having a higher efficacy and specificity, and capable of changing activity in response to light irradiation is an urgent problem.A promising approach to this problem is to modify CRISPR/Cas components, in particular guide RNA, by introducing photocleavable linkers.We developed an approach to the synthesis of photocleavable single guide RNA (sgRNA) for the CRISPR/Cas9 system containing linkers on the basis of 1-(2-nitrophenyl)-1,2-ethanediol.Such photomodified guide RNAs are cleaved under UV irradiation, thereby inactivating the CRISPR/Cas9 system.Methods: Automatic solid-phase phosphoramidate method was used for photomodified sgRNA synthesis.Model plasmid was used for designed system testing.Results and Discussion: We obtained three variants of photomodified sgRNA with different photolinker positions.Evidence was obtained showing that the sgRNA with the photolinker introduced in the protein Cas9 site of binding and hairpin formation is able to effectively guide Cas9 nuclease for target DNA cleavage before UV irradiation and lose its activity after irradiation.The conditions of controllable 40% cleavage of a model target DNA were chosen.Conclusions: The work presents the results of photocleavable sgRNA design and usage as a component of photoregulated CRISPR/Cas9 system.The developed approach makes possible specific inactivation of the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system in a specific time moment in a definite place.The photoregulation of the gene-editing system not only allows one to reduce undesirable off-target effects, but also forms the basis for genetic disease therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) represent a succession of short palindromic DNA sequences separated by unique spacer sequences, specific foreign DNA fragments (bacteriophages) responsible for antiviral resistance in bacteria [1,2].Adjacent to these repeats are proteincoding CRISPR-associated genes (Cas).At present CRISPR/Cas9 is a widely used genome-editing system.The CRISPR/Cas9 system consists of the Cas9 protein of Streptococcus pyogenes and two RNAs: crRNA, which recognizes foreign DNA by the complementarity principle, and tracrRNA, which binds to crRNA and the Cas9 protein.Instead of the pair of crRNA and tracrRNA, a specially designed single guide RNA (sgRNA) molecule can be used, containing a fragment that ensures DNA recognition and a fragment that ensures binding to the Cas9 protein [3][4][5].During the recognition process, the RNA-Cas9 complex finds PAM (Protospacer Adjacent Motif) sequences in the double-stranded target DNA.Then the DNA near the PAM partially unwinds, and a duplex is formed between crRNA and target DNA, after which the nuclease domains of the Cas9 protein come into action, cutting both DNA strands.PHOTOCLEAVABLE GUIDE RNA FOR PHOTOCONTROLLED CRISPR/Cas9 SYSTEM Numerous examples of the use of synthetic guide RNAs as part of the gene-editing CRISPR/Cas9 system are known [6].For controlled activation of the genomeediting system, photoblocked RNAs were proposed [7][8][9][10].

An important practical task is to create CRISPR/Casbased genome-editing systems, which would exhibit enhanced efficiency and specificity of action, along with the additional ability to change activity of guide RNA in response to light irradiation.A promising way to solve this problem is to modify the components of CRISPR/Cas systems, in particular guide RNAs [9,10].

The photocontrolled systems described in the literature are activated by irradiation, but they cannot be inactivated if necessary at a specific point in time.At the same time, the task to stop the CRISPR/Cas9 system inside cells is very relevant for reducing off-target editing, as well as for developing gene therapy for diseases caused by an increase in the number of copies of certain genes, when it is necessary to edit only part of the genomic loci.To solve this problem, a few approaches have been proposed, based on the use of Cas9 proteins able to undergo controlled dimerization [11,12] or on the inducible destruction of the Cas9 coding sequence itself [13,14].

In this work, we proposed the use of photocleavable guide RNAs, which would allow the CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system to be inactivated under near-UV irradiation.The proposed approach expands the range of guide RNAs capable of inactivating the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system and makes it possible to specifically and spatiotemporally inactivation the CRISPR/ Cas9 system in living cells.


RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The undeniable advantage of photolabile compounds is that they allow noninvasive regulation of biological processes, as well as the high spatiotemporal resolution of experiments with such compounds.Evidence for their benefits was obtained in numerous studies in which various photolabile groups were included in oligonucleotide-based constructs.

To obtain photocleavable guide sgRNAs, we developed an approach based on the introduction of photocleavable 1-(2-nitrophenyl)-1,2-ethanediol-derived linkers (PL) into the RNA at certain positions of the oligonucleotide chain.To this end, we synthesized phosphoramidate of 1-(2-nitrophenyl)-1,2-ethanediol, as described in [15].A 102-mer sgRNA and three of its photomodified analogs (sgRNA-PL1, sgRNA-PL2, and sgRNA-PL3), containing a photolinker in the region of the protospacer, binding loops, and hairpins in the sgRNA structure, were synthesized by the solid-phase phosphoramidate method.The protocols for the synthesis of oligoribonucleotides were optimized taking into account their length.To increase the yields of target compounds, in the synthesis of full-length sgRNAs on a domestic ASM-800 DNA/ RNA synthesizer, a modified tetrazole, specifically 5-[3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl]-1H-tetrazole (Activator 42 TM ), was used at the condensation stage as an activator, and propionic anhydride was used for capping.The condensation time and the volumes of phosphoramidates and condensing reagent solutions were increased with elongation of oligonucleotide chain length.To introduce the photolinker, a more concentrated solution of phosphoramidate compared to standard ribonucleotides was used, and the condensation time was prolonged to 30 min.The deblocking time during postsynthetic procedures was increased by 25% compared to the standard protocol.The time of treatment with a 40% methylamine solution was prolonged to 20 min, and the removal of 2′-tert-butyldimethylsilyl groups was carried out for 2 h instead of 1.5 h on standard deblocking.The isolation of sgRNA was performed by preparative electrophoresis in 12% denaturing PAGE.All these changes resulted in the successful preparation of extended 102-mer oligoribonucleotides and isolation of target sgRNAs in amounts sufficient for further experiments.

Using the above-described procedure, three variants of photomodified sgRNAs (sgRNA-PL1, sgRNA-PL2, and sgRNA-PL3) were obtained (Table 1).Additionally, for comparison, oligoribonucleotides sgRNA-PL2 (5ʹfr_sgRNA-PL2 and 3ʹfr_sgRNA-PL2) and sgRNA-PL3 (5ʹfr_sgRNA-PL3) were synthesized, corresponding to the RNA fragments that should be formed upon the irradiation of sgRNA-PL2 and sgRNA-PL3, respectively.An unmodified sgRNA, as well as a pair of guide RNAs (crRNA and tracrRNA) were synthesized as controls.The sequences of the synthesized oligoribonucleotides are presented in Table 1.

At the first stage, a comparative study of the ability of photomodified and control, unmodified guide RNAs, to induce the cleavage of the target plasmid DNA pBluescriptII SK(−) by the Cas9 protein, was carried out.The pBS2SKM plasmid contained protospacer and PAM (5′-TGG-3′) sequences.During the experiment, the photolabile guide RNAs sgRNA-PL1, sgRNA-PL2, and sgRNA-PL3 were preirradiated with UV light (365 nm) for 30 min.The irradiation time was selected so that to ensure a quantitative cleavage of PL in RNA.Then they were used for directed DNA cleavage with Cas9 nuclease; for comparison, the cleavage of target DNA in the presence of nonirradiated photolabile guide RNAs was also studied (Fig. 1).

Based on the results of electrophoretic analysis, it can be concluded that the introduction of a photolinker at position 1 (sgRNA-PL1) significantly reduces the efficiency of dsDNA cleavage compared to the control unmodified sgRNA, which is likely due to a disruption of the heteroduplex structure between sgRNA and target DNA (Fig. 2) .When a photolinker is introduced at position 2 (sgRNA-PL2), the cleavage efficiency remains quite high, and when localized at position 3 (sgRNA-PL3), it is practically no different from the control.Irradiation significantly reduces the cleavage efficiency for sgRNA-PL1 and sgRNA-PL2, but practically does not change the cleavage pattern for sgRNA-PL3.This fact may indicate that the corresponding region of the 3′-terminal region of sgRNA does not play a significant role in the cleavage of target DNA under these conditions.Thus, among the tested sites of location of the photolinker in the guide RNA, the most advantageous was found to be position 2 which ensures effective cleavage before irradiation and a significant decrease in the degree Table 1.Photolabile sgRNA guides and their unmodified analogs and fragments a sgRNA Sequence

To test the hypothesis of the reduced ability of sgRNA-PL2 to direct the Cas9 nuclease to target DNA after irradiation, we explored the cleavage of target DNA in the presence of the oligoribonucleotides 5ʹfr_sgRNA-PL2 and 3ʹfr_sgRNA-PL2 (43 and 59 nt, respectively), which correspond to the fragments of sgRNA-PL2, theoretically formed as a result of photolinker cleavage.In addition, to test the assumption that the 3′-terminal region of sgRNA-PL3 is not very important the functioning of the CRISPR/Cas9 system in vitro, we investigated the ability of the 5′fr_sgRNA-PL3 oligonucleotide, corresponding to the 5′-fragment of sgRNA-PL3, theoretically formed upon irradiation, to induce Cas9-mediated DNA cleavage .

The electrophoretic analysis of the plasmid cleavage products in an agarose gel confirmed the initial assumptions.It was shown that in the presence of chemically synthesized fragments of the photocleavable sgRNA-PL2 guide RNA, the efficiency of DNA cleavage by Cas9 nuclease is much lower than is the case with unmodified sgRNA (Figs.3a and 3c).Moreover, the efficiency of DNA cleavage in the presence of the 5′-terminal fragment of sgRNA-PL3 by the Cas9 protein is not inferior to the efficiency of cleavage in the presence of unmodified sgRNA under our experimental conditions (Figs. 3b and 3c).

The resulting data show that the selected site for introducing the photolinker into sgRNA-PL2 in the loop between the upper and lower stem in the region of binding to the Cas9 protein is critical for the efficient operation of the CRISPR/Cas9 system and allows the system to be effectively switched off by irradiation.It should also be noted that the guide RNA shortened to 69 nt is sufficiently effective for the cleavage of target DNA by the CRISPR/Cas9 system.Our experimental data on the activity of CRISPR/Cas9 systems containing truncated sgRNAs are consistent with the previously reported findings of other research groups concerning the minimum length of the chimeric guide RNA for the Cas9 nuclease [16,17].Thus, Nishimasu et al. [16] showed that the 68-mer sgRNA, as part of a complex with the Cas9 nuclease, was able to direct the cleavage of target DNA.

At the next stage, we compared the kinetics of target DNA cleavage by the Cas9 protein in the presence of different variants of guide RNAs.The excess of the effector complex relative to the target plasmid DNA was reduced from 50-to 15-fold compared to the previous cleavage efficiency study.The kinetic curves of plasmid cleavage by the effector complex with unmodified guide RNAs, including the shortened 69-nucleotide variant, are presented in Fig. 4.

After 40-min reaction, the truncated 69-mer sgRNA induced 90% target cleavage by Cas9 nuclease, as did the crRNA-tracrRNA guide pair.However, the cleavage rate constant for the 69-mer fragment turned out to be much lower (k sgRNA(69) = 0.09 ± 0.01 min -1 , k crRNA:tracrRNA = 1.47 ± 0.02 min -1 , and k sgRNA (102) = 0.57 ± 0.01 min -1 ) .This may be due to the fact that, even though the fraction of the productive Cas9/RNA complex with the truncated sgRNA is comparable to those with the full-length guide RNAs, the cleavage rate in the presence of the former is strongly reduced because of the absence of additional  protein interactions with the 3′-terminal hairpins, like is in the case with tracrRNA or sgRNA .

In view of the above data on the cleavage of plasmid DNA by the Cas9 protein in the presence of photosensitive guide RNAs, sgRNA-PL2 was chosen to study the kinetics of target DNA cleavage.The kinetic curves of plasmid DNA cleavage are presented in Fig. 5.

The plasmid is cleaved most quickly and efficiently by Cas9 nuclease in the presence of unmodified sgRNA.The action of the system using sgRNA_PL2 was stopped so that the final fraction of the cleaved plasmid was no more than 40% (Fig. 5, curves 4 and 5).In this case, we started to irradiate the reaction mixture after 20-min reaction in view of the slower progression of the plasmid cleavage reaction.Note that increasing the irradiation time from 5 to 30 min did not further reduce the rate and efficiency of splitting: the patterns the corresponding curves 4 and 5 in Fig. 5 were almost identical.

Synthesis of photocleavable oligoribonucleotides.

Oligonucleotides were obtained on an ASM-800 automatic DNA/RNA synthesizer (Biosset, Russia) by the solid-phase phosphoramidate synthesis on a 0.4 μmol scale, following a protocol optimized for this instrument.Ribonucleotide phosphoramidates were used at a concentration of 0.1 M in absolute acetonitrile; the condensation time was 5 min.The phosphoramidate of photocleavable linker (PL) was used at a concentration of 0.15 M in absolute acetonitrile, the condensation time was 30 min.

A 0.25 M solution of 5-[3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl]-1H-tetrazole (Activator 42 TM ) in absolute acetonitrile was used as a condensing reagent.With increasing oligonucleotide chain length, the condensation time and the volumes of solutions of phosphoramidates and the condensing reagent were increased by 15% after 50, 70, and 90 synthetic cycles.Mixtures of propionic anhydride, 2,6-lutidine, and THF (10 : 10 : 80 v/v/v) and N-methylimidazole and THF (16 : 84 v/v) were used as capping reagents.Oxidation was carried out with 0.02 M iodine in a mixture of pyridine, water, and THF (1 : 9 : 90 v/v/v).Dichloroacetic acid (3%) in methylene chloride was used as a detritylation reagent.

To deblock heterocyclic bases, remove cyanoethyl protecting groups from internucleotide phosphate groups, and separate oligoribonucleotides from the solid-phase substrate, treatment with a 40% methylamine solution for 20 min at 65°C and constant stirring was performed, followed by cooling for 10 min at -20°C.The polymer carrier was washed with a mixture of acetonitrile, ethanol, and water (1 : 1 : 1, v/v/v).The solutions were combined and evaporated to dryness.

To remove the 2′-OTBDMS protective groups from the oligoribonucleotide, 250 μL of a freshly prepared solution of NMP, Et 3 N, and Et 3 N•3HF (1.5 : 0.75 : 1, v/v/v) was added to the dry residue immediately after evaporation, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h at 65°C.Ethoxytrimethylsilane, 375 μL, was then added and, after stirring on a thermomixer for 10 min at 25°C and adding 1 mL of diethyl ether, the resulting mixture was stirred and centrifuged.The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was washed with ether and dried in air.

The target oligomers were isolated by preparative gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions (12% PAGE, acrylamide : N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide 30 : 0.5, 7 M urea, 50 µM Tris-H 3 BO 3 , pH 8.3, 0.1 M Na 2 EDTA).Visualization of oligonucleotides in the gel was carried out by applying the gel to a DC-Alufolien Kieselgel 60 F254 plate (Merck, Germany) under UV light (λ 254 nm).In the case of photosensitive oligonucleotides, to avoid activation of the cleavage reaction by UV irradiation, the main part of the gel containing the product was covered with glass, leaving only a small part of it, sufficient for visualization and approximation.Crushed gel containing the oligonucleotide material was placed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of 0.3 M sodium acetate solution (pH 5.2) and stirred on a Thermomixer Comfort instrument (Eppendorf, Germany) for 16 h at 25°C.After elution, oligoribonucleotides were precipitated from a 0.3 M sodium acetate solution with a 4-fold volume of ethanol and kept at -20°C for 2-12 h.The supernatant after centrifugation was discarded, and the precipitate was washed with cooled 80% ethanol and dried in air.

The homogeneity of the obtained oligonucleotides was confirmed by analytical gel electrophoresis in 12% PAGE (for conditions, see above).The test compounds were applied on the gel in 5 μL of a 7 M urea solution containing 0.025% xylene cyanol FF and visualized with a solution of 50 mg of Stains-all dye in 100 mL of aqueous formamide (1 : 1, v/v).After staining, the gel was dried a Model 583 Gel Dryer (Bio-Rad, USA).

Cleavage of plasmid DNA by the Cas9 protein in the presence of guide RNAs.The reaction was carried out in 10 μL of a cleavage buffer containing 20 µM of HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 µM of KCl, 1 µM of dithiothreitol, 0.5 µM of Na 2 EDTA, 2 µM of MgCl 2 , and 5% of glycerol.Solutions of 1.35 μL (1 μM, 1.35 pmol) of a guide RNA and of 0.6 μL (2.24 μM, 1.35 pmol) of the Cas9 protein (a 50-fold excess relative to the pBS2SKM plasmid), in 2 μL of the buffer (5×) were prepared.The volumes were adjusted to 9 μL with water.The solutions were mixed, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37°C to assemble the ribonucleoprotein complex.Solutions containing guide sgRNAs deactivated by UV irradiation for 30 min (λ = 365 nm) were prepared in a similar way.

Then, 1 μL of a solution (50 ng/μL, 27 fmol) of the pBS2SKM plasmid containing the protospacer and PAM sequence was added to each tube.The reaction mixtures were mixed and incubated for 1 h at 37°C in the absence of direct sunlight.

To stop the reaction, 2.5 μL of a solution containing 250 µM of Na 2 EDTA, 1.2% of SDS, 0.01% of bromophenol blue, and 30% of glycerol (quenching buffer) was added to the reaction mixture.

The efficiency of plasmid cleavage by the Cas9 protein in the presence of guide RNAs was analyzed by gel electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel in TAE buffer (4 µM Tris, 3 µM CH 3 COOH, 0.07 µM Na 2 EDTA) with the addition of 2.5 μL of ethidium bromide (concentration 10 mg/mL).The reaction mixture in the quenching buffer, 10 μL, was applied on the gel.The mobility of cleavage products in the gel was controlled using a 1 kb DNA marker; the fragment lengths were 250-10000 bp (SibEnzyme, Russia).Visualization was performed using the Quantum gel documentation system (Vilber Lourmat, France).To obtain quantitative characteristics, the images were converted into the digital form using the Quantity One software package (Bio-Rad, USA).

CONCLUSIONS

In this work, an approach to the synthesis of photocleavable guide sgRNAs for the CRISPR/Cas9 system containing 1-(2-nitrophenyl)-1,2-ethanediol linkers was developed.The developed approach was used to obtain three variants of photomodified sgRNAs with different photolinker locations, which were shown to be cleaved under UV irradiation.

The optimal localization of the photolinker in the guide sgRNA was found, which makes it possible to control the activity of the CRISPR/Cas9 system by its irradiation.Such sgRNAs, being part of a complex with the Cas9 nuclease, effectively direct the system to cleave target DNA before irradiation and enable its inactivation after UV irradiation.The irradiation mode was chosen to achieve a 40% reduction in the amount of target DNA under the action of our designed photomodified CRISPR/ Cas9 system.A combination of short irradiation times and the use of near-UV light, which is much safer for cells than UV-B, UV-C, and far-UV-A [20,21], opens up the prospects for in vivo application of photoinactivated sgRNAs.

The possibility of removing the 3′-terminal fragment of sgRNA without a critical decrease in its functional activity was confirmed.It was shown that sgRNAs shortened to 69 nt are able to cleave target DNA: although the cleavage rate in this case is lower compared to fulllength sgRNAs, the maximum degree of cleavage is practically not reduced.

The results obtained in this work provide evidence for the possibility of creating a photosensitive CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system with photocleavable linkers in the structure of guide RNAs.The spatiotemporal cleavage of the guide RNA will reduce off-target effects during gene editing.In the future, this strategy can be used to treat a number of genetic diseases caused by the presence of "extra" copies of a certain gene.Such pathologies include, for example, Charcot-Marie-Tooth syndrome, which can be caused by an extra copy of the peripheral myelin protein gene PMP22.We are the first to propose an approach to light-dependent "switching off" the activity of the CRISPR system.
