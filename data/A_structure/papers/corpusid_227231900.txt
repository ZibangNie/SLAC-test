24Trypanosoma cruzi is the intracellular parasite of Chagas disease, a chronic 25 condition characterized by cardiac and gastrointestinal morbidity. Protective immunity 26 requires CD4+ T cells, and Th1 cells and IFN-γ are important players in host defense. 27More recently, Th17 cells and IL-17 have been shown to exert protective functions in 28 systemic T. cruzi infection. However, it remains unclear whether Th17 cells and IL-17A 29 protect against mucosal infection, which is an important cause of human outbreaks. We 30 found that IL-17RA knock-out (KO) mice are highly susceptible to orogastric infection, 31indicating an important function for this cytokine in mucosal immunity to T. cruzi. To 32 investigate the specific role of Th17 cells for mucosal immunity, we reconstituted RAG1 33 KO mice with T. cruzi-specific T cell receptor transgenic Th17 cells prior to orogastric T. 34 cruzi challenges. We found that Th17 cells provided protection against gastric mucosal 35 T. cruzi infection, indicated by significantly lower stomach parasite burdens. In vitro 36 macrophage infection assays revealed that protection by Th17 cells is reversed with IL-37 17A neutralization or loss of macrophage NADPH oxidase activity. Consistent with this, 38 in vivo, mice lacking functional NADPH oxidase were not protected by Th17 cell 39 transfer. These data are the first report that Th17 cells protect against mucosal T. cruzi 40 infection, and identify a novel protective mechanism involving the induction of NADPH 41 oxidase activity in macrophages by IL-17A. These studies provide important insights for 42 Chagas vaccine development, and more broadly, increase our understanding of the 43 diverse roles of Th17 cells in host defense. 44 45 46

Introduction


47

Chronic infection with the intracellular protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi 48 causes Chagas disease, a neglected tropical disease characterized by life-threatening 49 cardiac and gastrointestinal pathology(1). The disease is endemic in Latin America with 50 geographical spread into non-endemic areas, and it affects at least 8 million people(2). 51

Infected reduviid "kissing bug" insects carry T. cruzi in their gastrointestinal tracts and 52 deposit infectious parasites in their excreta after taking a blood meal. People become 53 infected when parasite-containing excreta are accidentally ingested or inoculated into 54 the eye or a break in the skin. Thus, the major routes of vector-borne transmission are 55 mucosal and cutaneous. 56

During chronic T. cruzi infection, the parasite load is controlled but never 57 completely eliminated. A robust T cell response is sufficient for parasite control in some 58 models(3, 4), and lack of either CD4+ or CD8+ T cell responses increases susceptibility 59 to infection(5-9). Among the CD4+ T cell subsets, Th1 cells have been demonstrated to 60 protect against both systemic and mucosal infection(5, 10-12), while Th2 cells promote 61 parasite persistence and mortality (12,13). More recent studies have investigated the 62 role of Th17 cells and IL-17A. We previously discovered using an adoptive cell transfer 63 model that Th17 cells significantly reduce parasitemia and prevent mortality after a 64 normally lethal challenge administered via subcutaneous injection of parasites (3). 65

Other investigators have shown that mice deficient in IL-17A signaling due to genetic 66 mutation of  or its receptor(15), or through antibody neutralization(16), have 67 increased susceptibility to an intraperitoneally administered T. cruzi challenge. 68

Despite evidence that IL-17A and Th17 cells protect against systemic parasite 69 challenges, whether this type of response contributes to mucosal immunity is unknown. 70

Orogastric infection has caused hundreds of outbreaks in humans (17)(18)(19). It has grown 71 more common in recent years and is a leading route of transmission in some endemic 72 areas (19). Oral ingestion is also considered to be a major route of transmission in other 73 mammalian hosts such as domestic dogs, which are infected at high rates in endemic 74 areas(20). Th17 cells are highly abundant at mucosal surfaces, especially in the 75 intestinal gut, where they play a major role in homeostasis and immunity(21). Mainly 76 through the secretion of IL-17A, these cells can recruit neutrophils, induce the 77 expression of anti-microbial peptides, and upregulate factors that maintain epithelial 78 integrity. These functions contribute to immunity in the intestinal mucosa(21) and may 79 also be relevant for immunity in the gastric mucosa.

IL-17A signaling is important for mucosal immunity to T. cruzi infection 88

IL-17A is critical in systemic immunity against T. cruzi infection(14-16). To 89 investigate how IL-17A functions in T. cruzi mucosal immunity, we performed orogastric 90 infections in wild-type (WT) and IL-17RA knock-out (KO) BALB/c mice, which lack a 91 subunit of the heterodimeric receptor for IL-17A(21). Twelve days later, we performed 92 hematoxylin and eosin staining of tissue sections taken where T. cruzi preferentially 93 infects at the margo plicatus(22), the region of transition between the glandular corpus 94 of the stomach and the non-glandular forestomach. At baseline, the stomachs of WT 95 and IL-17RA KO mice were histologically similar, with the presence of well-differentiated 96 glands (Supplemental Fig 1). After infection, only mild histological abnormalities were 97 observed in WT mice ( Fig 1A). However, infected IL-17RA KO mice exhibited 98 pronounced inflammatory infiltrate and loss of specialized glandular cells representing 99 atrophy(23), indicating increased susceptibility to gastric mucosal parasite challenge 100 (Figs 1A and 1B). IL-17RA KO mice also had higher parasite burdens in the stomach 101 and a greater proportion of infected spleen cells (Figs 1C and 1D), indicating 102 comparably worse immune control. These data are the first evidence that IL-17 103 signaling plays a critical role in protective gastric mucosal immunity to T. cruzi infection. 104


Th17 cells can protect against gastric mucosal infection 105

Th17 cells are major CD4+ T cell producers of IL-17A. To investigate the role of 106

Th17 cells in gastric mucosal T. cruzi infection, we generated Th17 cells specific for T. 107

cruzi. This was done via in vitro Th17 differentiation of CD4+ T cells with transgenic T 108 cell receptors (TCR Tg) recognizing an immunodominant epitope of the T. cruzi trans-109 sialidase antigen(3). We have previously characterized the phenotype and persistence 110 of these TCR Tg Th17 cells in adoptive transfer models(3), and cells were confirmed to 111 express the canonical Th17 cell markers RORγt and IL-17A prior to every transfer. 112

We transferred these TCR Tg Th17 cells into RAG1 KO mice lacking 113 endogenous T cells, with or without T. cruzi-naïve polyclonal CD8+ T cells. Control 114 animals received CD8+ T cells alone or no T cell transfer. The following day, we 115 induced stomach infection via oral gavage of parasites. On day 12 post-infection, 116

previously identified as the time of peak parasite burden, we sacrificed the mice for 117 quantification of parasite burden in the stomach using qPCR and in the spleen using 118 parasite outgrowth assays (Fig 2A). Mice receiving Th17 cells with CD8+ T cells had 119 significantly reduced T. cruzi DNA in the stomach compared to control mice ( Fig 2B). In 120 addition, these mice had a smaller proportion of infected spleen cells compared to 121 control mice, indicating significantly improved overall control of the infection ( Fig 2C). 122

We previously demonstrated that in systemic infection, co-transfer of CD8+ T 123 cells is required for the protective effects of Th17 cells. Mechanistically, parasite-specific 124


Th17 cells protected by providing help to CD8+ T cells via IL-21 signaling(3). 125

Surprisingly, Th17 cells alone provided significant mucosal protection, and the addition 126 of CD8+ T cells did not improve protection (Figs 2B and 2C). These data indicate that 127

Th17-mediated mucosal protection operates via a different mechanism, involving direct 128 protective functions rather than helper effects on CD8+ T cells. 129

Th1 cells are known to provide direct protective effects that can control T. cruzi 130 infection independent of CD8+ T cells, primarily through IFN-γ-mediated activation of 131 macrophages(24). We next reconstituted RAG1 KO mice with either TCR Tg Th1 or 132

Th17 cells to compare the direct protective effects of these CD4+ T cell subsets. Both 133 Th1 and Th17 cells were able to confer direct protective effects against a gastric 134 challenge, as indicated by low levels of parasite DNA in the stomach after infection (Fig  135   2D). Th1 and Th17 cells similarly reduced the proportion of infected spleen cells 136 compared to control mice given no T cell transfer (Fig 2E), indicating that both Th1 and 137

Th17 cells protect against a mucosal T. cruzi infection even in a CD8+ T cell and B cell 138 deficient environment. 139


Th17 cells provide direct protective effects in vitro via IL-17A 140

To investigate direct protective effects of Th17 cells, we infected macrophages 141 with T. cruzi in vitro, and then co-cultured them with either parasite-specific Th1 or Th17 142 cells before enumerating the number of infected macrophages arising after 2 days (Fig  143   3A). Both Th1 or Th17 cells resulted in a significantly reduced number of infected cells 144 ( Fig 3B). Adding an anti-IL-17A neutralizing antibody partially reversed the protective 145 effects of Th17 cells (Fig 3C), suggesting that the protection is cytokine-mediated. 146

Consistent with this, treatment with IFN-γ or IL-17A, representing the major cytokines 147 produced by Th1 and Th17 cells, respectively, also significantly reduced the number of 148 infected cells in both murine ( Fig 3D) and human macrophages ( Fig 3E). These data 149 confirm that Th17 cells provide direct protective effects via IL-17A and suggest a similar 150 effect may exist in humans. 151


IL-17A induces NADPH oxidase activity in infected macrophages 152

Th1 cells prime macrophage activation for the killing of intracellular 153 microorganisms through IFN-γ-mediated induction of iNOS, which results in the 154 generation of microbicidal nitric oxide (NO)(10, 24). We confirmed that treatment of 155 infected BMDMs with IFN-γ resulted in an increase in NO concentration ( Fig 3F). In 156 contrast, treatment with IL-17A had no effect on increasing NO levels over control cells 157 ( Fig 3F). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated by NADPH oxidase during the 158 phagocyte respiratory burst response, and similar to NO, they can inhibit microbial 159 growth. To evaluate whether ROS were induced by IL-17A, we incubated IL-17A-treated 160 and infected BMDMs with DCFDA, a probe that can be oxidized into a fluorescent 161 substrate. T. cruzi infection in the presence of IL-17A induced a 3.5-fold increase in the 162 mean fluorescence intensity of oxidized DCFDA over uninfected cells, indicating a 163 higher oxidation state under these conditions ( Fig 3G). However, the amount of oxidized 164 DCFDA did not increase among gp91phox KO cells lacking functional NADPH oxidase 165 enzyme under the same conditions, reflecting the expected defect in ROS production 166 ( Fig 3G). Further, this deficiency of functional NADPH oxidase had no effect on IFN-γ-167 mediated protection, but reversed IL-17A-mediated protection ( Fig 3H). These data 168 indicate that direct protection by Th17 cells requires IL-17A signaling and NADPH 169 oxidase activity. 170


Th17 cells induce oxidation in mucosal immune cells in vivo 171

To determine whether parasite-specific Th17 cells given by adoptive transfer 172 could be detected in the stomach, we recovered cells from the gastric mucosa of mice 173 given no T cells or Th17 cells after orogastric infection with T. cruzi. We confirmed that 174 parasite-specific Th17 cells were present in the gastric mucosa post-infection ( Fig 4A). 175

Proportions of other immune cell subsets, including neutrophils, which can be recruited 176 by IL-17A, were not significantly altered compared to control mice receiving no T cell 177 transfer ( Fig 4A). 178

We determined that macrophages and neutrophils 182 recovered from the gastric mucosa of mice given Th17 cells had significantly higher 183 oxidation states compared to control mice (Fig 4B), indicating an increase in reactive 184 oxygen species. Drugs are 208 limited by side effects and poor efficacy during the chronic stage of infection(25), and no 209 vaccines are being tested in humans. The development of an effective vaccine would be 210 an economically sound approach (26) and requires a thorough understanding of the 211 protective immune response to this parasite, including mucosal immunity. 212 Th17 cells are well-established as important players against various fungi and 213 extracellular bacteria(21) but are only recently gaining recognition as effectors in the 214 immune response to intracellular pathogens like T. cruzi. Several studies over the past 215 decade have described a role for IL-17A in the protective response against systemic T. 216 cruzi parasite infection in mice(14, 16), and more recent work suggests a protective role 217 for this cytokine in human Chagas disease as well(27). However, none of these studies 218 has specifically examined the role of IL-17A in mucosal immunity against T. cruzi. 219 In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that Th17 cells and IL-17A contribute 220 immunity against a gastric mucosal T. cruzi infection. Although CD8+ T cells are critical 221 for Th17-mediated protection in systemic infection (3), they are dispensable in mucosal 222 immunity. Instead, Th17 cells provide direct protective effects via secretion of IL-17A 223 and induction of NADPH oxidase. While systemic T. cruzi infection involves multiple 224 tissues and requires CD8+ T cell responses, local direct protective effects of IL-17A 225 may be sufficient in mucosal infection. In addition, in systemic infection models, Th17 226 cells provided more protection against mortality compared to Th1 cells due to improved 227 helper effects. However, protection by Th1 and Th17 cells was comparable at the 228 gastric mucosa, where these cells act directly. 229In summary, we identify a role for Th17 cells and IL-17A in mucosal immunity 230against Trypanosoma cruzi and we describe a novel protective mechanism of IL-17A 231 against an intracellular pathogen. Th17 cells may have more broad protective functions 232 than previously believed, and these studies in T. cruzi could also provide insights into233 infections with similar mucosally transmitted intracellular protozoa. Ultimately, these 234 findings should be assessed for translational potential through studies targeting the 235 induction of Th17 cells through mucosal vaccination, with the goal of reducing the 236 significant morbidity and mortality associated with this disease. 237 238 Materials and Methods 239 Mice.
