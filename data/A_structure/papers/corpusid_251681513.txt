Effects of Time of Pruning on Growth and Longevity of Peach Trees1

JeffW.Daniell2 Georgia Agricultural Experiment Station, Experiment Abstract.Pruning dates as treatments were imposed for a 4 or 5-year period on peach trees [Prunus persica (L.) Batsch] growing on old and new peach land.The trees were pruned during the middle of each month with an unpruned check in 1 test only.Trees growing on old peach land and pruned in fall or early winter generally had greater mortality than non-pruned or trees pruned in spring.Time of pruning, however, had little or no effect on longevity when trees were grown on new peach land.Also time of pruning had no consistent effect on tree growth.

Premature death of peach trees has reached epidemic proportions in certain areas of the southeastern United States.The short tree-life problem, also referred to as peach tree decline, has become more severe in recent years due to successive peach plantings on the same land.An estimated 200,000 to 300,000 bearing peach trees in Georgia died in 1962, with the decline complex following bloom and setting of fruit (3).

Causes of peach tree short life appear to be many.Researchers have attributed the short life of trees to Cytospora canker (9,13,14,18) Phytophthora root rot (16), Cylindrocadium root rot (21,23), Pythium root rot (12), nematodes (11), and Clitocybe root rot (2,17).I have shown an association between cold injury and tree decline (6,7).

Stated objectives of conventional peach tree pruning are to develop a low tree with strong wide-angle scaffold branches having sufficient clearance from the ground to facilitate orchard culture, to keep fruiting wood near the trunk and to develop an open, spreading, and symmetrical tree (5,15,24).increases cold injury.Dowler and Petersen (9) found that peach trees pruned in October and inoculated with Pseudomonas syringae Van Hall, developed cankers.Typical decline symptoms were expressed and death occurred the following spring.Trees pruned and inoculated in February exhibited cankers but no progression of damage occurred after growth started.Trees also pruned in October but not inoculated developed no cankers but did exhibit symptoms of winter injury.These results suggest that pruning at certain times of the year predisposes trees to decline.This paper reports experiments to determine effects of time of pruning on growth and subsequent decline of peach trees grown on both old and new peach sites.


Materials and Methods

Differential time-of-pruning treatments were begun in 1967 at both Plains and Ft.Valley, Georgia, and a third test was initiated in 1968 at Ft. Valley.Both tests at Ft.Valley were conducted on old soil with a history of severe decline.The test at Plains was conducted on a site where peaches had not been grown before.

Existing trees were removed and trees of 'Loring' were planted on Facerville fine sandy loam soil in 1967 at Ft.Valley and 'Suwanee' was planted on Greenville sandy loam soil at Plains in 1966.All areas were limed, plowed, and subsoiled prior to planting.A 6.1 m x 6.1 m tree spacing was used in 1967 with either 3-tree plots and 6 replications or 5-tree plots and 4 replications.A 6.1 m x 7.3 m spacing was used for trees planted in 1968 with 2-tree plots and 6 replications.Time of pruning in both tests at Ft.Valley consisted of 12 treatments: an individual pruning for each month of the year plus an unpruned control added in the 1968 test.Ten treatments were used at Plains with the December and June pruning omitted.For each treatment, the trees were pruned each year during the middle of that particular month.All trees were headed back after planting to 90 cm from the ground.Unpruned trees were not subsequently pruned or cut for the 4-year period.Approx 453 kg/ha of 8-8-8 fertilizer was applied in the spring each year and 136 kg/ha of ammonium nitrate was applied after harvest.Weeds were controlled with herbicides to eliminate possibility of mechanical injury to roots and tops of trees.Decline symptoms and tree deaths were  noted.Visual ratings were made on existing trees at the end of tests using a 1-10 rating system with 10 designating no decline symptoms and 1 designating death or severe decline of trees.Extra trees in each plot were removed periodically and roots and tops examined for evidence of decline.Trunk diam was measured approx 40 cm above ground level after each growing season.

The vase system was used in the pruning treatments and trees were allowed to reach a height of 300 cm which is approx 90 cm taller than conventionally pruned trees in Georgia.


Results and Discussion

Declining trees expressed numerous symptoms previously characterized (2,4,7,11,18).Death of trees in this study was associated with one or more of the following group of symptoms: a) excessive gumming, canker development, leaf chlorosis, and excessive dieback of twigs, b) discoloration and zonation in cambium and xylem tissues, death of scaffold branches, and excessive water sprouts on trunk of tree, or c) sudden withering and death of tree in spring without visible symptoms in prior years but with some discoloration of cambial tissue during the previous winter.Trees exhibiting symptoms in group a or b visually declined for 2 or more years before death.

Only a few trees showed decline symptoms in 1967 and 1968.Short-life symptoms were prevalent in the spring of 1969 on trees in some pruning treatments on old peach land.No decline symptoms were apparent, however, on trees in the test on new peach land.

Differences in growth of trees between experiments could not be statistically compared due to differences in the test locations, but I observed that trees growing on old peach land, initially, did not have as much vigor or bloom nor did they grow as fast as trees on new peach land.Gilmore (10) showed that peach trees grow slower on replant sites than on new peach sites.

Evidence has been presented (20) that summer pruning results in smaller trees than winter pruning which is in contrast to data in this test (Tables 1, 2, and 3).Spring and summer pruning in my test resulted in growth equal to or significantly greater than winter pruning (December, January, and February).It should be mentioned, however, that care was taken not to prune too severely during the growing season.

Differences in mortality of trees from time of pruning were large on old peach land (Tables 1 and 2) but little or no differences were found on new land (Table 3).Mortality aggregating 66% occurred in 5 years on old land (Table 1), where trees were pruned in December as compared to only 6% for March-April-and May-pruned trees.Similar trends were noted in the 4-year test on old land (Table 2).Data on mortality were compiled for October, November, December, and January treatments, the conventional time when trees are pruned in Georgia, and compared to those for March, April, May, and June treatments, the early growing season (Fig. 1 and 2).Mortality was 32% for fall or early-winter-pruned trees, compared to only 10% mortality for spring pruned trees in test 1 (Fig. 1).Similar results were obtained in test 2 (Fig. 2) with 27% mortality from fall pruning.Average tree life is similar to percent mortality, but was used for statistical comparisons as this measurement reflects when death actually occurred.Commercial bearing begins in the third year, hence the time of death would determine whether part or all of the revenue was lost from individual trees.A commercial orchard is generally unprofitable when mortality reaches 50%.Time of pruning in addition to having an effect on tree mortality, had some effect on decline symptoms of the remaining live trees in the tests (Tables 1, 2, and 3).There were significantly more symptoms among December-pruned trees than some other treatments in the 5-year test on old land (Table 1).Severity of symptom data for live trees followed the same general trend as percent mortality and average tree life.

My data for time of pruning on decline land corroborates evidence (9,19) as to detrimental effects of fall or early winter pruning on tree longevity.The best time for pruning on decline land was in the spring.The data show that decline incidence was reduced by spring pruning but some tree death occurred.YEAR Fig. 2. Therefore, the short-life problem was not completely eliminated, indicating that other factors are involved in the complex.Data obtained on non-pruned trees (Table 2) are of equal importance to those for time of pruning.No mortality occurred in 4 years in this treatment on severe decline land and no decline symptoms were observed.Comparison of data obtained on new peach land (Table 3) with that on old peach land (Tables 1 and 2) also shows that pruning times resulting in severe loss of trees on old land produced little or no loss on new land.This adds more weight to the hypothesis that most decline problems may be avoided by switching to new peach land if it is available (22).

Mechanisms involved in reduction in decline by time of pruning are not known.Spring pruning had the greatest effect on tree longevity through a reduction in sudden withering and death of trees in spring.Characteristically, such trees developed no cankers but did show discoloration in cambial tissue during the winter.The latter would suggest cold injury.Trees, however, on new land pruned at the same time and in the same manner, exhibited few decline symptoms, little discoloration, and no sudden withering of leaves.Data to be published in a later paper show that cold injury is reduced by spring pruning, giving further evidence of the relationship of cold injury to peach decline.My results suggest strongly that cold injury is interacting with other factors which are prevalent on peach-decline sites.If this is true, the contributing decline factors must be absent or at a low level on land not previously planted to peaches.
