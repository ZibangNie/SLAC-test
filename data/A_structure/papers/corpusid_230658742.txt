Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 as adjuvant therapy in patients with chronic bacterial prostatitis: a non-blinded, randomized, controlled trial

World Journal of Urology
39202110.1007/s00345-021-03773-8Received: 20 April 2021 / Accepted: 21 June 2021 / Published online: 2 July 2021ORIGINAL ARTICLEChronic bacterial prostatitis · Escherichia coli Nissle · EcN · Fluoroquinolones · Microbiota
Purpose To evaluate the efficacy and safety of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) in association with levofloxacin in patients with chronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP). Methods Patients with CBP referred to our clinic from September 2017 to July 2019 were enrolled. At baseline, the symptomatology was assessed with the NIH-Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI), while the Meares-Stamey test was used to diagnose the infection. Patients were randomized (1:1) in two groups (A and B). All subjects underwent oral administration of Levoxacin® 500 mg once daily for 4 weeks. Only the patients in Group B underwent oral administration of EcN® 320 mg, twice daily for 4 weeks and then once daily for 8 weeks. After 3 months, each patient repeated the NIH-CPSI questionnaire, while the Meares-Stamey test was repeated at 3 and 6 months in patients who reported persistent symptoms. All adverse events (AEs) were recorded. Results A total of 110 patients were enrolled. After 3 months patients in Group B reported a significantly lower NIH-CPSI score (5.85 ± 3.07 vs. 7.64 ± 3.86; p = 0.009) and biological recurrences rate (9.8 vs. 26.9%; p = 0.043). At 6 months the biological recurrences rate was significantly lower in Group B (8.7 vs. 28.9%; p = 0.038). Only three patients in Group A and six in Group B (p = 0.25) complained mild AEs. Conclusions Combination therapy with EcN and levofloxacin allows a better control of symptoms and biological recurrences in patients with CBP, without worsening the safety of the treatment.Publisher's Note Springer Nature remains neutral with regard to jurisdictional claims in published maps and institutional affiliations.

Introduction

Prostatitis is a common cause of physician visit; however, a bacterial origin is demonstrated in less than 10% of cases [1]. A symptomatology lasting more than 3 months allows to differentiate chronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP) from acute bacterial prostatitis (ABP), which, respectively, constitutes type II and type I prostatitis according to the classification suggested by the National Institute of Diabetes Digestive and Kidney Diseases of the National Institutes of Health (NIDDK/NIH) [2]. Enterobacteriaceae, in particular E. coli, are the main pathogens in ABP, while the spectrum of bacterial species is wider in CBP and may include atypical microorganisms such as C. trachomatis, U. urealyticum, and M. genitalium, as well as T. vaginalis [3,4]. CBP is a condition which significantly hampers the patient's quality of life (QoL) because it is not only a physical discomfort, resulting from a heterogeneous pain symptomatology and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), but also for the significant negative influence on the psychological sphere [3,5].

ABP responds satisfactorily to antibiotic therapy and has a usually self-limiting course, while CBP is difficult to treat effectively and has a high recurrence rate, this results in a high percentage of patient dissatisfaction [5]. Fluoroquinolones are commonly the first-line treatment for CBP because of their favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics, good profile of safety, and antibacterial activity against Gram-negative microbes [6]. However, the increased resistance of bacteria to this class of antibiotics, the frequent gastrointestinal and neurological complaints, and the recent description of possible serious cardiovascular adverse events following their intake are important factors to consider before prescription [7]. A prolonged therapy (4-6 weeks) with optimal dosage of fluoroquinolones is still recommended by current European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines as first-line treatment for CBP, although this strategy may not be sufficient to permanently eradicate the infection [8]. No significant differences between levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, and prulifloxacin were found in terms of microbiological eradication, clinical efficacy, and adverse events [9].

Recent studies have highlighted the involvement of the human microbiota in the onset and clinical expression of prostatitis [10]. This paved the way for a new line of research, which has already revealed a possible role of probiotics in the management of CBP [11]. Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) is a non-pathogenic bacterial strain belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family with probiotic properties [12].

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of EcN in association with levofloxacin for the treatment of patients with CBP.


Materials and methods


Study design and patient enrollment

We designed a non-blinded, randomized, controlled trial (RCT) in which patients with CBP referred to our clinic from September 2017 to July 2019 were enrolled. The study was conducted in accordance with the Ethics Committee requirements (ID: B28/2017) and based on Declaration of Helsinki on ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects. Each patient provided a written informed consent to participate. The CONSORT checklist is shown in Supplementary Table 1. All patients were positive to the Meares-Stamey test [13], complained of a symptomatology (LUTS, pain and/or discomfort in the perineum, scrotum, penis or inner part of the leg) lasting more than 3 months, and showed sensitivity to fluoroquinolones in antibiogram. The exclusion criteria were: less than 18 years old; history of allergic reaction or other severe adverse events with fluoroquinolones; history of epilepsy; known anatomical abnormalities of the urinary tract or other urological diseases; known gastrointestinal diseases; suspected of prostate cancer; recent (< 4 weeks) intake of antibiotic, probiotic, prebiotic or phytotherapy; culture after Meares-Stamey test positive for C. trachomatis, U. urealyticum, M. genitalium, T. vaginalis, multiple pathogens or a pathogen known to be resistant to fluoroquinolones. Besides, subjects with major risk factors for adverse events related to fluoroquinolones were excluded (old age, kidney disease, organ transplantation, intake of systemic corticosteroid, family history of aneurysm disease or congenital heart valve disease, preexisting aortic aneurysm/dissection or heart valve disease).


Patient assessment and measured outcomes

Each patient enrolled underwent medical history as well as an accurate physical examination, including digital rectal examination. Symptomatology was assessed with the validated Italian version of the NIH-Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI) [14]. The four-glass Meares-Stamey test was used to evaluate inflammation and the presence of bacteria in the lower urinary tract. All Meares-Stamey test were performed by the same expert urologist and were considered positive for CBP when there was a tenfold increase in bacteria in the expressed prostate secretions (EPS) or voided-bladder 3 (VB3) specimen compared with the voided-bladder 1 (VB1) and voided-bladder 2 (VB2) specimens [15]. The processing and analysis of all biological samples were performed by the same expert microbiologist who was blinded to the patient's history and results of other tests. Each patient underwent an ultrasound evaluation of prostate, kidney, and bladder, as well as a PSA dosage before the start of treatment to exclude other urological diseases.

After 3 months from the beginning of therapy, each patient repeated the NIH-CPSI questionnaire. The biological recurrence was evaluated at 3 and 6 months with a Meares-Stamey test in patients who reported persistent symptoms of CBP. All adverse events (AEs) occurred during the study period were recorded.


Treatment protocol

Levofloxacin 500 mg (Levoxacin®, GlaxoSmithKline Spa, VR, Italy) [16] and E. coli Nissle 1917 320 mg (EcN®, CaDiGroup Srl, RM, Italy) were used for the purposes of the study. Each tablet of EcN contained 2.5-25 × 10 9 live cells [17].

A simple randomization was performed to assign patients in two groups (A or B). More specifically, a desktop software was used to generate a random sequence of numbers (1 or 2), and the enrolled patients were assigned to group A (number 1) or B (number 2) according to the sequence. All patients (Group A and Group B) underwent oral administration of Levoxacin 500 mg once daily for 4 weeks. Only the patients in the Group B underwent oral administration of EcN 320 mg, twice daily for 4 weeks and then once daily for 8 weeks. The posology of EcN was established on the basis of the package leaflet and the authors' previous clinical experience supported by an unpublished 10-patient pilot study. The administration of EcN started at the end of antibiotic therapy for the sensitivity of the probiotic to fluoroquinolones [17]. On the same way, according to the package leaflet, all patients were instructed to take Levoxacin [16] indifferently at meals or between meals but always at least 2 hours before or after taking iron salts, zinc salts, antacids containing magnesium or aluminum, or sucralfate. Patients in Group B were instructed to store EcN in the refrigerator between 2 and 8 °C and take it at meals [17].


Statistics

The categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, while for the quantitative variables we used mean and standard deviation (SD) as measure of central tendency and statistical dispersion, respectively. The Student's t test was used to analyze the data. 


Results

A total of 110 patients were enrolled in the study. After randomization, each group (A and B) consisted of 55 patients (Fig. 1). No statistically significant difference between the two groups was found in age (34.6 ± 7.5 vs. 33.7 ± 7.9 years; p = 0.55), duration of symptoms (15.8 ± 9.4 vs. 18.9 ± 9.2 months; p = 0.08), pretreatment NIH-CPSI (24.1 ± 5.0 vs. 23.8 ± 4.7 points; p = 0.72), and pretreatment PSA (0.9 ± 0.7 vs. 0.8 ± 0.7 ng/mL; p = 0.31). The complete baseline characteristics of patients are summarized in Table 1.

Enterobacteriaceae were the most common cause of CBP before the treatment, with no significant difference between the two groups (74.5 vs. 70.9%; p = 0.67). The most frequently isolated pathogen during the entire study period was E. coli (47.27%). Bacteria identified by the Meares-Stamey test at baseline are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Two patients for each group were lost to follow-up, while 1 patient in the Group A and 2 patients in the Group B discontinued the treatment, therefore, the analysis of data was conducted in 52/55 (94.5%) and 51/55 (92.7%) patients, respectively (Fig. 1). No significant difference in overall treatment duration was recorded between the two groups (p = 0.213). After 3 months of therapy in both groups there was a statistically significant reduction in NIH-CPSI (p < 0.001), the patients in Group B reported a significantly lower NIH-CPSI score than patients in Group A (5.85 ± 3.07 vs. 7.64 ± 3.86; p = 0.009). At 3 months 14/52 biological recurrences were observed in Group A and 5/51 in Group B (26.9 vs. 9.8%; p = 0.043), while at 6 months biological recurrences occurred in 11/38 patients in the Group A and in 4/46 patients in Group B (28.9 vs. 8.7%; p = 0.038). The post-treatment outcomes are shown in Fig. 2. Only 3/52 patients in Group A and 6/51 patients in Group B (5.8 vs. 11.8%; p = 0.25) complained mild AEs that did not require treatment suspension. No patient complained of multiple side effects. The comprehensive list of AEs found in the two groups is reported in Supplementary Table 3.


Discussion

The antibiotic therapy is the gold standard treatment for CBP and the fluoroquinolones are recommended as first-line agents [8]. Complete eradication of the infection to resolve the symptoms and the prevention of recurrences are the main therapeutic objectives in patients with CBP. The frequent inability of antibiotic therapy to achieve the desired results probably derive from the heterogeneous etiology of disease, complex pathogenesis of infection, poor permeability of the prostate to drugs, and inadequate duration of antibiotic administration [8]. Busetto et al. found that the association of Serenoa repens, Arbutin, and Lactobacillus sporogenes with prulifloxacin compared to antibiotic therapy alone in patients with CBP significantly improved the clinical efficacy in terms of logical recurrences (7.8 vs. 27.6%), NIH-CPSI score (3.67 vs. 13.26 points), and symptoms related to CBP reported (6.50 vs. 26.3%), without clinically relevant adverse effects [18]. Recently, Cai et al. have also evaluated the use of Lactobacillus casei DG® in addition to antibiotic in CBP patients with similar results [11]. Our research seems to confirm the positive effects of combination therapy reported from previous studies. In the present series, patients treated with levofloxacin plus EcN showed greater improvement in symptoms at 3 months (NIH-CPSI: 5.85 ± 3.07 vs. 7.64 ± 3.86; p = 0.009) and lower recurrence rate at 3 (26.9 vs. 9.8%; p = 0.043) and 6 (28.9 vs. 8.7%; p = 0.038) months. In addition, only mild AEs were recorded, with no significant differences between the two treatment arms (5.8 vs. 11.8%; p = 0.25). The long duration of combined treatment can reduce patient compliance, as shown, albeit in a low percentage of cases, in our study; on the other hand, the significant cost of probiotics and supplements is a further issue that patients frequently highlight in the common clinical practice and which can lead to discontinuation of therapy.


Combined treatment for CBP


Microbiota and CBP

The beneficial effects of probiotics in CBP patients could be explained by the microbiota alteration found in this specific population [10]. The microbiota is the community of microorganisms found in a particular environment and it seems to play a fundamental role in controlling host physiology, especially in the induction, education and function of the immune system [19]. While for the intestine the presence of a microbiota has long been known, traditionally the urinary tract was considered a sterile environment, however, recent studies have shown, through gene sequencing techniques (i.e., 16S rRNA GS), that there is also a urinary microbiota [20]. The qualitative or quantitative alteration of the microbiota is defined "dysbiosis" [21]. Drugs, infections, stress, unhealthy diet, and food intolerances seem to be predisposing factors for human microbiota alterations [22]. In last few years, research has focused on association between urinary and intestinal dysbiosis, development of urological diseases, and patient response to therapy [23][24][25].

Vicari et al. described that prostatitis syndromes (PS) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) were frequently associated (~ 30%), besides, patients with PS plus IBS had a significantly higher frequency of CBP and non-inflammatory prostatitis compared to patients with PS alone. In this context, it is important to underline how an intestinal dysbiosis can favor CBP, but at the same time the intake of antibiotics for CBP can favor an intestinal dysbiosis [26]. Finally, Liu et al. suggested that the urethral dysbacteriosis could be an underlying primary cause of chronic prostatitis. In particular the urethral microflora could infect the prostate through a reflux of urine into the prostate ducts, and an excessive use of antibiotics excreted in the urine could lead to urethral dysbacteriosis and consequently prostate infection [27].


Role of probiotics in CBP

Based on the apparent correlation between CBP and microbiota, the use of probiotics seems to be reasonable. It could limit the harmful effects of long-lasting antibiotic therapy reducing the risk of intestinal dysbiosis, on the other hand, it could directly improve the intestinal microbiota when it is altered by primitive or secondary conditions that are independent of antibiotics. In addition, it could have an indirect beneficial effect on the urinary microbiota, although there is no clear evidence that correlates the use of probiotics with the modification of the urinary microflora [28].

EcN is a non-pathogenic Gram-negative strain that was isolated by Prof. Alfred Nissle in 1917 [12]. EcN is currently the only probiotic containing a strain of E. coli, able to compete directly against E. coli involved in the development and persistence of CBP. Moreover, it has antibacterial activity against a large part of other pathogens commonly involved in CBP. There are several theories about its mechanism of action: (1) Direct antimicrobial effects; (2) Bacterial-epithelial crosstalk; (3) Immune-modulatory proprieties. EcN has a flagellum which allow to efficiently compete with pathogens for binding sites on host tissue, and a specific lipopolysaccharide that is responsible for its immunogenicity and provide a powerful effect on intestinal immune function [29]. In addition, the anti-inflammatory action of EcN at the level of distal colon was proven [30], therefore, we hypothesize that this effect can also be maintained in contiguous prostatic tissues.


Strengths and limitations

Our findings seem confirm the benefit of combination therapy in patients with CBP and indirectly highlight the possible role of the microbiota in the development and maintenance of this disease. Our results should be read and interpreted considering the limitations of the study. The main limitations of our research are the relatively small sample size and the short follow-up time. Conclusions

The association of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 with levofloxacin for the treatment of patients with CBP seems to favor better control of symptoms and reduce the risk of biological recurrences, without increasing the risk of adverse events. Further well-designed RCTs with larger sample size and longer follow-up are needed to confirm our encouraging results.
